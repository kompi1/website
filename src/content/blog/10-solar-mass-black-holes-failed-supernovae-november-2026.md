Most stars die loudly. A massive star, having burned through its nuclear fuel, collapses under its own weight and then detonates, hurling its outer layers into space in a supernova that can briefly outshine an entire galaxy. The wreckage left behind is a neutron star or a black hole, and the debris seeds the cosmos with the heavy elements that eventually become planets and people. It is one of the most violent and most familiar stories in astronomy.

But some stars may die in silence. According to a new study led by researchers at Caltech, a large share of the black holes now colliding across the universe may have been born not in a blaze of light, but in a collapse so quiet that no telescope on Earth would ever have noticed it. These are the failed supernovae: massive stars whose cores fall inward and never rebound, swallowing the star whole and leaving behind a black hole with no explosion to mark the event. The evidence, published on 10 November 2026 in the journal *Physical Review D*, comes not from a telescope image but from the statistical fingerprint of 257 black hole mergers recorded by the global network of gravitational-wave detectors. The pattern the team found is stark: black holes cluster near ten times the mass of the Sun, and then, in the range from 12 to 16 solar masses, they almost vanish.

If the interpretation holds, it would solve a puzzle that has nagged astronomers since the first gravitational-wave detection in 2015. It would also mean that the most common black holes in the universe are the products of a kind of stellar death that no one has ever directly seen.

## The peak that should not be there

The story begins with a census. Since the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-wave Observatory, LIGO, made the first direct detection of gravitational waves in 2015, its detectors in Louisiana and Washington — joined later by Virgo in Italy and KAGRA in Japan — have recorded more than 300 black hole collisions. Each merger sends ripples through spacetime, and the shape of each signal reveals the masses of the two black holes involved. Taken one by one, the events are individual tragedies of orbital decay. Taken together, they form a population, and populations have shapes.

Isaac Legred, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign who carried out the research while at Caltech, led the new analysis with Jacob Golomb, formerly of Caltech and now at Asari AI, and Katerina Chatziioannou, a professor of physics at Caltech and a member of the LIGO collaboration. Their question was simple: when you plot the masses of every merging black hole ever detected, what does the distribution look like?

The answer, it turns out, is lopsided in a way that demands an explanation. The team examined 257 black hole mergers detected by the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA network over the past decade. Three-quarters of the entire sample — roughly 76 percent in the team's main model — clusters in a narrow peak around ten times the mass of the Sun. The peak sits at 9.95 solar masses, with a 90 percent credible range of 8.70 to 10.48, and it is strikingly narrow, with a spread of only about 0.7 solar masses. In other words, the most common merging black hole in the universe is a ten-solar-mass object, and it is ten solar masses with remarkable precision.

"A vast majority of the merging black holes in the universe are about 10 times the mass of our Sun," Chatziioannou said. "Our data are showing that this group of black holes may have a common origin — they may be a distinct subpopulation that failed to go supernova."

That is the first half of the finding. The second half is what is missing. Just above the peak, between 12 and 16 solar masses, the merger rate collapses. In the team's main model, the rate is consistent with effectively zero across the range from 12.0 to 16.1 solar masses, at 90 percent credibility, before climbing again where a second, heavier population switches on near 16.2 solar masses. Black holes of 14 solar masses, which ought to be common if stellar deaths produced a smooth range of remnants, are instead strikingly rare.

"We see 10 solar-mass black holes merging all the time," Legred said. "But why are, say, 14 solar-mass black holes so rare? The gap we see in the distribution of masses indicates there may be a special cocktail of internal physics that goes into forming the abundance of 10 solar-mass black holes."

The gap is the fingerprint the team went looking for. Earlier analyses of the gravitational-wave catalogue had already noticed the peak near ten solar masses, and earlier work with the third gravitational-wave catalogue, GWTC-3.0, had found the merger rate dropping by factors of roughly 10 to 100 between the peak and about 15 solar masses. But no previous model had allowed the rate to fall all the way to zero. This one does — and the data, the authors report, are comfortable with that.

## How gravitational waves weigh the dead

To understand why the result is persuasive, it helps to understand how the measurement works. A gravitational-wave detector does not photograph a black hole. It listens to spacetime itself. When two black holes spiral together and merge, they broadcast a chirp — a signal whose frequency rises as the pair tightens — and the precise shape of that chirp encodes the masses of the two objects. Heavier mergers are louder and easier to detect, which means any raw count of events is biased toward the heavy end. The Caltech-led team corrected for that bias, fitting a population model to all 257 events at once rather than simply counting events in mass bins.

The model has two parts. One is a narrow bell curve representing a possible population of black holes born in failed supernovae. The other describes every heavier black hole, with its own starting mass. Earlier models forced that heavier population to extend downward underneath the ten-solar-mass peak, which smeared out any gap. This model lets the heavier population start where the data say it starts. That single change is what allows the gap to appear — if the data want one. And the data, it seems, do.

The team first fitted the model to 153 binary black holes from the GWTC-4.0 catalogue, then reran the analysis on 257 events from GWTC-5.0, the newest catalogue, which was released while the paper was under review. The picture held. With the larger sample, the probability of a clean separation between the two populations was 0.85, and the zero-rate range was 12.3 to 15.7 solar masses — slightly narrower than in the main fit, but the same shape: a peak, a gap, and a heavier population beyond it.

The numbers behind the pattern are precise enough to be striking. At the peak, the merger rate is 7.36 mergers per solar mass per year per cubic gigaparsec — a cubic gigaparsec being a cube about 3.26 billion light-years on each side. Between 13 and 15 solar masses, the highest value the rate reaches is 0.24 in the same units, roughly thirty times lower, and its 90 percent range reaches zero. The authors describe the drop from the peak to the gap as a factor of about ten thousand. It is not a gentle slope. It is a cliff.

The spins tell the same story from a different angle. The team also examined the distribution of the black holes' spin rates, which are influenced by how the objects formed. The effective spin of the ten-solar-mass black holes — a measure of how much their spins line up with the orbit — is tightly clustered, with a spread of 0.07. The heavier group's effective spin is about twice as broad, at 0.13. Two populations that differ in both mass and spin are more likely to have different origins than one population that happens to look lumpy. Together, the mass peak, the gap, and the spin clustering support the idea that the ten-solar-mass black holes are distinct from the overall population, and thus likely share a common origin: the quiet implosion of massive stars.

## What a failed supernova is

A massive star ends its life when its core runs out of fuel. For most of the star's life, the outward pressure of nuclear fusion balances the inward pull of gravity. When the fuel is exhausted, that balance breaks. The core collapses in a fraction of a second, and in the standard story the collapse rebounds: a shock wave races outward, the star's outer layers are blasted into space, and the explosion we see as a supernova leaves behind a neutron star — or, for the heaviest cores, a black hole.

A failed supernova is what happens when the rebound never comes. The core collapses, but the shock wave stalls. The star's outer layers, instead of being ejected, fall inward as well, and the entire star is swallowed by the black hole forming at its centre. There is no brilliant flash, no expanding shell of glowing debris, no months-long afterglow for telescopes to study. The star simply disappears.

The idea is well supported in theory, but to date no concrete example of a failed supernova has been confirmed. That is the central frustration of the field, and the reason the new study matters: it offers a way to detect a population of failed supernovae without ever seeing one happen. If a particular kind of star reliably fails to explode, it should leave a distinctive signature in the black hole population — a narrow pile-up of remnants at the mass those stars produce, and a gap where the stars that did explode leave few remnants behind.

Computer simulations of dying stars point to just such a narrow band. In one set of models for stars with the Sun's mix of heavy elements — what astronomers call solar metallicity — only stars born with 11 to 14 solar masses failed to explode and made black holes. Another simulation series found that stars born near 12 solar masses were the only failures. Stars slightly lighter or heavier exploded instead. The reason, in the models, is the compactness of the stellar core: stars in that narrow birth-mass range develop cores so dense that the collapsing material falls inward too fast for the shock wave to escape. Stars just outside the range have less compact cores, explode, and shed almost all their mass — leaving neutron stars, or much lighter black holes, behind.

That is why the gravitational-wave data look the way they do, in the team's reading. Stars born between roughly 12 and 14 solar masses collapse directly into black holes of around ten solar masses — the birth mass minus the small amount of material lost before collapse. Stars slightly lighter or heavier explode and leave almost nothing behind in the black hole mass range, which creates the gap at 12 to 16 solar masses. Heavier stars, above about 16 solar masses, form black holes through other channels, and the population rises again.

The explanation also survives a quantitative test that simpler ideas fail. One competing explanation for the ten-solar-mass peak is that some stellar cores are unusually compact and collapse easily, producing an excess of black holes at that mass without any failed supernovae. But that mechanism predicts an excess of only about two to three times the nearby rate. The observed peak is at least fifty times the rate nearby, according to the authors — far too tall for compactness alone. Something else has to be feeding the peak, and a dedicated population of failed supernovae fits.

## The numbers behind the peak

The strength of the new study is that it is quantitative at every step. The table below gathers the key figures from the paper, all drawn from the analysis of the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA catalogues. Together they describe a population with a sharp peak, a deep gap, and a spin signature that sets the ten-solar-mass group apart.

The table makes the shape of the discovery easy to see. It is not a subtle excess or a marginal bump. Three out of four merging black holes sit in a peak barely wider than a single solar mass, and the rate then falls by orders of magnitude before recovering. Whatever produces the ten-solar-mass black holes, it produces them in bulk, and whatever produces heavier black holes largely skips the 12-to-16 range.

## A gap with an asterisk

For all its sharpness, the result comes with an honest asterisk, and the authors do not hide it. No failed supernova was observed in this study. No individual black hole has been traced back to a star that quietly vanished without exploding. The link between the mass distribution and failed supernovae rests on a statistical model, and the strength of the case depends on choices made inside that model.

The most important caveat concerns how the heavier black hole population is described. In the team's main model, the heavier group is represented by a power law plus a peak, and the separation between the two populations is supported with a probability of 0.88. Swap that description for a different one — a broken power law — and the zero-rate range shrinks to 12.3–14.1 solar masses, and the probability of a clean separation falls to 0.61. The Bayes factor comparing the two models is about 2.6, which the authors describe as neither strong nor easy to interpret. In plain terms: the gap is preferred by the data, but it is not required by them. The data are also consistent with a small but nonzero merger rate in the 12-to-16 range.

There are other caveats. The gap appears only in the primary mass — the heavier black hole in each pair — not in the lighter partner's mass, and the very act of sorting each pair into heavier and lighter can shape the distributions. Repeated mergers, in which a black hole made by one collision merges again later, could also place objects inside the gap. And the "effectively zero" label has a precise, limited meaning: the authors estimate that current detectors have surveyed about 20 cubic gigaparsec-years for pairs of 15-solar-mass black holes, so a rate at their threshold would predict only about a tenth of a detection. A rate that low would be invisible to today's instruments — which is why the gap is a statement about what the data can and cannot see, not a claim that no such black holes exist.

None of this undermines the finding, but it frames it correctly. The ten-solar-mass peak itself is solid: several earlier analyses found it, and the new study confirms it with the largest sample yet. What is new is a model that lets the merger rate fall all the way to zero above the peak, and the finding that the current data are comfortable with that. If later catalogues confirm the gap, it would be strong evidence that the most common merging black holes have their own birth route. If they fill it in, the peak will still need explaining — but the explanation may be different.

The authors are careful to say exactly that. Their paper, published after review that began when it was received on 10 April 2026 and accepted on 19 August 2026, presents the failed-supernova origin as a proposed interpretation, not an observation. The gravitational-wave data are consistent with most binary black holes forming from a very special kind of failed supernova, Legred said — but more research is needed.

## The star that vanished

There is one other line of evidence, and it comes from looking rather than listening. Astronomers have previously identified stars that appear to have disappeared without a visible explosion. The best-known candidate is the giant star N6946-BH1, which was observed before and after it faded from view — a massive star, present in earlier images, that simply winked out. It is the kind of object a failed supernova should leave behind: a star that was there, and then was not, with a black hole presumably sitting where it used to be.

Searching historical records for such vanishing stars is one of the routes the Caltech team highlights for testing the idea further. It is a difficult search, for an obvious reason. "We can look up at the sky and try to study historical records of massive stars that failed to explode, but it's challenging because they just vanish without exploding, leaving a dark patch of sky," Legred said. A supernova announces itself across the universe. A failed supernova leaves nothing to point a telescope at — only an absence where a star used to shine, and, years or billions of years later, a black hole drifting through the dark.

That absence is also what makes the gravitational-wave approach so valuable. The detectors do not need light. They record the final moments of black holes that were born in darkness, however long ago, and the population they reveal is a fossil record of stellar death. Every merger is a data point about how a star died. With 257 of them, the record is now detailed enough to show structure — a peak and a gap — that a single event could never reveal.

The same logic connects this study to other recent work on how black holes are born. Telescopes have spent the past year chasing different pieces of the same puzzle: the James Webb Space Telescope has probed the "little red dots" that may be the first supermassive black holes, and Chandra has watched a supposed supernova impostor that turned out to hide a black hole companion. The gravitational-wave census adds a third angle — not a single exotic object, but the demographics of the ordinary, ten-solar-mass black holes that dominate the merging population.

## What comes next

The test of the failed-supernova idea is, in the end, a matter of counting. LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA keep adding events, and every new merger sharpens the picture. With a larger catalogue, the gap — if it is real — will become harder to explain away, and the peak's shape will come into finer focus. The authors also suggest modelling repeated mergers through their spins, which could sharpen the test by separating first-generation black holes from ones built by earlier collisions. And fully general-relativistic simulations of failed supernovae will be needed to predict the detailed shape of the peak — not just that it exists, but exactly where it sits and how wide it is.

There is also the prospect of catching a failed supernova in the act, or at least in its aftermath. Surveys that monitor millions of stars night after night are now capable of noticing when one disappears, and the candidate N6946-BH1 shows that such disappearances can be found. A confirmed case — a massive star, monitored for years, that fades without an explosion, with a black hole's gravitational signature eventually detected nearby — would turn an interpretation into an observation. Until then, the gravitational-wave census is the strongest evidence available, and it is getting stronger with every observing run.

The study was funded by the National Science Foundation, the U.S. Department of Energy, and the Simons Foundation. LIGO itself is operated by Caltech and MIT. Those details matter because they describe a measurement that no single telescope, and no single country, could make: a decade of listening by detectors on three continents, pooled into catalogues that any researcher can analyse, and a model built to ask whether the universe's most common black holes share a single, silent origin.

The answer, for now, is a qualified yes. The data are consistent with most binary black holes forming from a very special kind of failed supernova. The peak is real. The gap is favoured but not proven. And somewhere in the dark, the stars that died without a sound are still merging — their final chirps the only eulogy they will ever get.
