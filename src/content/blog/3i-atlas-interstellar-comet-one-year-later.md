Next week marks one year since the strangest comet in the history of astronomy plunged past the Sun. On October 29, 2025, an object from another star system — only the third interstellar visitor ever found — rounded our star at terrifying speed and kept going. Today, 3I/ATLAS is somewhere beyond Jupiter's orbit, outbound on a trajectory that will never bend back toward us. We will never see it again.

But it is not done talking to us. Two new studies published this month — one in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, one reported this week — show that the comet essentially shed its skin during its solar passage, losing dust hundreds of kilograms per second and possibly having meters of its surface stripped away by the Sun's heat. They are the latest chapters in one of the richest observing campaigns ever mounted for a single object: a year in which the James Webb Space Telescope, Hubble, two ESA Mars orbiters, radio telescopes, and alien-hunting arrays all took their turn staring at a chunk of another solar system.

## A visitor from another star

The story began on July 1, 2025, when the ATLAS survey telescope in Chile picked up a faint moving dot against the stars of Sagittarius. Follow-up observations revealed something extraordinary: the object was traveling on an open, hyperbolic orbit — the kind of path that never closes into a loop. That meant it was not one of ours. It had fallen in from interstellar space, been bent slightly by the Sun's gravity, and would be flung back out into the dark between the stars. Astronomers designated it 3I/ATLAS, the third confirmed interstellar object ever detected, after 1I/'Oumuamua in 2017 and 2I/Borisov in 2019.

Each of the three has been weirder than the last. 'Oumuamua was a dark, elongated sliver that showed no visible coma yet accelerated in a way no one could fully explain — sparking years of debate. Borisov behaved like a fairly ordinary comet, but with a chemistry richer in carbon monoxide than anything in our solar system. 3I/ATLAS was different again: big, bright, and wildly active, with a glowing coma that grew as it approached the Sun. It was, in the best sense, the most comet-like of the three — and the most informative, because scientists had months of warning to organize what became the largest coordinated observing campaign ever mounted for an interstellar object.

NASA quickly confirmed the object posed no threat to Earth. But that reassurance did nothing to dampen the excitement. For planetary scientists, every interstellar object is a free sample return mission from another star system: unlike exoplanets, whose compositions must be inferred from light-years away, these travelers carry the actual chemistry of their parent systems into our backyard, where telescopes can examine them in unprecedented detail.

## The Mars flyby: our closest look

The geometry of the encounter was almost cruelly teasing. 3I/ATLAS would never come closer to Earth than about 1.8 astronomical units — nearly twice the Earth-Sun distance — but it would sweep past Mars at just 30 million kilometers, about a fifth of that. For once, the best seat in the house was not on Earth but at the Red Planet.

Between October 1 and October 7, 2025, the European Space Agency's ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter and Mars Express turned their cameras toward the visitor as it swept past. At its closest on October 3, the comet came within roughly 30 million kilometers of the two orbiters — about 18.6 million miles — nearer to Mars than Earth would ever get to this icy traveler. It would be the closest view of an interstellar object anyone would ever get.

The pictures came from the Colour and Stereo Surface Imaging System (CaSSIS) aboard the ExoMars orbiter — a camera built to photograph the bright surface of Mars from a few hundred kilometers away, not a dim speck of ice tens of millions of kilometers out in the dark. To catch the comet at all, the science team stacked multiple five-second exposures into a single sequence. In the resulting animation, 3I/ATLAS appears as a small, fuzzy white dot drifting across a field of streaked stars. That fuzziness is the comet's coma: a halo of gas and dust a few thousand kilometers wide, boiling off the nucleus as sunlight warmed it.

Resolving the solid nucleus itself was never on the table — that would have been like spotting a mobile phone sitting on the Moon from Earth. But the images, combined with Hubble's sharp eye, helped astronomers narrow the nucleus down to roughly 2.5 kilometers across — considerably larger than both 'Oumuamua and Borisov. This was no pebble. It was a mountain of ancient ice.

## The plunge — and the brightening nobody could explain

After the Mars encounter, the comet dove toward perihelion, its closest approach to the Sun on October 29, 2025 — vanishing into the Sun's glare from Earth's perspective as it passed through solar conjunction. Astronomers expected it to brighten as solar heat vaporized its ices, releasing the gas and dust that reflect sunlight. What they did not expect was how violently it did so.

Between mid-September and late October, 3I/ATLAS's luminosity surged far faster than typical comets — its brightening followed an unusually steep trend, scaling roughly as the inverse of its distance to the power of 7.5, about double the rate astronomers expected. By perihelion, the comet may have reached around magnitude 9, hundreds of times brighter than when it was first seen. "The reason for 3I's rapid brightening, which far exceeds the brightening rate of most Oort Cloud comets at similar distances, remains unclear," wrote astronomers Qicheng Zhang and Karl Battams, who documented the surge.

Normally, a comet's activity ramps up smoothly as its ices sublimate. In 3I/ATLAS's case, some unusual mechanism or property must have triggered the extra boost. Researchers have floated several ideas: perhaps the comet's high approach speed drove more violent outgassing, or perhaps something about the nucleus — its composition, its shape, its internal structure — led to a sudden breakup of surface material. "Oddities in nucleus properties like composition, shape, or structure — which might have been acquired from its host system or over its long interstellar journey — may likewise contribute to the rapid brightening," Zhang and Battams noted.

The comet emerged from behind the Sun in late 2025, reappearing in the morning sky — and immediately offered a new spectacle. On November 26, 2025, the Gemini North telescope captured images of a striking green glow: diatomic carbon (C2) and cyanogen (CN) gases fluorescing in the coma, unusually prominent in this visitor. Then, between December 17 and 21, 2025, as Earth crossed the comet's orbital plane, 3I/ATLAS displayed an "anti-tail" — a rare optical illusion in which a streak of dust more than 500,000 kilometers long appeared to point toward the Sun rather than away from it, rotating and even reversing direction as the geometry shifted.

## The chemistry of deep time

But the real revolution came from spectroscopy. Recognizing the object's scientific importance, Webb's observing schedule was interrupted so researchers could point its Near-Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec) at the comet in December 2025, just as it was moving away from the Sun with its ancient ices freshly warmed and sublimating into a bright, observable coma. What they found, published over the following months, rewrote what we know about where and when this object formed.

First, the methane. Webb recorded the first-ever direct detection of methane gas streaming from an interstellar object. Because methane molecules are easily destroyed by prolonged exposure to harsh cosmic radiation, its presence meant Webb was reading pristine material boiling off from deep inside the comet's interior — material shielded from radiation since before our solar system existed. The results were published in April 2026 (Belyakov et al., ApJL 1001 L11).

Second, the carbon dioxide. Webb found carbon dioxide outnumbering water eight to one in the comet's coma — sixteen times higher than expected, the highest ratio ever measured in any comet. Something in this object's history had preserved or enriched carbon dioxide far beyond anything seen in our own solar system's comets.

Third — and most profound — the isotopes. In a study published in Nature on June 22, 2026, a team led by astro-chemist Martin Cordiner of NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center reported that the comet's water carried deuterium — heavy hydrogen — at levels roughly 30 times higher than typically found in solar system comets. At the same time, the comet contained only trace amounts of carbon-13 relative to the lighter carbon-12, a pattern associated with very old cosmic origins, since carbon-13 builds up over successive generations of star formation. The measurements pointed to a birthplace colder than 30 kelvin, in a metal-poor stellar nursery. Their conclusion: 3I/ATLAS likely formed 10 to 12 billion years ago — roughly three times older than the Sun.

"This was a unique opportunity to study an ancient object from the distant galaxy, probably pre-dating our Sun and solar system," Cordiner said. "On the one hand, we get direct insight into that distant time and place, and on the other, we learn something about how unusual our own solar system may be."

The strangeness kept coming. Hubble, which tracked the comet from July 2025 onward, helped pin the nucleus at about 2.5 kilometers across. The XRISM and XMM-Newton X-ray observatories detected an X-ray halo around the comet — the first X-ray emission ever seen from an interstellar object, produced as the solar wind collided with outgassing oxygen and carbon. Ground-based radio arrays including ALMA, along with the SPHEREx space telescope, detected additional chemical species in the coma, including methanol, hydrogen cyanide, and heat-resistant dust particles. December analyses even pointed to a higher nickel-to-iron ratio than typically seen in local comets — another fingerprint of a star system with a different chemical history than ours.

Then came nitrogen. Using a new instrument called WEAVE-LIFU on the William Herschel Telescope in the Canary Islands, astronomers observed the comet on November 30 and December 2, 2025, just after perihelion, and detected nitrogen, carbon monoxide, hydroxyl, and methylidyne ions in an antisolar tail. Their analysis set a lower limit on the nitrogen-to-carbon-monoxide ratio indicating that 3I/ATLAS is nitrogen-rich compared to solar system comets — a property that, again, points to formation in an extremely cold environment, far from its host star, in the equivalent of that star's Oort cloud. The emerging picture was consistent and remarkable: this comet was born in the deep freeze at the outskirts of an ancient star system, preserved for billions of years, and is now delivering that primordial chemistry to our telescopes.

## New: the dust tells a story of violent shedding

That brings us to this month. In a paper published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters (ApJL 1008, L26) and posted to the arXiv on October 15, 2026, Bin Liu and colleagues presented a detailed analysis of the Zwicky Transient Facility's observations of 3I/ATLAS, tracking how its dust activity evolved before and after perihelion.

The numbers are striking. Before perihelion, the comet was losing dust at an estimated 123 to 203 kilograms per second. After perihelion, that rate jumped to between 596 and 1,412 kilograms per second — several times higher. The inner coma faded faster after the solar passage than before it, and the comet's surface-brightness profiles steepened sharply in late November and early December before relaxing by January. That phase coincided with an anomalously blue outer coma that had disappeared by January — a color signature the researchers attribute to a short-lived component of volatile-bearing icy grains, roughly 5 to 10 micrometers across, containing a small absorbing fraction of carbonaceous material.

In plain language: as 3I/ATLAS rounded the Sun, it violently exhaled. The early post-perihelion coma contained a transient population of icy grains that could not survive long in the inner solar system, and their destruction painted the comet's outer halo blue before they vanished. The study gives astronomers a way to connect the comet's changing chemical signals with possible depths inside the nucleus — separating what 3I/ATLAS inherited at its birth from what happened to it on its way through our neighborhood.

A second study, reported this week, goes further. By modeling the comet's decreasing size and increasing production rates through its solar passage, researchers concluded that the Sun's intense radiation may have stripped away several meters of material from the comet's surface as it approached perihelion — essentially flaying the outer layers off the ancient nucleus. The authors themselves caution that the conclusion is not definitive and requires further investigation, since it depends on assumptions about the comet's internal structure and the role of different ices in shaping its chemical signals. But if correct, it means the bright, active object we watched last autumn was a comet being peeled open, exposing fresher and older layers as it went.

## The search for something artificial — and the long farewell

No account of 3I/ATLAS would be complete without the episode that briefly set the internet alight. Because the object was so unusual, some commentators speculated wildly about artificial origins — a déjà vu of the debates that surrounded 'Oumuamua. The scientific response was exactly what it should have been: check. The Allen Telescope Array and the Breakthrough Listen project both searched the comet for artificial radio signals. They found nothing. Whatever 3I/ATLAS is, it is gloriously, fascinatingly natural.

And now it is leaving. The comet passed Jupiter in March 2026 and is currently beyond the giant planet's orbit, falling back into the dark on its open hyperbolic path. Its speed exceeds the solar system's escape velocity, so there is no coming back: it will coast outward past Neptune and into interstellar space, joining the vast population of objects thought to drift between the stars. The window to observe it is closing fast; the data collected during that window is all astronomers will ever have.

That finality is part of what makes this story matter. Every interstellar visitor is a one-shot experiment. Missions to intercept such an object have been proposed — including the European Space Agency's Comet Interceptor, designed to wait in space for a suitable target — but for 3I/ATLAS, no spacecraft was in position. We did it all with telescopes, and telescopes delivered: methane on an interstellar object, carbon dioxide at record levels, deuterium at 30 times the solar-system norm, a nitrogen-rich tail, an X-ray halo, a green coma, an anti-tail, and now evidence of a nucleus shedding its skin as it rounded our star.

A year ago next week, a mountain of ice older than the Sun fell past it, breathed out secrets no solar-system comet ever could, and kept going. The comet is gone. The science is just getting started.
