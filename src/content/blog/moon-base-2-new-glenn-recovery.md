The Moon is getting a new address book. On May 26, NASA announced that the Griffin-1 lunar lander mission — the big, boxy commercial spacecraft that Astrobotic plans to send to the lunar south pole later this year — would henceforth be known as "Moon Base 2." The renaming is more than a coat of fresh paint. It signals that NASA is done treating its Commercial Lunar Payload Services program as a loose experiment and has begun assembling it, deliberately and visibly, into something with an architecture's name on it: a moon base.

The timing could hardly be more charged. Just two days after the May 26 unveiling in Pittsburgh, a New Glenn rocket exploded on its launch pad at Cape Canaveral during a routine engine test, shredding key ground equipment and casting a long shadow over the schedules of everyone who depends on Blue Origin — including the team behind Moon Base 1. Now, as the dust literally settles at Launch Complex 36, NASA is placing its bets on Moon Base 2 to carry the agency's lunar momentum through 2026.

## A Name with a Mission

The unveiling took place at Astrobotic's headquarters in Pittsburgh, where the company presented the Griffin Mission One lander inside its clean room and spacecraft integration facility. Representatives from NASA, Astrolab, Voyager Technologies, the Keystone Space Collaborative, and the Moonshot Museum gathered to see the spacecraft as it nears the end of final integration.

At the event, NASA announced the new designation: Griffin-1 would be called Moon Base 2, part of a renaming of three robotic lander missions already under development through Commercial Lunar Payload Services, the agency's program for buying rides to the Moon from private companies. Moon Base 1 is Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 1 lander. Moon Base 3 will ride an Intuitive Machines lander carrying science instruments including the Lunar Vertex package.

"We're using CLPS as the vehicle, but we want to approach it a little bit different way. The contracts haven't changed, but our focus and attention has," Carlos García-Galán, NASA's program executive for the Moon Base effort, said in an interview around the unveiling. The shift, he explained, puts the full weight of the agency behind the companies developing the landers — more NASA experts, more testing facilities, more institutional knowledge about the dark art of landing on other worlds.

That institutional knowledge is sorely needed. Of the four CLPS missions that have flown so far, only one — Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost 1 — touched down safely and completed its full mission. Intuitive Machines' first two lunar landers each toppled over at touchdown, severely limiting what they could accomplish. And Astrobotic's own first attempt, the Peregrine-1 lander launched in January 2024, never got the chance to try: a propellant leak from a defective oxidizer valve crippled the spacecraft hours after launch, and it burned up in Earth's atmosphere ten days later, its thirteen NASA science payloads lost.

NASA is betting that more hands-on involvement can tilt the odds. "We're basically working to put the entire NASA might behind these companies that are developing landers," García-Galán said, "including access to NASA subject matter experts and testing facilities."

## Griffin-1: The Heavyweight

Griffin is among the largest lunar landers developed under the CLPS program, and when it flies it is expected to deliver the largest commercial payload ever sent to the lunar surface. The mission will carry more than 1,100 pounds of cargo to the Moon's south polar region — the same forbidding, shadow-haunted terrain that NASA's Artemis astronauts are ultimately meant to explore.

The lander takes its name from the mythical griffin, the lion-bodied, eagle-headed creature that for centuries symbolized strength and the guardianship of treasure. Astrobotic says the name fits a spacecraft whose job is to haul and protect valuable cargo across a quarter-million miles of void.

When launch day comes — currently targeted for late 2026, pending completion of testing and integration — Griffin will ride to space aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy lifting off from Launch Complex 39A at NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida. The Falcon Heavy, with its three reusable boosters, is one of the few rockets on the market with the muscle to throw such a heavy lander toward the Moon. The choice also says something about how far the commercial launch market has matured: NASA's flagship lunar cargo missions now fly on the same rocket family that launches Starlink satellites and national-security payloads.

García-Galán has framed the mission as a stepping stone rather than a stunt. The south pole is where NASA hopes to build its permanent presence — the water ice trapped in permanently shadowed craters could supply drinking water, breathing air, and even rocket propellant. But the agency still knows the region mostly from orbit. "We really need to get ground truth on different areas of the south pole," he said, citing terrain and temperature conditions that can only be measured by something that actually lands there.

That is what Griffin-1 is for. It is a cargo run, a technology demonstration, and a scouting expedition rolled into one — and now it has a name to match the ambition.

## The Rovers Riding Along

The star passenger on Moon Base 2 is FLIP, Astrolab's four-wheeled rover, which recently completed its own payload integration and is now undergoing environmental testing and checkouts. FLIP is a pathfinder for the technology Astrolab is developing for its much larger lunar terrain vehicles — the Crewed Lunar Vehicle and the Flexible Logistics and Exploration rover — which are designed to eventually carry astronauts across the lunar surface on Artemis missions.

Joining FLIP is CubeRover, a small rover built by Carnegie Mellon University — the same institution from which Astrobotic itself was spun out as a robotics research venture by founder John Thornton. The symmetry is hard to miss: a university spinout returns to its roots, sending a university-built rover to the Moon on its own lander.

The pair will do what rovers do best: trundle across alien terrain, photograph it, measure it, and report back. For FLIP, the mission is a proving ground for Astrolab's mobility systems. For CubeRover, it is a demonstration that small, relatively cheap rovers can survive and operate in the brutal thermal swings of the lunar surface. Together, they give NASA the ground truth García-Galán is asking for — the first close-up look at the exact sort of terrain where a moon base might one day stand.

## The Ghost of Peregrine

Astrobotic's path to Moon Base 2 runs through failure, and the company does not pretend otherwise. Peregrine-1, launched in January 2024 aboard a United Launch Alliance Vulcan Centaur rocket, was NASA's first CLPS mission — and it ended in heartbreak. The defective oxidizer valve, the propellant leak, the slow realization over the following hours that the Moon was unreachable: it was the kind of public failure that can define a young company.

What followed has become a case study in how the new space economy treats failure. Astrobotic did not fold. It pressed ahead with Griffin, a far larger and more capable lander originally designed to carry NASA's VIPER rover to the south pole. When NASA canceled VIPER in July 2024, the agency kept the CLPS task order for Griffin-1 alive — and later revived VIPER to fly instead on Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 1. Griffin kept its manifest of commercial and scientific payloads and kept its date with destiny.

Then, in June 2026, the company found itself with new owners. Voyager Technologies, a defense and space technology firm, announced it was acquiring Astrobotic for up to $300 million. Astrobotic would operate as Voyager Lunar Systems — the dedicated lunar arm of Voyager's portfolio — headquartered at the company's "Moon Base" facility in Pittsburgh. Voyager's president of defense and national security, Matt Magaña, attended the May 26 unveiling and spoke of scaling the company toward its biggest goal yet: putting a habitat on the lunar surface.

For NASA, the acquisition is a feature, not a bug. The whole premise of CLPS is that commercial companies — backed by private capital, free to merge and grow — can build a lunar transportation business more cheaply and flexibly than the agency ever could alone.

## The Elephant in the Sky

None of this happens in a vacuum — and certainly not while a rocket is still smoldering on a launch pad seventy miles from the Moon Base 2 clean room. On May 28, just two days after the Griffin unveiling, a New Glenn rocket exploded during an integrated-vehicle hotfire test at Launch Complex 36 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station.

The details, as Blue Origin has reported them so far, are stark. The rocket was on the pad for a preflight engine test when a significant anomaly tore through the vehicle. The blast destroyed the rocket itself, along with the lightning tower, the transporter-erector — the massive structure that moves the rocket to the pad and raises it vertical — and hydraulic cylinders. Drone footage shared by CEO Dave Limp in June showed the launch mount and launch bridge collapsed into the flame trench, twisted metal where a spaceport used to be.

There was better news mixed in. On June 1, Limp said inspections indicated the damage was less extensive than initially feared: the propellant storage tanks and the water tower had survived largely intact, avoiding the need to replace items with long manufacturing lead times, and the launch tower itself could be repaired rather than replaced. The first stage already built and named *Never Tell Me The Odds* — along with three second stages stored in the nearby integration facility — were undamaged. Because the transporter-erector was destroyed, Blue Origin would accelerate a previously planned redesign, replacing the old horizontal-integration scheme with a vertical integration system.

The explosion mattered far beyond Blue Origin's own manifest. New Glenn's fourth orbital flight was supposed to carry 48 Amazon Leo satellites, and the rocket is one of the few heavy lifters available to NASA's CLPS contractors and national-security customers. Most pointedly for the Moon Base program, the explosion threw a cloud over the schedule of Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 1 lander — Moon Base 1 — which NASA had revived as the new ride for the VIPER rover. As of the June announcements, Moon Base 2 was now likely to fly first, a reversal of the intended order that nobody at NASA had planned for.

The FAA had already been circling New Glenn's operations. On May 22, a week before the explosion, the agency closed its investigation into an unrelated anomaly on the rocket's previous flight — a cryogenic leak that froze a hydraulic line and produced a thrust anomaly during the second-stage burn. The FAA cleared New Glenn to return to flight with nine corrective actions. That clearance, of course, concerned the April flight, not the wreckage now being cleared from LC-36. A separate draft environmental assessment on raising New Glenn's launch cadence was also in motion, a reminder of how quickly Blue Origin had intended to ramp up.

## Recovery at the Cape

By mid-June, the recovery effort was visibly underway. Hundreds of Blue Origin personnel were assigned to the pad, and Limp was posting a steady stream of updates on social media — the modern equivalent of a ship's log for a disaster at sea.

The emerging plan, as Limp described it, was to avoid rebuilding the destroyed transporter-erector at all. Instead of rolling New Glenn out horizontally and raising it at the pad — the process used on its earlier flights — the company would mate the first and second stages horizontally inside the integration facility, move the assembled stack to the pad, and raise it vertically with a crane onto a refurbished launch table. The payload would be lifted and attached after the rocket was upright. It is a significant change to ground operations, touching lifting sequences, mechanical interfaces, fueling systems, and safety procedures — all of which have to be proven before the next flight.

Limp said he expected to return to flight before the end of 2026, and that the company would keep producing New Glenn vehicles during the downtime, storing completed stages for future launches. Veteran observers of the industry have called the timeline aggressive — engineers who lived through SpaceX's AMOS-6 pad explosion in 2016 know how long pad rebuilds can take — but Blue Origin has not wavered publicly from the year-end objective.

As of late June, the cause of the explosion remained under investigation, with wreckage recovered from the pad and component tests underway. What was clear was the scale of the ambition on both sides of the equation: Blue Origin rebuilding a spaceport at breakneck speed, and NASA pressing ahead with a lunar program that cannot afford to wait.

## Why the Name Matters

It would be easy to dismiss "Moon Base 2" as marketing — a snappier label for a mission that already had a perfectly good name. But NASA's rebranding of its CLPS missions is doing real work. For years, the program's robotic landers have flown as one-off missions with company-chosen names: Peregrine, Odysseus, Blue Ghost, Athena. Each was, in effect, its own story. The Moon Base banner stitches them into a single narrative: these are not stunts. They are deliveries. They are the logistics chain for a permanent human presence.

The naming also manages expectations. A moon base will not be built in a single landing. It will arrive the way the International Space Station did — module by module, cargo run by cargo run, with failures and retries along the way. Moon Base 1, Moon Base 2, Moon Base 3: the numbering promises a sequence, a program, a cadence. It tells Congress, international partners, and the public that the agency has a plan and is executing it in order.

For Astrobotic, the designation is both an honor and a burden. Griffin-1 is now formally the second installment in NASA's moon base story — and, thanks to the New Glenn explosion, quite possibly the first to actually fly. If it lands safely at the south pole, deploys FLIP and CubeRover, and phones home with data about the terrain, it will vindicate the entire CLPS model: a startup that failed publicly in 2024, bought by a defense tech firm in 2026, delivering the Moon's future on a SpaceX rocket.

If it fails, the questions will be louder this time. NASA is putting, in García-Galán's words, "the entire NASA might" behind these companies. The agency has staked its lunar credibility on the proposition that commercial landers can be made reliable. The south pole will not grade on a curve.

## The Road to Late 2026

As June draws to a close, Griffin-1 is in the final stages of integration in Pittsburgh, preparing to begin a comprehensive environmental testing campaign — the vibration, thermal vacuum, and acoustic trials that simulate the violence of launch and the cruelty of space. The spacecraft has already been presented to the world; now it has to prove it can survive the journey.

The calendar ahead is crowded. Artemis II, the first crewed flight around the Moon in more than half a century, is behind the program, and Artemis III — the mission meant to put astronauts back on the surface — remains targeted for mid-2027. Hardware for Artemis IV and V is moving into integration at Kennedy Space Center. Work continues with Blue Origin and SpaceX on the human landing systems. And China, by its own public statements, is aiming for crewed landings before the end of the decade.

In that race, the unglamorous cargo runs matter as much as the flags and footprints. Nobody builds a base without deliveries. Moon Base 2 will not carry astronauts. It will not plant a flag. But if it lands — a heavy commercial lander, guided by NASA's hard-won expertise, setting down in the most valuable real estate in the solar system with a rover that will scout the ground where humans may one day live — it will be the most important Moon landing since the last one.

The griffin of myth guarded treasure. NASA is counting on this one to deliver it.
