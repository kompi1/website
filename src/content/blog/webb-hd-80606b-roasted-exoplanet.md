Every 111 days, a world 217 light-years from Earth endures the most violent weather in the known galaxy. For most of its year, the gas giant HD 80606 b drifts through the deep freeze of interplanetary space, hundreds of millions of kilometers from its sun-like star. Then, on an orbit so stretched and lopsided that it barely resembles a circle at all, the planet plunges inward — falling toward its star until it skims just 0.03 astronomical units above the stellar surface, roughly eight times closer than Mercury orbits our own Sun. In a matter of hours, its atmosphere heats up by some 1,100 degrees Fahrenheit. Storms of unimaginable ferocity tear through its skies. Molecules that were stable in the cold are ripped apart and reformed in the heat. And then, just as quickly, the planet swings back out into the dark.

Astronomers have long called it the "roasted exoplanet" — NASA even gave it its own poster in the agency's beloved exoplanet travel-poster series. But until now, nobody had ever watched the roasting happen in detail. This week, at the 248th meeting of the American Astronomical Society in Pasadena, California, a team led by Tiffany Kataria of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory presented the first time-resolved portrait of the planet's inferno, captured by the James Webb Space Telescope's Mid-Infrared Instrument. The verdict: the flash-heating is even more extreme than anyone anticipated.

## A different beast

To appreciate what makes HD 80606 b special, start with what hot Jupiters usually look like. The classic hot Jupiter is a gas giant parked in a tight, nearly circular orbit just a few million kilometers from its star, tidally locked so that one face bakes in permanent daylight while the other radiates into the dark. They are extreme places, but they are steady-state extreme places: the heat is always on, the atmospheric circulation settles into a stable pattern, and astronomers can model them with reasonable confidence.

HD 80606 b, as Kataria puts it, is a completely different beast. The planet is about four times the mass of Jupiter, but it follows one of the most eccentric orbits ever measured for a planet — an ellipse so elongated that its distance from its star varies by a factor of more than twenty over the course of each 111-day year. At its farthest point, the planet receives barely more starlight than Earth does. At periastron, its closest approach, the flood of stellar radiation is roughly 800 times stronger. No other known planet subjects its atmosphere to such a dramatic, such a rapid transformation.

That transformation is exactly what makes the planet scientifically precious. Most exoplanets require years of patient observation to reveal how their atmospheres behave under different conditions. HD 80606 b compresses an enormous range of atmospheric conditions — from frigid to furnace — into a single, repeating, few-hour event. It is, in effect, a natural laboratory for atmospheric physics, one that lets scientists watch chemistry and cloud formation respond to a thermal shock in real time.

## Years in the planning

Catching the event was anything but easy. The observation, presented as "Flash-Heating a Roasted Planet: Time-Resolved Characterization of HD 80606b with JWST/MIRI," required Webb to stare at the planet continuously before, during, and after periastron — and the timing had to be exquisite. The planet's 111-day orbit meant the team had a narrow window, and Webb's own restrictions on where it can point at different times of year, dictated by Earth's position in its orbit and the telescope's sunshield geometry, narrowed that window further. Scheduling the run took years of careful planning.

The geometry of the observation added one more gift. During periastron, HD 80606 b also passes behind its star as seen from Earth — a secondary eclipse. That alignment is extraordinarily useful: by measuring the total infrared light of the star-plus-planet system just before the planet disappears behind the star, and then the starlight alone while it is hidden, astronomers can subtract one from the other and isolate the planet's own thermal glow with remarkable precision. Combined with the continuous light curve leading into and out of the eclipse, the team could reconstruct how the planet's temperature evolved hour by hour through the heating event.

Webb's Mid-Infrared Instrument, MIRI, was the ideal tool for the job. Infrared light is heat made visible, and MIRI's spectrographs can split that light into its component wavelengths — a technique called spectroscopy — revealing not just how hot the planet is but what its atmosphere is made of. Different molecules absorb and emit at different wavelengths, leaving chemical fingerprints in the spectrum. Where the now-retired Spitzer Space Telescope, which pioneered infrared studies of this planet, could measure the overall brightness, Webb can dissect it.

## Hotter than expected

The headline result is the sheer violence of the heating. Webb confirmed that the planet's temperature shoots upward by about 1,100 degrees Fahrenheit — roughly 590 degrees Celsius — during the periastron passage, and the data show the increase was even more extreme than models based on Spitzer's earlier observations had predicted. "Webb has shown that the planet's increase in temperature was even more extreme than we anticipated based on Spitzer data," Kataria said.

Why the surprise? Spitzer's measurements, groundbreaking in their day, were essentially thermometers: they measured how much infrared light the planet emitted, but with limited spectral detail. The new Webb data reveal that the atmosphere does not simply get hotter — it changes character. Rapid heating can vaporize cloud particles, dissociate molecules, and drive supersonic winds that redistribute heat around the planet faster than the old models assumed. The temperature spike Webb measured is the signature of an atmosphere being pushed far out of equilibrium, then scrambling to catch up.

The team has only begun to analyze what Kataria's colleagues describe as an incredibly rich dataset. The temperature curve alone — the hour-by-hour rise and fall — encodes information about how quickly the atmosphere absorbs heat, how winds carry that heat from the day side to the night side, and how the planet's interior responds to a thermal pulse that penetrates deeper with each orbit. Every wiggle in the light curve is a clue.

## Reading the air

Temperature is only half the story. The other half is chemistry, and this is where Webb's advance over Spitzer is most dramatic. "Spitzer did amazing work on this exoplanet, and now Webb is building on that legacy by enabling us to drill down to distinguish specific chemical signatures like methane and carbon dioxide, which is just amazing progress," said Ryan Challener, a co-author of the study and research associate at the Cornell Center for Astrophysics and Planetary Science. "There's so much to learn from this one dataset here — we really are just getting started deciphering what Webb has to tell us."

Methane and carbon dioxide are familiar molecules — the first dominates the atmospheres of Jupiter and Saturn, the second is the gas driving climate change on Earth — but detecting them on an exoplanet undergoing a 1,100-degree thermal shock is something else entirely. In the frigid stretches of HD 80606 b's orbit, methane should be stable and abundant, much as it is on the Solar System's gas giants. As the planet plunges toward its star and temperatures soar, methane molecules are expected to break apart, their carbon and hydrogen recycled into carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and other high-temperature species. Watching that chemical transformation unfold in real time — methane fading as the planet heats, then reforming as it cools — would be a direct observation of atmospheric chemistry operating far from equilibrium, a regime that barely exists on the well-behaved planets of our own Solar System.

The presence of carbon dioxide is equally telling. On a planet this hot during periastron, CO2 is a sensitive tracer of the atmosphere's carbon-to-oxygen ratio, which in turn encodes the story of where and how the planet formed. Did HD 80606 b assemble far from its star, in the cold outer reaches of its protoplanetary disk where water ice was abundant, and then get flung inward by gravitational interactions? Or did it form closer in, from different raw materials? The chemical fingerprints Webb is now reading are, in a very real sense, fossils of the planet's birth.

## Why eccentric giants matter

HD 80606 b belongs to a small but growing class of planets that are reshaping how astronomers think about planetary systems. The extreme eccentricity of its orbit is not an accident — it is a scar. Planets are born in flat, orderly disks of gas and dust, and they start life in neat, circular orbits. Something violent must have happened to HD 80606 b: a gravitational shove from another massive planet, perhaps, or a slow gravitational dance with the planet's distant binary companion star, HD 80607, that pumped the orbit into its current elongated shape over millions of years.

That violent history makes eccentric hot Jupiters Rosetta Stones for planetary migration. The dominant theories for how hot Jupiters end up so close to their stars all involve some kind of orbital upheaval — planets forming far out and migrating inward through the disk, or being scattered inward by gravitational encounters and then having their orbits circularized by tides. HD 80606 b appears to be caught in the middle of that story: its orbit is still eccentric, meaning tidal forces have not yet had time to circularize it. It is a snapshot of migration in progress, a process that for most hot Jupiters finished billions of years ago.

There is also a practical reason the planet matters. The coming years will bring an avalanche of atmospheric data from Webb and from future observatories, and astronomers need benchmark objects — planets whose atmospheres are well understood — against which to interpret the flood. HD 80606 b, with its extreme but repeating and predictable heating cycle, is becoming exactly that: a reference point for hot Jupiter studies, a world where the same experiment runs every 111 days and the results can be checked, refined, and compared.

## From Spitzer's legacy to Webb's future

The story of HD 80606 b is also the story of infrared astronomy coming of age. When Spitzer turned its modest 85-centimeter mirror toward the planet in the late 2000s, the detection of the periastron heating was itself a triumph — proof that exoplanet atmospheres could be studied at all. Spitzer's measurements defined the questions: how fast does the planet heat, how deep does the heat penetrate, what happens to the clouds? Webb, with its 6.5-meter gold-coated mirror and its exquisitely sensitive spectrographs, is now delivering the answers, wavelength by wavelength.

The observation is a technical achievement in its own right. MIRI operates at temperatures just a few degrees above absolute zero, cooled by a cryocooler to suppress its own thermal glow. Holding that instrument steady on a single planetary system for the many hours bracketing periastron — while the telescope's exquisite pointing system kept the star centered to a fraction of a pixel — required the kind of operational choreography that the Webb mission has spent years perfecting. That it worked flawlessly on one of the most demanding exoplanet observations ever attempted bodes well for even more ambitious campaigns to come.

And there will be more. The team has made clear that the analysis presented at the AAS meeting is only the beginning. The full dataset contains spectroscopic information across MIRI's wavelength range, and extracting every molecule, every cloud signature, and every wind pattern from it will take months or years. Future periastron passages offer the chance to watch the event again — to see whether the atmosphere behaves the same way every orbit, or whether the planet's weather has a chaotic streak. Other eccentric giants, discovered by NASA's TESS mission and by ground-based surveys, are waiting for the same treatment.

## The hunt for more roasted worlds

HD 80606 b may be the most dramatic example of its kind, but it is not alone. NASA's TESS mission and ground-based radial-velocity surveys have spent the last decade turning up gas giants on wildly elongated orbits — planets with names like HD 20782 b, whose eccentricity of 0.96 is the highest ever measured, and a growing census of warm Jupiters caught mid-migration. Each one is a potential target for the same kind of time-resolved infrared spectroscopy that Webb has now demonstrated. The technique Kataria's team perfected — the long stare bracketing periastron, timed to catch a secondary eclipse — is a template that can be reused, and the exoplanet community is already drawing up target lists.

That matters because the field is entering its data-rich era. Webb's exoplanet program in 2026 is simultaneously mapping the atmospheres of temperate rocky worlds, dissecting the clouds of directly imaged giants, and now flash-heating eccentric Jupiters — and each campaign calibrates the others. A molecule confidently identified in the roiling, high-signal atmosphere of HD 80606 b becomes easier to recognize in the faint, ambiguous spectrum of a smaller, cooler world. In that sense, the roasted planet is doing quiet service as a laboratory standard: the better we understand the extreme cases, the more we can trust our readings of the subtle ones.

The next decade will widen the hunt further. NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, which completed its build this year and is headed for launch, carries a coronagraph technology demonstrator designed to image giant planets directly — a completely different way of studying the same class of objects. On the ground, the Extremely Large Telescope rising in Chile's Atacama Desert will bring 39 meters of light-gathering power to bear on exoplanet atmospheres, resolving details Webb can only glimpse. And the concepts for a future Habitable Worlds Observatory, the mission astronomers hope will one day photograph an Earth twin, are being shaped right now by exactly the kind of atmospheric physics that HD 80606 b is teaching us: how heat moves, how chemistry responds, and how a planet's air tells the story of its past.

## A poster come to life

There is something fitting about the fact that HD 80606 b — the planet NASA's artists imagined as a scorched world for a travel poster — has turned out to be even more dramatic in reality than in illustration. The poster's tagline invited viewers to a world of extreme heat; Webb's data show a world where the heat arrives like a hammer blow, 1,100 degrees in a matter of hours, tearing apart molecules and rebuilding them as the planet swings back into the cold.

For the astronomers who have chased this planet since the Spitzer era, the new results are both a vindication and a beginning. The vindication is that the decades of planning, the careful scheduling, the faith that Webb could do what no telescope had done before — all of it paid off. The beginning is everything the dataset still holds: the full chemical inventory of a roiling atmosphere, the wind speeds, the cloud physics, the deep story of a planet that was thrown at its star and survived.

Every 111 days, HD 80606 b dives into the fire and comes back out. Now, for the first time, we are watching.
