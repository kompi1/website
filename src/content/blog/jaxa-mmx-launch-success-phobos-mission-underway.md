In the dark hours before dawn on Japan's Tanegashima Island, a pillar of orange fire rose from the Yoshinobu Launch Complex and climbed into a sky already turning pale at the edges. The two-stage H3-24L rocket, flight number H3 F10, carried one of the most ambitious deep-space missions ever attempted — the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's Martian Moons eXploration spacecraft, MMX. Liftoff came at 4:41 a.m. Japan Standard Time on Tuesday, October 20, 2026 — 7:41 p.m. UTC on Monday evening, or 3:41 p.m. Eastern Daylight Time on October 19 in the United States. One hour and eleven minutes later, the spacecraft separated from the rocket's upper stage exactly as planned, and the mood in the control room turned from nail-biting concentration to celebration: flight controllers burst into applause, jumped to their feet, and traded handshakes and embraces. Japan's grand quest to touch one of the moons of Mars — and bring a piece of it home — is officially underway.

The stakes are enormous. No spacecraft has ever landed on Phobos or its smaller sibling Deimos, and no one has ever returned samples from the Mars system. The $578 million mission's roughly 4,480-kilogram spacecraft will spend about ten months in interplanetary cruise before arriving at Mars in August 2027, then spend years surveying the moons, landing on Phobos, and scooping up at least ten grams of regolith before making the long voyage home. The return capsule is expected to descend by parachute into the Australian desert in 2031. If it succeeds, humanity will for the first time hold physical material from the orbital neighborhood of another planet — a handful of dust that could rewrite our understanding of how moons and planets form, and how the ingredients for life traveled through the young solar system.

"This is a mission that has been decades in the dreaming," the scene at Tanegashima suggested. JAXA first announced MMX in June 2015 and approved it for development in February 2020; six years of engineering, three modules, eleven science instruments, and the cooperation of five space agencies have now been compressed into a single blazing streak of light. For Japan, which has already mastered asteroid sample return but has never succeeded at Mars — the Nozomi probe failed to enter Mars orbit in 2003 — this is the mission that finally reaches the Red Planet. And it is reaching it with an international crew of hardware: American spectrometers, a German-French rover, a French-built infrared instrument, and an Australian landing zone, all lashed to a Japanese spacecraft riding a Japanese rocket.

## A Flawless Ride to Space

Every successful deep-space mission begins with the part that cannot be rehearsed at the destination: the launch. JAXA and contractor Mitsubishi Heavy Industries threaded a needle of orbital mechanics — Earth and Mars align for an efficient interplanetary transfer only about every 26 months, and this year's window was the one MMX's schedule could not afford to miss. A backup launch period ran from October 21 through November 7, but needing it would have meant delays rippling through a five-year choreography of flybys, landings, and departures. The rocket never gave them a reason to wait: the launch, the upper-stage burn, and payload separation all went exactly to plan.

The H3 is Japan's newest flagship rocket, the successor to the long-serving H-II series, and the flight was its tenth. It lifted from pad LP-2 at the Yoshinobu Launch Complex on Tanegashima, the largest rocket launch complex in Japan, perched on a subtropical island south of Kyushu. From that coastal site, the H3-24L — the variant with four strap-on solid rocket boosters — sent MMX arcing east over the Pacific and into an interplanetary trajectory. The spacecraft separated from the upper stage after one hour and eleven minutes of powered flight, deployed its solar arrays, and began the slow, precise work of orienting itself for the ten-month cruise.

"There's a lot of adrenaline and a lot of excitement and a lot of wow," NASA astrophysicist Tom Statler, who is involved in the project, told reporters as the mission began its journey. The remark captured a feeling familiar to anyone who has watched years of engineering collapse into a single, irreversible moment: once the rocket commits, there is no recall button. The spacecraft is now beyond any possibility of a redesign, a fix, or a second chance at the launch pad. Everything from here on — the midcourse corrections, the Mars orbit insertion, the landing on a world with one-thousandth of Earth's gravity — must work with the machine that exists, on the schedule that exists, in the radiation and cold that exist between the planets.

That irreversibility is part of what makes the early telemetry so reassuring. The launch went, as Sky & Telescope reported, "flawlessly" — a word launch directors do not use lightly. Separation confirmed, power positive, signal acquired: three simple phrases that stand in for thousands of individual systems behaving themselves at the same time. The rest of the mission is hard — almost impossibly hard in places — but it is the kind of hard that engineering can solve, now that the rocket has done its job.

## The Long Road to the Red Planet

MMX will arrive at Mars in August 2027, roughly ten months after leaving Earth. The timing is no accident: late 2026 offers a favorable biannual launch window to head to Mars, ahead of opposition in early 2027. MMX is not the only mission riding this window — NASA's two ESCAPADE spacecraft are already in space, currently orbiting near the Sun-Earth L2 Lagrange point, and are expected to make their way to Mars by September 2027. MMX will beat them there by about a month, arriving first at the planet both missions intend to study.

The spacecraft itself is a stack of three modules — propulsion, exploration, and return — designed to shed pieces of itself as the mission proceeds. After the long cruise and arrival at Mars, the propulsion module will be discarded once its job of getting there is done. The exploration and return modules will then settle into a careful campaign: first a series of flybys past Deimos, the more distant and sedate of Mars's two moons, and then a rendezvous with Phobos, the larger, closer, and stranger of the pair.

What follows is a long reconnaissance. MMX will use an innovative quasi-satellite orbit to station-keep near the small moons, spending roughly a year mapping topography, measuring gravity, analyzing surface composition, and monitoring the Martian climate. Only after this remote survey builds a complete picture will the mission commit to its riskiest maneuvers: descending to Phobos, touching down in near-weightless gravity, and collecting samples. Unlike the touch-and-go sampling maneuvers of missions like NASA's OSIRIS-REx, MMX will actually land on Phobos and remain there for a short period before sampling — a bolder approach made possible by the moon's feeble gravity, which is roughly one-thousandth of Earth's.

The sampling hardware comes in pairs, because JAXA is hedging its bets. The C-Sampler is designed to collect a subsurface core — digging beneath the radiation-weathered top layer to reach more pristine material. Flying alongside it is the P-Sampler, a version of Honeybee Robotics' PlanetVac technology that uses a "vacuum cleaner-like approach" to collect surface grains. As Honeybee's Kris Zacny explained before launch, the device is mounted on one of the lander's four legs and works with startling speed: "P-Sampler is mounted on one of the four legs of the MMX lander, and it's designed to complete its sampling operation in a matter of seconds," Zacny said. "You blink and its all done! P-Sampler uses vacuum cleaner-like approach to collect Phobos material, hence the name. It uses high-pressure, ultra-pure nitrogen gas to excavate and capture regolith without the need for any complex mechanisms." The same basic design has already proven itself in California's Mojave Desert and — tellingly — on the Moon, where it flew on Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost Mission 1 lander in 2025 and collected 13 grams of lunar regolith. It is one of the most flight-proven sampling mechanisms ever aimed at another world.

But the mission's most charming passenger is also its most daring: a 25-kilogram (55-pound) rover named IDEFIX, built by Germany's DLR and France's CNES. The name is a wink — Idefix is the French name for Asterix's small dog in the beloved comic series — and this little four-wheeled explorer is the mission's scrappy sidekick. MMX will drop it toward Phobos from about 40 meters above the surface, and in the moon's ghostly gravity the rover will begin a slow descent, bouncing several times before coming to rest. From there it will unfold its solar generator, recharge its batteries, and roam for at least 100 Earth days, using its cameras, radiometer, and Raman spectrometer to scout the terrain and provide ground truth for the orbiter's remote sensing. Its measurements of the surface's texture and composition will directly feed the choice of where the main spacecraft lands to sample — meaning a microwave-sized rover named after a cartoon dog could decide where humanity first digs into another planet's moon.

Sample collection itself is planned for no earlier than December 2028. The spacecraft is designed to lift off and touch down on Phobos a second time — possibly on the moon's other side — to collect another sample, before departing the Mars system in 2030. On the way out it will make additional flyby observations of Deimos. Then the exploration module is jettisoned, and the return module — carrying its precious cargo — makes the long cruise back to Earth. In 2031 the return capsule will separate, streak through Earth's atmosphere, and descend by parachute to the Woomera Prohibited Area in the South Australian desert, where the Australian Space Agency will lead recovery operations.

The target is at least ten grams of Phobos regolith — about the mass of five packets of sweetener. One gram will be distributed quickly to Japanese scientists and international partners for immediate study, while the remainder will be curated at the Extraterrestrial Sample Curation Center in Sagamihara, Japan, preserved for future researchers with tools not yet invented, answering questions scientists do not yet know how to ask.

## Why Phobos? The Strangest Moons in the Solar System

Seen through a telescope, Phobos and Deimos are unremarkable: two lumpy, crater-pocked rocks. Up close, they are among the most puzzling objects in the solar system. Phobos, the larger, measures about 22 kilometers across — roughly 27 kilometers long — and circles remarkably close to Mars, whipping around the planet once every 7 hours and 39 minutes, faster than Mars itself rotates. Deimos, about 16 kilometers wide, follows a more distant, sedate 30-hour orbit. Both are irregular, potato-shaped bodies — nothing like Earth's nearly spherical Moon — and their dark, carbonaceous surfaces have teased planetary scientists for nearly a century and a half.

For decades, two hypotheses have competed to explain them. The capture hypothesis holds that Phobos and Deimos are asteroids from the outer solar system — likely carbon-rich D-type asteroids — that wandered too close to Mars and were snared by its gravity. That would make them time capsules of the primordial solar system, among the most ancient and unaltered materials in the inner planets' neighborhood, and a direct record of how water and organic compounds migrated inward toward the young Earth. The giant impact hypothesis, by contrast, argues that the moons formed from debris blasted off Mars itself by an enormous collision in the planet's youth — a scenario echoing the leading theory for the formation of Earth's Moon. If Phobos is made of reaccreted Martian crust and mantle, its chemistry would reveal what the Martian interior was like billions of years ago.

As recent analyses have noted, the debate has only grown more complicated: surface spectra of the moons tend to favor a captured-asteroid origin, yet their near-equatorial, nearly circular orbits look more like debris-disk relics than captured wanderers. Remote sensing alone cannot break the deadlock — not from orbit, and not from the surface. Only laboratory analysis of actual samples, measured with instruments too large, too power-hungry, and too precise to fly on a spacecraft, can tell the difference. That is why MMX exists: ten grams of soil, read by the finest instruments on Earth, could settle one of planetary science's longest-running arguments and rewrite a chapter of solar system history.

## Reading Phobos Through Gamma-Ray Glasses

MMX carries eleven science instruments with names drawn from Japanese mythology and optics, but one of them embodies the mission's central detective work. MEGANE — the Mars-moon Exploration with GAmma rays and NEutrons — is a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer built by the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory as NASA's principal hardware contribution. "Megane" is the Japanese word for eyeglasses, and the pun is deliberate: the team thinks of the instrument as a pair of gamma-ray and neutron glasses that let MMX see through Phobos's surface and read the bulk chemistry of the moon.

"MEGANE is a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer that will make remote sensing measurements to characterize Phobos' elemental composition," said David Lawrence of Johns Hopkins APL, the instrument's principal investigator. "One of the primary mysteries surrounding Phobos (and Mars' other moon Deimos) is that we do not know how they formed." And then the crucial link between measurement and meaning: "It turns out that these two theories predict different elemental compositions for Phobos," Lawrence explained. "So by measuring these compositions, we expect to be able to help understand which theory is more consistent with our data." A captured asteroid and a chunk of blasted-off Mars would have different fingerprints — different ratios of elements forged in different parts of the early solar system — and MEGANE will read those fingerprints from low-altitude passes before the lander ever touches down, guiding the search for the most revealing sampling sites.

The rest of the instrument suite fills out the mission's senses. TENGOO, a telescopic nadir imager, will map surface geomorphology; OROCHI, an optical radiometer of chromatic imagers, will study color and brightness; a LIDAR will range and measure topography; and MIRS — the MMX Infrared Spectrometer supplied by France's CNES — will read surface mineralogy in the near-infrared. CMDM, a circum-Martian dust monitor, will watch for a faint ring of dust around Mars, and MSA, a mass spectrum analyzer, will sample the thin envelope of ions around the moons. Together they will build the most complete portrait ever assembled of two worlds that, until now, have been little more than smudges in telescope eyepieces and fleeting targets of opportunity for Mars orbiters.

## Five Agencies, One Moonshot

MMX is a JAXA-led mission, but it flies as a genuinely international effort — five of the world's space agencies have joined forces on this one small spacecraft. NASA's contribution goes beyond MEGANE and the P-Sampler: the agency is providing communications support through the Deep Space Network, and its 14-year life-cycle cost for the mission is $87 million. In return, NASA will receive at least 15 percent of the returned samples for analysis and long-term curation at Johnson Space Center — a share that connects this mission directly to the Artemis-era laboratories preparing to study material from the Moon and, one day, Mars itself.

Europe's footprint is equally significant. The German Aerospace Center (DLR) built the IDEFIX rover's casing, locomotion system, spectrometer, and radiometer; France's CNES supplied MIRS and partnered on the rover; and the European Space Agency will provide communications and ground-station relay through its Estrack network, complementing NASA's Deep Space Network. When a spacecraft is a billion kilometers from home and a command takes the better part of an hour to arrive, having two independent global antenna networks listening is not a luxury — it is mission insurance.

And then there is the final act, which belongs to Australia. The Australian Space Agency will lead sample recovery over the Woomera Prohibited Area, the vast desert range in South Australia where the capsule is expected to land in 2031. The South Australian government has framed the role as a milestone for the state's growing space sector. "By contributing to missions of this scale, we can strengthen our space capabilities, create opportunities for Australian researchers and industry, and help inspire the next generation of scientists and engineers," said South Australia's Space Industries Minister Chris Picton. It is a fitting symmetry: a mission that begins on a subtropical Japanese island will end in an Australian desert, with the most precious dust on Earth — or rather, off it — as the cargo in between.

## A Long History of Near-Misses

The road to Phobos is littered with wreckage — which is part of what makes this launch feel so momentous. The story began with American astronomer Asaph Hall, who discovered both moons in 1877 from the U.S. Naval Observatory in Washington. NASA's Mariner 7 gave humanity its first close-up views of Phobos in 1971, and Mariner 9 first photographed Deimos in 1972. Since then the moons have turned up in occasional orbiter views — from NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, ESA's Mars Express, and the United Arab Emirates' Hope Probe — and even NASA's rovers on the surface of Mars have played a role, observing transits of Phobos and Deimos across the face of the Sun to refine their orbits.

But getting to the surface has proven brutally difficult. The Soviet Union's Phobos 2 probe came closest but fell silent just 50 meters above the moon in 1989; its sibling Phobos 1 was lost in transit. In 2011, Russia's Phobos-Grunt failed to depart Earth orbit entirely and fell back to Earth. Japan's own Mars history is no kinder: the Nozomi mission failed to enter Mars orbit and was terminated in 2003 — making MMX the agency's second attempt at the Red Planet, more than two decades later.

What changed in between is Japan's mastery of the hardest part of this mission: bringing extraterrestrial material home. Hayabusa 1 completed its round trip to the asteroid Itokawa in 2010; Hayabusa 2 returned samples from the asteroid Ryugu in 2020. Those two missions taught JAXA how to navigate to a tiny body, touch it, and get pieces of it back to Earth — exactly the skills MMX needs, scaled up to a far more distant and unforgiving target. MMX is, in a real sense, Hayabusa's lessons applied to the Mars system, with a rover, a lander, and eleven instruments along for the ride.

There is also a quiet cosmic deadline written into the moons themselves. Phobos is spiraling slowly inward, and in some 30 to 50 million years its orbit will shrink enough that it crashes into Mars — while Deimos, feeling an outward torque, is drifting slowly away. MMX is visiting Phobos in its prime, before geology erases it. Not that the mission needs the poetry: ten grams of regolith will be scientific treasure whenever it arrives.

## The Race to Return Mars Material

MMX's 2031 return date lands it in the middle of one of the great undeclared races in space exploration: who will bring Mars material back to Earth first. NASA's own Mars Sample Return effort — built around the samples the Perseverance rover has been caching in Jezero Crater — has been mired in cost and schedule turmoil, and China has declared its own sample-return ambitions for the early 2030s. MMX, by aiming at Phobos rather than the Martian surface, neatly sidesteps the hardest part of the problem: it never has to launch off a planet's surface, never has to drill through Martian atmosphere, and never has to rendezvous in Mars orbit with an ascent vehicle. It lands on a moon with barely any gravity, scoops, and leaves.

If the schedule holds, MMX could deliver samples from the Mars system before either NASA or China completes its own Mars sample-return campaign. That would be a staggering achievement for a mission that, at $578 million, costs a fraction of what a full Mars surface sample return demands. It would also be scientifically complementary rather than competitive: Phobos samples may contain material blasted off Mars by ancient impacts — meaning MMX might return pieces of Mars itself without ever touching the planet — alongside whatever the moons' own formation history reveals about the migration of water and organics through the early solar system.

The spacecraft is now committed. The cruise phase will be quiet by the standards of what follows — months of systems checkouts, trajectory corrections, and instrument calibrations against the stars — but every milestone from here is a first: the first arrival at Phobos by a mission designed to land there, the first touchdown on a Martian moon, the first sample collected beyond the Earth-Moon system by a Japanese spacecraft, and the first return of Mars-system material to Earth.

## The Long Wait Begins

For now, the mission enters the long, silent stretch. Ten months of cruise. A year of reconnaissance. A landing in gravity so weak that a dropped wrench would take an eternity to fall. Then the slow voyage home, and a capsule falling out of the sky over Woomera in 2031 carrying ten grams of another world's moon.

It is worth pausing on what those ten grams represent. They are the product of a launch window that opens twice a decade, a spacecraft that sheds pieces of itself to do its job, a rover named after a cartoon dog bouncing across an airless rock, and the cooperation of five space agencies that decided the origin of two potato-shaped moons was worth $578 million and five years of their best people's lives. They represent Japan's return to Mars after a 23-year absence, and a bet — placed this morning on a pillar of fire over Tanegashima — that the most important geology of the next decade might come not from the surface of Mars, but from the small, dark moon circling above it.

The rocket did its part flawlessly. Now the waiting begins — and the science. Safe journeys, MMX.
