For years, the atmospheres of sub-Neptunes have been among the most frustrating targets in astronomy. These worlds — bigger than Earth, smaller than Neptune — are the most common kind of planet in the galaxy, yet their skies have mostly refused to speak. Time and again, telescopes have pointed at them and returned spectra as flat and featureless as a blank page, their atmospheres hidden behind clouds or high-altitude haze. Now, a cold and puffy world 126 light-years away has broken the silence. Astronomers using the Hubble Space Telescope have recorded the strongest atmospheric signal ever measured from a sub-Neptune, a clear absorption feature that may be the fingerprint of methane drifting through the skies of a world colder than a winter night on Earth.

The planet is TOI-6478 b, and the result, posted on 18 November 2026, is already reshaping how astronomers think about which sub-Neptunes are worth watching. Its equilibrium temperature is just 220 Kelvin — about minus 53 degrees Celsius — and it circles a small, dim red dwarf star every 34 days or so. At that temperature, the usual suspects that smother sub-Neptune spectra seem to lose their grip. Clouds sink. Hazes fail to form. And for the first time, starlight filtering through the planet's atmosphere during a transit has carried a message loud enough to read.

The detection is not yet a definitive chemical identification. Hubble's Wide Field Camera 3, the instrument behind the measurement, sees only a narrow slice of infrared light, and within that slice water vapour and methane absorb at almost exactly the same wavelength. But the models point one way. At 220 Kelvin, water should freeze out of the upper atmosphere and stay trapped far below, while methane remains aloft, free to leave its mark on the passing starlight. The team behind the study is careful about the distinction, and so is this article: what Hubble found is a real atmospheric feature, the strongest of its kind, and the best physical explanation for it is methane. Confirming that explanation will fall to the James Webb Space Telescope, whose broader wavelength coverage can separate the two candidate molecules cleanly. Until then, TOI-6478 b has done something no sub-Neptune has done before. It has given astronomers a spectrum worth arguing about.

## A signal six times the noise

The measurement at the heart of the story is a transmission spectrum: the rainbow of starlight that passes through a planet's atmosphere as the planet crosses the face of its star. When a planet transits, a tiny fraction of the star's light filters through the thin shell of gas around the planet's edge. Molecules in that gas absorb light at wavelengths specific to their chemical identity, carving dips into the spectrum. The deeper the dip, the more of that molecule is present — and the easier the atmosphere is to read.

For TOI-6478 b, the Hubble team measured an absorption feature near a wavelength of 1.4 microns, in the near-infrared. The feature stands at a signal-to-noise ratio of about six, meaning the dip is roughly six times larger than the random scatter in the data. That may sound modest, but in the world of sub-Neptune spectroscopy it is a record. No sub-Neptune observed with Hubble's Wide Field Camera 3 has ever produced a stronger spectral feature. Previous attempts on similar worlds have typically yielded marginal bumps barely above the noise, or nothing at all.

The reason TOI-6478 b speaks so much more clearly than its cousins is written into its body. The planet is about 4.5 times the radius of Earth, yet its mass is less than 9.9 Earth masses — the study places only an upper limit on the mass, because the planet is so light that pinning down its exact weight is itself a challenge. That combination makes it a low-density world, a puffball whose gravity is weak for its size. Weak gravity means a large atmospheric scale height: the atmosphere puffs outward, and the layer of gas that starlight must cross during a transit is thick. The result is spectral features about 2.5 times taller than those of K2-18 b, the sub-Neptune that has long served as the field's standard target for atmospheric work. Where K2-18 b whispers, TOI-6478 b speaks at more than twice the volume.

The study behind the result is titled "The SPACE Program IV: Atmospheric Features on the Cold Transiting Sub-Neptune TOI-6478 b", led by Anastasia Triantafillides and submitted to the Astronomical Journal on 18 November 2026. It is the fourth paper in the SPACE program series, a systematic effort to measure the atmospheres of sub-Neptunes. The paper runs to 16 pages with nine figures and four tables, and its author list spans eleven researchers, including Jason S. Pero, Angelique Kahle, Thomas Beatty, Renyu Hu, Caroline V. Morley, David J. Wilson, Laura Kreidberg, Drake Deming, Ian J. M. Crossfield and Madison G. Scott. As a preprint posted to arXiv, it has not yet completed peer review, and the authors present their conclusions with the caution that status demands. But the central measurement — a strong, well-characterised spectral feature from a cold sub-Neptune — stands on its own data.

## Why cold worlds have clearer skies

The most interesting sentence in the paper may not be about TOI-6478 b at all. It is about a pattern. Among the sub-Neptunes studied so far, the authors note, there are tentative signs that the coldest ones — those with equilibrium temperatures below 400 Kelvin — are the ones that show detectable atmospheric features. Warmer sub-Neptunes, by contrast, tend to present flat, featureless spectra.

The physics behind the pattern is straightforward, even if the details are still being worked out. On a warm sub-Neptune, the chemistry of the upper atmosphere can cook up hydrocarbon hazes, tiny particles that form high in the sky and scatter starlight before it can probe the gas below. Clouds, too, can form at altitudes that block the view. The result is a spectrum that tells astronomers almost nothing about what lies underneath, like trying to read a letter through frosted glass. On a cold world, the same processes may simply fail to get going. Hazes may form inefficiently, or not at all. Clouds may settle deeper into the atmosphere, sinking below the layer that a transit spectrum can reach. The glass clears, and the molecules beneath finally show themselves.

TOI-6478 b, at 220 Kelvin, sits well inside the cold regime where the pattern predicts clear skies. Its host star helps as well. TOI-6478 is an M5 red dwarf, a small and cool star with only about 0.23 times the mass of the Sun and 0.234 times its radius, glowing at a surface temperature of 3,230 Kelvin. The system is estimated to be around 6.5 billion years old, an old thick-disc star, and the star is fully convective, meaning its interior churns from core to surface. A small star is a gift to transit spectroscopy: the planet blocks a larger fraction of the star's disc, and the atmospheric signal grows relative to the starlight. A cold planet around a small star is, in other words, close to the best-case geometry astronomers could ask for.

None of this makes TOI-6478 b Earth-like, and the authors are explicit that it is not. A world of 4.5 Earth radii with a thick hydrogen-rich envelope and an equilibrium temperature of 220 Kelvin is a miniature Neptune, not a second Earth. Its interest lies elsewhere: in what it can teach astronomers about how sub-Neptune atmospheres work, and in whether the coldest members of this common planet family are systematically easier to study than anyone expected. If the pattern holds, the catalogue of characterisable sub-Neptunes may grow quickly, not by building bigger telescopes, but by pointing the ones we already have at colder targets.

## Water or methane? The 1.4-micron question

The single most important caveat in the study is also the most scientifically interesting part of it. The feature Hubble detected sits at 1.4 microns, a wavelength where two very different molecules — water vapour and methane — both absorb strongly. Hubble's Wide Field Camera 3 covers too narrow a band of wavelengths to tell them apart. The spectrum shows that something is absorbing at 1.4 microns, but on Hubble's evidence alone, astronomers cannot say whether that something is water or methane.

This is where atmospheric modelling takes over from raw observation. The team ran self-consistent forward models of the planet's atmosphere — simulations that build a planet's temperature structure and chemistry from first principles and predict what its spectrum should look like — and the models came down on the side of methane. The reasoning is grounded in the cold. At 220 Kelvin, water vapour should condense and become trapped at higher pressures, deep in the atmosphere where a transit spectrum cannot reach it. Methane, by contrast, stays aloft at these temperatures, free to absorb starlight at the altitudes Hubble can probe. The models the team highlights use a planet of 5 Earth masses, a metallicity of +0.5 dex, and a carbon-to-oxygen ratio of 1.0, and in those models methane is the dominant absorber at the observed wavelength.

The authors are careful to present this as a physical argument rather than a detection. Their paper states plainly that Hubble's narrow bandpass cannot distinguish between the two candidate absorbers, and that the self-consistent models provide physical motivation for methane being the dominant absorber, not proof. That honesty is part of why the result is credible. A claim of methane would be a bigger headline, but a claim of a strong atmospheric feature with methane as the favoured explanation is the claim the data actually supports.

What would settle it is wavelength coverage. The James Webb Space Telescope observes across a far broader range of infrared wavelengths than Hubble's Wide Field Camera 3, and at other wavelengths water and methane absorb in different places. Webb can look at TOI-6478 b in bands where methane has features water lacks, and vice versa, and read off the answer. The authors say exactly this: Webb's broader coverage will be able to measure the atmospheric composition in detail, thanks to the high signal-to-noise features of this target. Because the Hubble signal is so strong, TOI-6478 b now sits near the top of the list for that follow-up. A planet that was, until this month, one entry among thousands in the TESS catalogue has become one of the most promising atmospheric targets in the sky.

## Ruling out the star itself

There is a trap in transit spectroscopy, and it is the star. A planet's host star is not a uniform disc of light. It has spots — cooler, darker patches — and brighter regions, and as a planet crosses the disc, the mixture of spotted and unspotted starlight that reaches the telescope changes. That changing mixture can imprint features on a spectrum that look exactly like an atmosphere, even when the planet has no detectable atmosphere at all. For a planet orbiting an active red dwarf, a star covered in spots, this is not a hypothetical worry. It is the first thing a careful team must rule out.

The TOI-6478 b team did rule it out, and the way they did it is part of the result. Their analysis incorporated the Transit Light Source Effect, a correction that models how starspots and bright regions contaminate a transit spectrum, and accounts for that contamination before any atmospheric claim is made. The 1.4-micron feature survived the correction. That is what allows the authors to call the feature planetary rather than stellar, and it is why the detection is described as robust in the paper's abstract.

This matters beyond this one planet. As transit spectroscopy pushes toward smaller signals and fainter features, the difference between a real atmosphere and a spotted star becomes the difference between a discovery and an embarrassment. The field has learned this lesson the hard way in the past, with claimed detections later traced to stellar activity rather than planetary chemistry. A study that builds the correction into its analysis from the start, and still finds a signal six times the noise, is a study that has done the unglamorous work that makes the glamorous claim believable.

The correction also underscores something about TOI-6478 itself. The host star is old, around 6.5 billion years, and old red dwarfs tend to be quieter than young ones. A quiet star, a small star, a cold planet, a puffy atmosphere: every factor in this system leans in the direction of a clean measurement. That alignment of favourable circumstances is part luck and part selection. The SPACE program, the series this study belongs to, is designed to find exactly these cases — sub-Neptunes where the atmosphere can actually be measured, rather than those where the telescope returns only a flat line and a shrug.

## A new benchmark for sub-Neptune skies

To appreciate what TOI-6478 b changes, it helps to compare it with the worlds astronomers have been studying until now. K2-18 b has been the standard target for sub-Neptune atmospheric science, the planet against which others are measured. TOI-6478 b's spectral features are about 2.5 times taller than those of K2-18 b, which means that for the same amount of telescope time, astronomers get a far clearer view of its atmosphere. A taller feature is not just a prettier spectrum. It means the chemistry can be measured with fewer observations, the uncertainties shrink, and molecules that would be marginal detections on K2-18 b become firm ones on TOI-6478 b.

The comparison runs deeper than a single number. K2-18 b sits in the habitable zone of its star, and its atmosphere has been the subject of intense debate, including contested claims about molecules of biological interest. TOI-6478 b, at 220 Kelvin, makes no such claims and invites none. It is too cold, too gassy, and too far from anything Earth-like for habitability to enter the conversation. Its value is as a laboratory: a clean, strong signal from a well-understood kind of planet, where the physics can be tested without the noise of a habitability debate. If astronomers can nail down the atmosphere of TOI-6478 b — methane or water, the metallicity, the carbon-to-oxygen ratio — they will have a calibrated benchmark against which to judge the murkier spectra of warmer, more complicated worlds.

That benchmarking role connects TOI-6478 b to other recent work on this site. <a href="/blog/ltt-1445-a-b-cosmic-shoreline-november-2026/">LTT 1445 A b and the cosmic shoreline</a> showed the James Webb Space Telescope measuring the dayside heat of a rocky world just 6.9 light-years away, testing the boundary where small planets keep or lose their atmospheres. <a href="/blog/webb-flip-51-eridani-b-november-2026/">Webb's FLIP technique and 51 Eridani b</a> showed how new processing methods can pull a hidden giant planet out of old Webb data, improving sensitivity by up to 15 times. And <a href="/blog/webb-wasp-17b-true-shape-egg-planet/">WASP-17b, the egg-shaped exoplanet</a>, showed Webb mapping the distorted shape of a hot Jupiter's atmosphere. Each of those stories is about a different tool — eclipse photometry, image processing, atmospheric mapping — but together they describe a field learning to read planets it could barely see a decade ago. TOI-6478 b adds a new chapter to that story: sometimes the breakthrough is not a new instrument, but a cold target that finally lets an old instrument do its best work.

The haze question, meanwhile, remains an active frontier. A comparative analysis of haze in sub-Neptunes observed with Webb, dated 25 November 2026, is part of the same conversation, asking how often these worlds hide behind photochemical smog and what sets the boundary between hazy and clear. TOI-6478 b is a data point on the clear side of that boundary, and every additional cold sub-Neptune that shows a clean spectrum will sharpen the picture of where the boundary lies and why.

## Why sub-Neptunes matter

It is worth stepping back to ask why a single spectrum from a cold, uninhabitable world 126 light-years away deserves this much attention. The answer is abundance. Sub-Neptunes — planets larger than Earth and smaller than Neptune — are the most common type of planet found by transit surveys, and the Solar System contains none of them. There is no local example to study up close, no sub-Neptune to visit, no analogue among our own eight planets. Everything astronomers know about this entire class of world must come from light gathered across interstellar distances, from spectra like the one Hubble just took of TOI-6478 b.

That absence shapes the science. The Solar System offers rocky planets and gas giants, with a gap between them where sub-Neptunes would sit. Planets in that gap have sizes and densities that could correspond to many different interiors: a rocky core wrapped in a thick hydrogen envelope, a water-rich world with a steamy atmosphere, or something in between. Their atmospheres are the main window into that interior, because the gases astronomers can measure carry the signature of how the planet formed, what it accreted, and how it has evolved. But for most sub-Neptunes, that window has been shut. As the TOI-6478 b paper notes, very few sub-Neptunes have had their atmospheric composition identified, primarily because high metallicity, clouds, or hazes obscure their absorption features. A field that cannot measure the atmospheres of its most common planets is a field working half-blind.

TOI-6478 b does not solve that problem on its own. One clear spectrum does not open every window. But it does something nearly as valuable: it shows that the window can open, and it points to where to look next. If the coldest sub-Neptunes systematically show clearer skies, then the observing strategy for the coming years writes itself. Point Hubble and Webb at the cold ones, collect the strong signals, and build the sample of measured atmospheres from the cold end upward. Each new measurement then tests whether the pattern is real or a coincidence of a small sample, and each test refines the models of haze formation, cloud settling and atmospheric chemistry that the whole field depends on.

There is also a humbler lesson in the result. The Hubble Space Telescope is 36 years old, and its Wide Field Camera 3 was installed in 2009. The idea that a telescope of that vintage could still set a record in a fast-moving field, on a planet discovered by a newer survey, is a reminder that in astronomy, the instrument is only half the story. The other half is choosing the right target, and the right target can be one that has been sitting in a catalogue for years, waiting for someone to notice that its temperature, its size and its star add up to an opportunity. The SPACE program's systematic approach — working through sub-Neptunes methodically rather than chasing the brightest headline — is what found it.

## What happens next

The immediate future of TOI-6478 b belongs to the James Webb Space Telescope. Webb's broader wavelength coverage is the tool the authors themselves identify as the one that can settle the water-versus-methane question, and the strength of the Hubble signal makes the planet a high-priority follow-up target. Webb observations would not only separate the two candidate molecules. They could measure the atmosphere's metallicity, constrain its carbon-to-oxygen ratio, and test the model predictions — a planet of about 5 Earth masses, a metallicity of +0.5 dex, a carbon-to-oxygen ratio of 1.0 — against real data across a wide span of wavelengths. Those numbers, in turn, would feed back into models of how the planet formed and whether its atmosphere is primordial hydrogen, enriched by ices, or something else entirely.

Before that happens, the study must complete peer review. As a preprint, its conclusions are provisional in the formal sense, and independent scrutiny may refine the numbers or qualify the claims. That is the normal course of science, and the paper's own caution — its plain statement that Hubble alone cannot distinguish the major absorbers — suggests its authors expect exactly that scrutiny and welcome it. The feature itself, six times the noise and corrected for stellar activity, is unlikely to vanish under review. The interpretation, methane favoured over water, is where the debate will concentrate, and it is a debate Webb is well equipped to resolve.

Longer term, TOI-6478 b may be remembered less for its own chemistry than for the observing strategy it validated. If cold sub-Neptunes are systematically the clearest, then the next few years of Hubble and Webb time should tilt toward them, and the catalogue of sub-Neptunes with measured atmospheres — today a short list — should grow. Each new entry will test the pattern, and the pattern, if it holds, will become a tool: a way to predict which planets are worth the telescope time before a single photon is collected. That is how a field moves from anecdotes to statistics, one cold, clear sky at a time.

TOI-6478 b is not a second Earth, and nobody involved in the study pretends otherwise. It is a cold, puffy world around a dim red dwarf, 126 light-years from the Sun, with an atmosphere that finally let itself be seen. For a class of planets that has spent years hiding behind haze, that is enough. The sky, it turns out, was never empty. Astronomers were just looking at the wrong temperature.
