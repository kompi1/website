Late on Wednesday morning, a slender white rocket stood on the deck of a converted ship anchored in the Yellow Sea, and then — at 11:39 a.m. Beijing time — it was gone, climbing on a column of fire into a pale October sky. The vehicle was a Ceres-1S, the sea-launched variant of the commercial Ceres-1 rocket built by Beijing-based Galactic Energy. Its single passenger was Xiguang-4 01, a sharp-eyed Earth observation satellite bound for a 541-kilometer orbit. And its mission carried a poetic name: *Hai Dui Yangcheng Kuo* — "The Sea Stretches Vastly Before Guangzhou."

The flight was the eighth launch of a Ceres-1S from the sea, and it was flawless. Minutes after liftoff, the satellite separated and entered its planned low Earth orbit, where it will begin work as the pathfinder of something its backers describe as a world first: the Wuyang Constellation, or the Five Rams, a commercial remote sensing network designed to fly in low-inclination orbits that sweep repeatedly over the low-latitude belt of the planet — the southern provinces of China, Southeast Asia, and the maritime routes of the Belt and Road Initiative. If the full plan holds, up to twenty-eight satellites will join it by the end of 2028, revisiting key locations as often as every thirty minutes.

It was, in other words, not just another small launch. It was the opening move of a new kind of constellation — one aimed squarely at the crowded, disaster-prone, fast-growing latitudes where more than a billion people live, and where the global remote sensing market has historically been underserved.

## Liftoff From the Waves

The launch came from waters near Rizhao, a port city in east China's Shandong Province, where the rocket lifted off from the deck of the *Dongfang Hangtiangang* launch ship. The mission, designated Ceres-1S Y8, had been tracked for days by launch watchers, who watched its window settle on the morning of October 21. Liftoff came at 03:39 UTC — 11:39 a.m. local time — and the solid-fueled rocket performed exactly as advertised.

For Galactic Energy, the flight continued a run of reliability that has become one of the quiet success stories of China's commercial space sector. Wednesday's launch was the seventh sea-launch mission of the Ceres-1S, and the vehicle has now maintained a perfect success record across every one of its sea flights since its maiden voyage on September 5, 2023. The Ceres-1 family, the company notes, is China's first commercial rocket model capable of launching from both land and sea platforms — a flexibility that turns any stretch of coastal water into a potential spaceport.

That flexibility was on full display at Rizhao. The city has been methodically building itself into a hub for offshore launch operations, and this mission marked the second rocket launch conducted off its coast in 2026, and the eighth sea-launch support mission handled in the area by the Shandong Offshore Commercial Aerospace Launch Co. Across seven successful launches using Ceres-1 and Smart Dragon-3 rockets, offshore missions off Rizhao have now placed fifty satellites into orbit — a tally that keeps climbing.

## The Satellite With Three Names

Xiguang-4 01 has accumulated names the way ambitious missions do. Formally it is Xiguang-4 01 — "Western Light-4 01" — built around a remote sensing payload developed with support from the Xi'an Zhongke Xiguang Technology Group. As the first satellite of the new network, it also carries the designation Xiandao-A, or Pioneer-A, the lead pathfinder of the constellation to come. And in its operational life it will be known as Guangda-1, the first "Guangda" satellite serving the city whose ambitions it carries.

The spacecraft is operated by Guangdong Tianyuan Technology Co., with support from Guangzhou University and the municipal government of Guangzhou — a lineup that makes the mission's civic character unmistakable. This is a satellite built for a city: its job, once commissioned, is to collect imagery of Guangzhou and the surrounding Pearl River Delta, feeding data into ecological monitoring, disaster response and planning, agricultural support, and urban governance.

The instrument suite is designed for what its developers call a "full-spectrum quantitative" observation system. The centerpiece is an optical camera resolving details down to half a meter — sharp enough to distinguish individual buildings, vehicles, and infrastructure. Alongside it flies a two-meter-resolution multispectral camera, which captures the same scenes in multiple wavelength bands to reveal crop health, water quality, and vegetation stress invisible to the ordinary eye. A third instrument, an infrared imager with a thirty-meter-wide observation swath, adds a thermal dimension: hot spots from industrial activity, wildfire fronts, and the heat signatures of urban districts.

Together, the three instruments give the satellite a layered view of the same ground — shape, color, chemistry, and temperature — that later satellites in the constellation are meant to inherit and expand.

Once operational, Xiguang-4 01's services are expected to extend well beyond Guangzhou. Its backers describe a service footprint covering the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area, South China, and the low-latitude regions along the Maritime Silk Road. The applications list reads like a catalog of twenty-first-century governance challenges: natural resource monitoring, agricultural production, marine observation, ecological protection, flood and typhoon emergency response, disaster mitigation, and urban management.

## Why the Rams?

The constellation's name reaches back more than two thousand years. Guangzhou is known as the City of Five Rams — *Wuyang Cheng* — after an old legend in which five immortals descended from the heavens riding rams, each animal bearing sheaves of rice, bringing abundance to the Pearl River Delta. The tale is commemorated by the famous Five Rams statue in Yuexiu Park, one of the city's most beloved landmarks.

Naming a satellite constellation after the legend is a deliberate piece of civic branding: the Wuyang Constellation (五羊星座) is, in effect, Guangzhou's bid to stamp its identity on the space age. And there is a neat symmetry in it. The rams of the legend brought the gift of grain to a river delta; the satellites of the constellation are meant to bring the gift of information — harvest forecasts, flood warnings, urban plans — to the same delta, now one of the most densely populated and economically vital regions on Earth.

Two more pathfinder satellites, carrying the same "Xiandao" pioneer designation, are expected to follow in the near future, before the main build-out of the constellation begins.

## A Constellation Built for the Low Latitudes

The Wuyang network's defining technical choice is its orbit. Most commercial Earth observation constellations fly in Sun-synchronous polar orbits, which sweep over the entire planet but concentrate their coverage at high latitudes and pass over any given equatorial site relatively infrequently. The Wuyang satellites will instead fly in low-inclination, low-latitude orbits — paths that hug the tropics and subtropics and revisit the same low-latitude locations far more often.

That geometry is the whole point of the "world's first low-inclination remote sensing constellation" claim reported around the launch. Southern China, Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean rim, and the island nations of the western Pacific sit in a band of the planet that global observation networks have historically covered thinly. It is also a band where typhoons form, where monsoon floods displace millions, where shipping lanes crowd, and where some of the fastest-growing cities in the world are rising. For disaster response, the difference between a satellite that passes overhead twice a day and one that passes every thirty minutes is the difference between watching a flood happen and managing it.

The constellation's design target is exactly that: no more than twenty-eight satellites in low-latitude orbits, arranged so that key locations come under observation roughly every half hour. The build-out is planned to run through the end of 2028, with the satellites deployed in waves. If the cadence holds, the network will offer something no commercial operator currently provides at this scale: persistent, high-revisit optical and infrared watch over the tropics.

The commercial logic is equally deliberate. Rather than competing head-on with the established polar-orbit imaging giants, the Wuyang network is positioning itself as a regional specialist — a constellation tuned to the latitudes where its anchor customers live. Guangzhou's government, the Greater Bay Area's planners, and agricultural and maritime users across Southeast Asia form a natural first market. And the Maritime Silk Road framing ties the project to the broader Belt and Road Initiative, whose participating nations across Southeast Asia, South Asia, and the Indian Ocean are precisely the low-latitude customers a low-inclination constellation serves best.

## Land Command, Sea Launch

One of the more quietly significant aspects of the October 21 flight had nothing to do with the rocket or the satellite — it was where the countdown was run from. The mission was managed under a new "land-command, sea-launch" framework: the rocket stood on its ship offshore, but the launch was commanded from a brand-new onshore facility, the Rizhao Command and Control Center, inaugurated by the Shandong Offshore Commercial Aerospace Launch Co. ahead of the mission.

The center is equipped with dedicated telemetry, satellite communications, and wideband network systems that stream real-time data between the offshore platform and the ground team — managing countdown protocols, issuing ignition commands, and tracking the rocket's trajectory through ascent. It is the first time an offshore mission off Rizhao has been run this way, with the operational brain on land and the launch pad at sea.

The logic is straightforward. Sea launches are operationally demanding: the platform must be towed into position, the rocket integrated and fueled in a marine environment, and the whole operation coordinated across ship and shore. A permanent onshore command center turns what used to be an expedition into a routine — a standing capability that can support a growing launch cadence rather than a one-off campaign. For a province positioning itself as China's commercial sea-launch hub, the control center is infrastructure in the truest sense: the equivalent of building a proper airport terminal after years of operating from a field.

The framework also hints at how seriously China is taking the operational side of its commercial launch boom. Rockets get the headlines, but launch cadence is won or lost on ground systems — range scheduling, telemetry networks, weather monitoring, and command infrastructure. Rizhao is building all of it.

## The Rocket: Ceres-1S

The vehicle that carried Xiguang-4 01 deserves a closer look, because the Ceres-1S embodies a particular philosophy of commercial launch. It is the sea-launched variant of Galactic Energy's Ceres-1, a four-stage small-lift rocket designed to deliver up to 400 kilograms to low Earth orbit. The first three stages burn solid propellant — simple, storable, and well suited to the rough handling of a sea deployment — while a fourth stage using hydrazine provides the precise final push that circularizes the orbit.

Solid rockets are the workhorses of responsive launch: they can sit ready for long periods, need minimal ground infrastructure, and ignite reliably. That makes them a natural fit for sea launch, where the entire vehicle must be transported by ship, erected on a barge, and launched with a fraction of the support equipment a land range provides. The trade-off is efficiency — solids deliver less punch per kilogram than liquid engines — but for a 400-kilogram-class payload headed to low orbit, the math works.

The numbers tell the story of a maturing system. Wednesday's flight was the second Ceres-1 launch of 2026, and the seventh sea launch of the Ceres-1S variant — every one of them successful, stretching back to the maiden flight in September 2023. In an industry where new rockets often stumble early, a perfect record across seven sea launches is a genuine credential, and it is the foundation on which Galactic Energy is building its manifest.

## Why Go to Sea at All?

Sea launch looks exotic, but its advantages are practical. China's inland launch centers — Jiuquan, Taiyuan, Xichang — are superb facilities, but they are far from the coast, and every rocket stage dropped from them falls on land, constraining launch corridors and requiring evacuation zones. Coastal launches, and sea launches especially, let spent stages fall harmlessly into the ocean and open up launch azimuths that land ranges cannot offer. For low-inclination orbits like the ones the Wuyang constellation needs, launching from the sea off China's east coast is close to ideal: the rocket can fly the right trajectory without overflying populated land.

There is also a capacity argument. China's launch manifest — state and commercial combined — has been growing at a pace that strains its established ranges. Sea launch adds pads without pouring concrete: a ship, a command center, and a stretch of open water. As the country's commercial constellations multiply — broadband networks, remote sensing fleets, science missions — that extra capacity matters.

And there is a competitive signal in the cadence. The Ceres-1S's seven-for-seven record, the fifty satellites orbited from Rizhao, the standing command infrastructure — these are the markers of a commercial launch sector moving from demonstration to routine. Galactic Energy is not the only player; the Smart Dragon-3, a larger solid rocket, has also flown from the same waters. But the pattern is clear: China's east coast is becoming a working spaceport, one ship at a time.

## What Xiguang-4 01 Will Watch

To understand what the Wuyang constellation is for, consider a typhoon season in the South China Sea. A storm makes landfall in Guangdong; rivers rise; farmland floods. In the hours that follow, emergency managers need to know which roads are passable, which levees are holding, where the water is deepest — and they need to know it repeatedly, as conditions change. A half-meter optical camera revisiting every thirty minutes turns a satellite constellation into something close to a live feed: damage mapped in near-real time, rescue routes planned on fresh imagery, the recovery tracked block by block.

Agriculture offers a quieter but equally consequential use case. The multispectral camera's view of crop vigor — the spectral signatures of healthy versus stressed vegetation — lets analysts estimate yields, spot pest outbreaks, and guide irrigation across the vast rice and fruit-growing regions of the south. The infrared imager adds the thermal layer: urban heat islands mapped across the Greater Bay Area's megacities, industrial hot spots monitored, wildfire fronts tracked at night when optical cameras go blind.

Marine observation rounds out the portfolio. The Pearl River Delta is one of the busiest maritime regions in the world, and regular revisits support ship tracking, coastal erosion monitoring, aquaculture management, and the policing of illegal fishing — applications where persistence matters more than exquisite resolution.

None of this is science fiction; it is the standard playbook of modern remote sensing. What is new is the combination: commercial service, low-inclination persistence, and a customer base concentrated in the latitudes the constellation is built to serve.

## What Comes Next

Xiguang-4 01 is now in commissioning — the weeks-long process of checking out its instruments, calibrating its cameras, and validating its data before it enters full service. Behind it, the pipeline is already moving: two more Xiandao pathfinder satellites are expected to follow, testing the constellation's design before the main deployment waves begin.

The full Wuyang network — up to twenty-eight satellites by the end of 2028 — will require a sustained launch cadence, and the sea-launch infrastructure at Rizhao looks built for exactly that. Each Ceres-1S flight can carry a single satellite of this class; larger rideshare missions on vehicles like the Smart Dragon-3 could loft several at once. Either way, the constellation's construction will be one of the steadier drumbeats of China's commercial launch manifest over the next two years.

It will not be alone. The global remote sensing market is crowded and getting more so, with constellations multiplying on every continent. The Wuyang network's bet is that specialization wins: that a constellation designed from the start for the low latitudes, backed by a city government that is also its anchor customer, and launched from ships off its own coast, can carve out a durable niche.

On Wednesday morning, that bet began with a pillar of fire over the Yellow Sea — and with five rams, two millennia old, finally headed for orbit.
