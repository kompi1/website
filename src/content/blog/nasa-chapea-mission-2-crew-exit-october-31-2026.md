On Saturday, October 31, 2026, a door opened at NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston, and four people walked out into a world they had not touched for more than a year. They had not been to Mars. They had not left Earth at all. Yet the moment carried the weight of a homecoming, because for 378 days Ross Elder, Ellen Ellis, Matthew Montgomery and James Spicer had lived as if they had: sealed inside a 3D-printed habitat called Mars Dune Alpha, working, exercising, growing food and waiting out delayed messages from the outside world, all in the name of learning how a real crew might survive a stay on the Red Planet.

The crew of CHAPEA Mission 2 — CHAPEA stands for Crew Health and Performance Exploration Analog — entered the habitat on October 19, 2025. They emerged on October 31, 2026, closing the second of NASA's planned year-long Mars simulations. Live coverage of the exit began at 3 p.m. Eastern time on NASA Johnson's YouTube channel and social media accounts, with remarks scheduled from Johnson Center Director Vanessa Wyche, NASA astronaut Jack Hathaway and the four crew members themselves. Media were invited to watch from a screen outside the hangar that holds the CHAPEA facility, and to capture the moment the crew stepped outside for the first time in more than a year.

That image — four volunteers blinking in Texas daylight after a simulated year on Mars — is easy to romanticize, and easy to misunderstand. CHAPEA is not a dress rehearsal for a Mars landing, and NASA has been careful to frame it as something narrower and, in its own way, harder: a human research study. The habitat cannot reproduce Martian gravity, which is about 38 percent of Earth's, or the radiation environment of deep space. What it can reproduce, with unusual fidelity, is the daily grind of a long surface stay: confinement in a small space with the same three colleagues, a finite stock of food and supplies, equipment that breaks and must be fixed with what is on hand, and conversations with mission control that arrive minutes late, if they arrive at all. Over 378 days, those pressures stop being abstractions. They become the texture of life.

## A year that began on October 19, 2025

The second CHAPEA mission was designed to mirror the first. Both lasted 378 days on NASA's count, which includes the first and last days. Both used the same habitat. Both asked four people to live and work inside it while simulating Mars mission operations: conducting simulated spacewalks, known as Marswalks, performing habitat maintenance, exercising, growing plants and managing the resource limitations and communication delays that future deep-space crews will likely experience. The symmetry is deliberate. If two crews, in the same building, under the same rules, show similar patterns of adaptation or strain, researchers gain more confidence that what they are seeing is about the mission, not just about four particular personalities.

The Mission 2 crew was announced by NASA on September 5, 2025, about six weeks before ingress. Commander Ross Elder, a United States Air Force major and experimental test pilot from Williamstown, West Virginia, brought more than 1,800 flying hours in over 35 military aircraft to the commander's seat. Medical officer Ellen Ellis, a United States Space Force colonel from North Kingstown, Rhode Island, had previously worked as an intercontinental ballistic missile operations officer and as a GPS satellite engineer — a background that spans both the human and the hardware sides of a long mission. Science officer Matthew Montgomery, a hardware design consultant from Los Angeles, specialized in LED lighting, robotics and controlled-environment agriculture, skills that map directly onto a habitat that must grow some of its own food and keep its own systems running. Flight engineer James Spicer, an aerospace and defense technical director who had built satellite communications networks and held commercial and glider pilot licenses, rounded out a crew chosen as much for practical competence as for scientific curiosity. Marine Corps pilot Emily Phillips and airline pilot Laura Marie were named as alternates.

None of the four were NASA astronauts, and that distinction matters. They were research volunteers, selected and screened for a study, not members of an astronaut corps training for a flight assignment. Their job was not to prove that they personally could go to Mars. Their job was to generate data — day after day, for more than a year — about how people in general hold up under Mars-like surface conditions. That is why the mission's choreography felt both ordinary and strange: a schedule of science tasks, maintenance chores, exercise sessions and crop-tending, punctuated by simulated walks on red sand inside the building and by the long silences between a question sent to mission control and an answer that, in a real Mars mission, could take anywhere from a few minutes to more than twenty minutes each way, depending on the distance between the two planets.

Mars Dune Alpha itself is part of the experiment. The roughly 1,700-square-foot habitat was 3D-printed by the company ICON, and its layout reflects the compromises of a real surface home: private crew quarters small enough to feel private but not spacious, shared work and living areas where four people must negotiate every routine, a dedicated area for growing crops, and an airlock through which the crew passed for simulated excursions. Leaving the living area meant passing through that airlock, a ritual that reinforced the boundary between inside and outside even though the outside, in this case, was a hangar floor in Houston rather than the Martian regolith. The point was never to fool the crew into believing they were on Mars. It was to make the constraints real enough that their behavior under them would be worth studying.

## What a simulated year actually measures

When NASA talks about CHAPEA, it talks about health and performance, and both words deserve unpacking. Health, in this context, is not only the absence of illness. It includes sleep quality over months of artificial lighting and fixed schedules, nutrition on a constrained and partly self-grown diet, physical fitness in a small exercise space, and the slow accumulation of stress in a body that never gets a true day off, because there is nowhere to go. Performance is the other half of the ledger: can a crew still make good decisions together in month twelve, after a year of the same faces, the same walls and the same routines? Can they still run a complex task, troubleshoot a failure and communicate clearly with a control room whose replies are always late?

Those questions sound abstract until they are translated into the daily life of Mars Dune Alpha. A crop experiment that fails is not just a lost data point; it is food the crew counted on. A piece of equipment that breaks is not just a maintenance ticket; it is a problem the crew must solve without a resupply flight, because on Mars there would be no resupply flight, at least not quickly. A disagreement that would be trivial in a normal workplace — who cleans what, who gets quiet time when — becomes a mission risk when the same four people must live together for another six months and still trust each other with safety-critical work. CHAPEA's value lies in watching those dynamics play out at full length, rather than inferring them from a two-week simulation or a survey.

That length is also the study's greatest limitation, and honest coverage of the exit should say so plainly. Four people is a small sample. Two missions, eight people in total, is still a small sample. Researchers will be cautious about generalizing from one crew's experience to every future Mars crew, and they will need to separate what was caused by isolation and confinement from what was caused by individual temperament, team chemistry or the particular quirks of a ground-based habitat. Coverage of the exit weekend noted that no results from Mission 2 had been published yet, and that data analysis was only beginning. That is normal for a study of this kind — the exit is the end of data collection, not the end of the science — but it means the public should treat the ceremony as a milestone, not a verdict. The findings, when they come, will arrive as papers and technical reports, not as applause on a livestream.

There is a second limitation that no ground simulation can fix. The crew lived under Earth's gravity for the entire mission, not Mars gravity, and they were shielded by Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field from the radiation that a real Mars crew would face during transit and on the surface. CHAPEA therefore says little, directly, about bone loss in partial gravity or about radiation risk. What it can say, with real authority, is how isolation, confinement, delayed communication and resource scarcity interact over a year — and those are precisely the factors that are hardest to test any other way. A spacecraft can be tested in a vacuum chamber. A crew's morale cannot.

## The first crew set the template

Mission 2 did not happen in isolation from Mission 1, and the comparison between the two is part of why NASA ran the same protocol twice. The first CHAPEA crew — Kelly Haston, Ross Brockwell, Nathan Jones and Anca Selariu — entered Mars Dune Alpha on June 25, 2023, and emerged on July 6, 2024, also after 378 days. Their mission established the baseline: a year of simulated Mars operations in the same building, under the same broad set of stressors, with the same kind of daily schedule. Mission 2 then repeated the experiment with a different crew, allowing researchers to ask whether the patterns observed the first time would recur.

Added together, the two missions represent 756 days of habitat occupation and 3,024 crew-days of observation — four people, times 378 days, times two missions. That is a substantial dataset for human spaceflight research, even if it comes from only eight individuals. It covers two full annual cycles of seasons, holidays and personal milestones missed, two winters of short days outside the habitat walls, and two sets of crew dynamics that began as strangers learning to live together and ended as a team that had shared a year of its life. When NASA eventually publishes comparative analyses, the contrasts may prove as informative as the similarities: which problems every crew encountered, and which problems belonged to one crew alone.

The program itself was announced in 2021 with three one-year missions planned in total. That structure matters for expectations. Mission 2's exit does not close the CHAPEA program; it completes its second chapter. A third mission, if it proceeds on the same model, would extend the dataset further and test whether findings from the first two crews hold for a third. For now, though, NASA's next announced step changes the format rather than simply repeating it, and that change says something about where the agency thinks the most urgent questions now lie.

## What comes next: a transit, then a surface stay

In early July 2026, NASA unveiled the Moon and Mars Exploration Analog, or MMEA, and began recruiting volunteers for it. The new simulation is scheduled to begin no earlier than August 2027, and its design is deliberately more ambitious than CHAPEA's. Instead of starting directly in a surface habitat, the MMEA crew will first spend a transit phase in an adapted version of HERA, the Human Exploration Research Analog, simulating the journey to the Moon or Mars, and then move into Mars Dune Alpha for the surface phase. A rover able to house two people, capable of carrying crew members on multi-day excursions, is part of the concept — a recognition that a real surface mission would not confine its crew to a single building for a year, but would send them out, for days at a time, in a pressurized vehicle that is itself a small habitat.

That evolution is worth pausing on, because it reveals how analog research progresses. CHAPEA isolated one phase of a Mars mission — the surface stay — and studied it in depth. MMEA joins two phases together — the transit and the surface stay — into a single continuous year, testing the handover between them: the psychological shift from a cramped spacecraft to a surface base, the operational shift from transit routines to surface routines, and the cumulative fatigue of a mission that never offers a clean break. It also reflects NASA's nearer-term priorities. The agency has repeatedly said that CHAPEA's findings are meant first for the Moon, through the Artemis program and its plans for sustained lunar surface operations, and later for Mars, since a long stay on the lunar surface raises many of the same problems of housing, feeding and sustaining a crew far from help. The MMEA name itself — Moon and Mars — makes that dual purpose explicit.

For readers following the robotic side of Mars exploration, the human research story runs in parallel with a very different kind of progress. While the CHAPEA crew tended crops in Houston, <a href="/blog/perseverance-mars-driving-record-45km/">Perseverance Just Broke the All-Time Off-World Driving Record: 45 Kilometers on Mars</a>, passing a mark set by Opportunity over nearly fifteen years. Engineers are also working on the machines that might one day scout ahead of, or alongside, human crews, including <a href="/blog/nasa-roamx-0201-mars-helicopter-blade-design/">NASA's Radical New Mars Helicopter Blades Could Fly 27 Times Farther Than Ingenuity</a>, a rotor design that could extend a Mars helicopter's single-flight range dramatically. And the propulsion frontier is moving too, with <a href="/blog/nasa-sr-1-freedom-nuclear-mars-2028/">NASA SR-1 Freedom Nuclear Mars 2028: The Fission Spacecraft Still on Track for Launch</a>, a program aiming to test nuclear electric propulsion on the way to the Red Planet. CHAPEA asks a quieter question than any of those missions: once the machines can get us there, can the people stay well enough, and work well enough together, to make the stay worthwhile?

## The two weeks after the door opens

The exit ceremony is not the end of the crew's obligations. Reporting ahead of the egress said the crew would face about two weeks of post-mission evaluations and debriefs with NASA scientists — medical checks, psychological assessments, detailed interviews about what worked and what did not, and the unglamorous work of handing over a year's worth of logs, samples and observations to the researchers who will spend months, or longer, making sense of them. For the crew, that period is a strange coda: free to walk outside, to eat fresh food, to call family without a simulated delay, yet still tethered to the mission by appointments and questionnaires. For researchers, it is when some of the most valuable data is collected, because first impressions after a year of confinement — what the crew remembers most vividly, what they wish had been different, what they would tell the next crew — are freshest in the days immediately after egress.

It is also worth remembering what the crew gave up. A year inside Mars Dune Alpha meant a year of birthdays, anniversaries and ordinary days missed, a year of news arriving filtered and late, a year in which the sky was never actually visible except as an idea. The volunteers knew that going in, and they volunteered anyway, which is part of why the exit ceremony drew an audience. There is something genuinely moving about four people who agreed to be studied for a year so that strangers, decades from now, might live more safely on another world. Sentiment should not, however, be mistaken for evidence. The crew's endurance proves that four screened, motivated, well-supported volunteers can complete 378 days in a ground habitat. It does not prove that any crew can, or that a real Mars crew — facing real radiation, real partial gravity, real danger and a real inability to come home early — would fare the same. The difference between a simulation and the thing itself is not a footnote. It is the reason the research exists.

That distinction also helps explain why NASA stages these exits so publicly. Analog missions are, by nature, invisible work: months of routine inside a building that looks unremarkable from the outside. An egress event makes the invisible visible, if only for an afternoon, and gives the public a human face for a research program whose outputs will otherwise appear as charts and conference papers. The livestream, the remarks from Wyche and Hathaway, the crew's own words — all of it serves a dual purpose, celebrating a completed mission and reminding taxpayers and policymakers that human spaceflight research is, at its core, research about humans, with all the patience and uncertainty that implies.

## Why a Houston habitat matters for the Moon first

It may seem counterintuitive that a Mars simulation should matter most, in the near term, for the Moon. Yet the logic is straightforward. Artemis aims to establish a sustained human presence on the lunar surface, with crews living and working there for weeks and eventually months, far from the immediate support that low Earth orbit provides. Many of the stressors CHAPEA studies — isolation from family and normal life, communication delays (shorter for the Moon than for Mars, but still real), limited supplies, equipment maintenance with no quick resupply, and the need for a small team to function as both colleagues and housemates — apply directly to a lunar base, even if the lunar version is shorter and closer to home. Lessons about habitat layout, schedule design, food systems, conflict resolution and the timing of support from mission control can be transferred from a Mars-length simulation to a lunar mission, and then tested again at lunar scale.

The reverse is also true. A lunar base, once established, becomes a proving ground for Mars: a place to test habitats, rovers, power systems and operational procedures in a real partial-gravity, high-radiation environment, with an abort option that Mars does not offer. In that larger arc, CHAPEA is an early, ground-based step — not the destination, but a necessary rehearsal of the human side of the problem while the hardware side catches up. The rovers, helicopters and propulsion systems described above will determine whether humans can reach Mars and move around once there. CHAPEA, and MMEA after it, will help determine whether humans can live there, together, for a year, and still be healthy and effective at the end of it.

There is also a quieter, institutional lesson in Mission 2's completion. Large research programs survive on continuity: the same habitat, the same protocol, the same commitment to a second and third run even when the first run's results are still being analyzed. In a field where schedules slip and priorities shift, the fact that CHAPEA Mission 2 entered on schedule in October 2025 and exited on schedule in October 2026, with its full 378 days intact, is itself a small achievement of program management. It suggests that NASA's Human Research Program can sustain the long, unglamorous timelines that human factors research requires — timelines measured in years, not news cycles.

## What to watch now

The most important story after the exit is the one that will unfold slowly, and largely out of public view. Watch for NASA's first published analyses comparing Mission 1 and Mission 2: do the same stressors produce the same effects, or does each crew write its own story? Watch for any changes to habitat design, schedule structure or support protocols that NASA attributes to CHAPEA data, because those changes are the real-world payoff of a year in a Houston building. Watch, too, for the MMEA crew selection process, which will reveal how NASA is refining its volunteer criteria as the simulations grow more complex and the transit phase is added. And watch for how Artemis surface planning incorporates human factors findings — not as a separate chapter, but as a constraint on every other decision, from how large a lunar habitat's private quarters should be to how often a crew should expect a real-time conversation with Earth.

None of that will arrive with the drama of an airlock opening. It will arrive as papers, presentations and, eventually, design requirements. That is the nature of the work. CHAPEA's contribution is cumulative: each crew adds a layer of evidence to a foundation that future mission planners will stand on, whether they remember the names Elder, Ellis, Montgomery and Spicer or not. The crew members themselves will return to ordinary lives — flying aircraft, engineering systems, designing hardware — carrying a year that few people will ever experience, even in simulation. Their names will fade from headlines within days. The data they generated will not.

For now, the honest summary of October 31, 2026, is this: four volunteers completed a difficult, carefully designed year inside a simulated Mars habitat in Houston, and then they walked outside. They did not go to Mars, and they did not prove that Mars is safe. They gave researchers a second full year of evidence about what a Mars-length surface stay does to people, and what people do to cope with it. In a program whose goal is to send humans to another planet and bring them home healthy, that evidence is not a sideshow. It is the mission.

The next crew to attempt something like this will not start from scratch. They will start from two years of notes, measurements and hard-won routines left behind by eight people who lived, for 378 days at a time, in a 3D-printed house in Texas, pretending — rigorously, patiently, and for the benefit of strangers — that they were already on Mars. When the door of Mars Dune Alpha opens for the third time, or when the MMEA crew begins its transit phase in 2027, the question will be the same one CHAPEA has asked from the start, only sharper: not whether humans can endure a year away from Earth, but whether we have learned enough from those who already did to make the next year safer, saner and more productive than the last. That is the work the October 31 exit quietly advanced, one airlock, one year and four volunteers at a time.
