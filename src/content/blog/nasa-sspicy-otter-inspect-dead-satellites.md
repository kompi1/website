The debris inspector is already in orbit. On October 1, 2026, a SpaceX Falcon 9 lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California carrying 130 payloads on the Transporter-18 dedicated rideshare mission — and among them was a small spacecraft with an unusual job: to go and look closely at dead satellites.

The spacecraft is the first full-scale Otter satellite servicing vehicle built by Starfish Space, a startup based in Tukwila, Washington. It will fly NASA's Small Spacecraft Propulsion and Inspection Capability mission — SSPICY for short — the first commercial space debris inspection the space agency has ever funded. Beginning in early 2027, the vehicle will autonomously approach up to four inoperable American-origin satellites and rocket bodies in low Earth orbit, studying how they tumble, which way they face, and what condition their surfaces are in — information that could one day make it possible to repair them, or to drag them safely out of the sky.

"This is a defining moment for Starfish," the company's leadership signaled after launch, and the celebration spilled off the planet and onto Times Square: the day after liftoff, a massive digital billboard in New York flashed, "Nasdaq congratulates Starfish Space on its first Otter satellite launch," beside a rendering of the vehicle.

For NASA, though, the spectacle is secondary. What matters is data — close-range, engineering-grade data about the largest and most dangerous category of space debris: the intact, dead hulks of satellites that no one can contact and no one can steer, drifting through some of the busiest lanes in orbit. Nobody has ever systematically inspected them before. SSPICY is designed to change that.

## What SSPICY actually is

SSPICY — the Small Spacecraft Propulsion and Inspection Capability — is a technology demonstration, not an operational cleanup mission. Its purpose is narrower and, in some ways, more important: to prove that a single small spacecraft can find a dead object, approach it safely without human help, inspect it from close range, and then do it again with another object somewhere else in orbit. NASA awarded Starfish Space the mission in 2024 under a three-year Small Business Innovation Research Phase III contract worth $15 million.

The plan, as described by NASA, runs on a deliberate timeline. The Otter spacecraft, designated Otter 24C, launched on October 1 and will spend the rest of 2026 being commissioned in orbit — powering up, checking out its sensors and thrusters, and validating the software that will eventually fly it within hundreds of yards of its targets. The inspections themselves begin in early 2027, and NASA describes the overall mission as lasting approximately two years.

During each inspection, Otter will measure the target's spin rate, its orientation, the condition of its surfaces, and its geometry and orbital characteristics. That sounds like a modest observing campaign, but for the engineers who design servicing missions, it answers questions that currently have no answers at all. NASA's 2024 award announcement described plans to examine target surfaces and to measure how objects rotate, including the direction of their spin axes. As NASA technology lead Bo Naasz put it when the award was announced, the mission would "gather data on what may leave spacecraft stranded."

That phrasing deserves unpacking. When a satellite dies — a battery fails, a processor glitches, a propellant line freezes — ground controllers lose their window into what happened. Telemetry stops. The spacecraft becomes a silent, tumbling mystery. Before anyone can service, refuel, repair, or deorbit such an object, someone has to answer basic questions: Is it spinning fast or slow? Which axis is it tumbling around? Are its solar panels intact, crumpled, or missing? Is the structure sound enough to grab? Today, those questions can only be answered from the ground with radar and telescopes, which cannot resolve fine detail. SSPICY will answer them from hundreds of yards away, with cameras and sensors, under autonomous control.

Critically, Otter will not touch or dock with any of its targets. The mission is inspection only. That restraint is deliberate: docking with a non-cooperative, tumbling object is one of the hardest problems in spaceflight, and SSPICY's job is to prove the sensing, navigation, and propulsion steps that must come first. The capabilities demonstrated here, NASA says, could later support missions to check the health of active satellites, service or repair stranded ones, or dispose of defunct objects by pulling them into Earth's atmosphere.

## The ride up: Transporter-18

The launch itself was, by SpaceX's current standards, routine — which is precisely what makes it remarkable. Liftoff came at 18:32 UTC on October 1 from Space Launch Complex 4E at Vandenberg, bound for a sun-synchronous orbit. The Falcon 9's first-stage booster returned to a nearby landing pad while the upper stage continued to orbit with its cargo. About an hour and three minutes after liftoff, according to SpaceX's flight timeline, "SSPICY Otter" deployed into space and powered up for orbital operations.

It was one small release among 130. The Transporter-18 manifest, as reported at the time, included Cowboy Space's first power-beaming satellite, a prototype satellite for Google's planned AI data-center constellation, and a NASA-funded probe built to track the energy released by galaxies and supernovas — the SPRITE CubeSat, developed by the University of Colorado Boulder's Laboratory for Atmospheric and Space Physics. That a debris-inspection demonstrator could hitch a ride on a commercial rideshare, wedged between a power-beaming experiment and a Google prototype, says something about how normalized orbital operations have become. The inspection business is entering the era of the rideshare: small, specialized spacecraft launched cheaply and often.

For Starfish Space, it was the culmination of seven years of work. The company was founded in 2019 by Austin Link and Trevor Bennett, two engineers who had previously worked at Jeff Bezos' Blue Origin. It has grown to more than 120 employees and has raised over $150 million in venture capital, including a Series B of about $110 million in April 2026 led by Point72 Ventures, with Activate Capital and Shield Capital as co-leaders. The Otter vehicle is roughly the size of a kitchen oven, with solar panels — orange and black, folded flat against its body for launch — that unfold once it reaches orbit.

## A long road of small prototypes

Otter's first full-scale flight followed two smaller, harder lessons. Otter Pup 1, about the size of a microwave oven, launched in June 2023 and almost immediately ran into trouble: an anomaly sent it into a high-rate tumble. The team recovered the spacecraft and, in April 2024, managed to rendezvous with a D-Orbit vehicle to within about a kilometre, imaging it — a partial success salvaged from a shaky start.

Otter Pup 2 launched in June 2025 aboard Transporter-14 to attempt something bolder: docking with a satellite that had never been designed to be docked with. Its original target was withdrawn, and in May 2026 Starfish switched to ElaraSat, a satellite operated by Australia's Gilmour Space that had flown on the same rideshare. The full outcome of that docking attempt has not been publicly verified.

Those prototypes are the unglamorous foundation under SSPICY. Rendezvous and proximity operations — RPO in the industry's shorthand — look simple in renderings and are brutally difficult in practice. Closing the last few hundred yards with another object at orbital velocities of roughly 7.8 kilometres per second requires a spacecraft to know exactly where it is relative to its target, to predict where both will be seconds from now, and to fire its thrusters with exquisite timing, all while the target may be tumbling and the lighting shifts from blinding sunlight to pitch darkness every 45 minutes. Doing it with minimal input from the ground — as SSPICY will — raises the bar further.

Starfish also ran a software test in 2025, code-named Remora, to validate its approach technologies before committing to the full-scale vehicle. The company's pitch is that Otter's small size — "an order of magnitude smaller than other servicing vehicles," in the company's own description — makes it faster to build, faster to launch, and economically viable at a scale the servicing industry has never achieved.

## How the inspections will work

The technical choreography of SSPICY is where the mission earns its name. Three systems work together, and NASA notes that this mission marks the first time they will be tested together as an integrated system.

First, autonomous guidance, navigation, and control. Approaching a dead satellite is not like docking with the International Space Station, which cooperates: it holds still, it communicates, it has docking targets and lighting. SSPICY's targets do none of that. Otter will use computer vision and onboard sensors to track each target's relative position and motion in real time, feeding software that decides moment by moment whether to continue the approach or abort. That abort logic is arguably the most important piece of software on the mission — a servicer that cannot safely back away is a servicer that becomes debris itself.

Second, electric propulsion. Moving between up to four targets in different orbits takes a great deal of maneuvering, and chemical propellant is heavy. An electric propulsion system — efficient, low-thrust, long-burning — lets Otter travel between objects without carrying an enormous fuel load. It is the difference between a sprinter and a marathon runner, and inspection tours are marathons.

Third, an articulating robotic boom that directs the spacecraft's thrusters for precise maneuvers between orbits. Pointing thrusters on a gimbaling arm, rather than firing fixed thrusters in the traditional way, gives Otter fine control over how it moves between targets — a capability NASA specifically wants demonstrated, because future servicing missions will need exactly that kind of precision to rendezvous with uncooperative objects.

What Otter collects at each stop is an engineering dossier: spin rate, orientation, surface condition, geometry, orbital characteristics. Multiplied across four targets, that becomes the first close-range dataset on what dead satellites actually look like after years — sometimes decades — of exposure to micrometeoroids, thermal cycling, and radiation. Engineers designing capture mechanisms, grapples, and deorbit tugs have been working largely from theory and ground observations. SSPICY gives them ground truth.

## Why this matters now: the sky is filling up

SSPICY arrives at a moment when the orbital debris problem is visibly worsening — and the numbers are stark.

In September 2026, ESA's Space Debris Office published its annual Space Environment Report, and the trend lines all point the same way: up. Tracking data cited in October put the population of low Earth orbit at more than 31,000 tracked objects, including more than 18,600 payloads — working and nonworking — of which more than 15,800 are functioning satellites. Over 10,000 of those working satellites are Starlinks. ESA's own figures show the tracked debris population climbing from roughly 16,000 objects in 2005 to more than 44,000 in 2026, an increase of about 180 percent. NORAD tracks more than 32,000 objects larger than about 10 centimetres, while ESA estimates roughly 500,000 fragments between 1 and 10 centimetres and more than 100 million particles smaller than a centimetre. The total mass of tracked orbital debris now exceeds 9,000 tonnes, and more than 3,000 defunct but intact satellites are still circling.

These are not abstract statistics. Fragmentation events — explosions of leftover propellant, battery failures, collisions — continue at an average rate of about 12 per year. Just days after SSPICY launched, the commercial tracking company LeoLabs reported that Meteor 2-7, a defunct Soviet weather satellite that ceased operations in 1982, had broken apart in orbit around October 5, 2026, generating an estimated tens of fragments. The satellite had already fragmented once before, in March 2004; now one of its pieces appears to have broken up further. And in January 2026, a Russian "inspector" satellite disintegrated in the much higher geostationary orbit.

The collision risk is no longer theoretical for operators, either. In a semiannual report to the U.S. Federal Communications Commission earlier this year, SpaceX disclosed that each Starlink satellite performed more than 40 collision-avoidance maneuvers per year between June 2025 and May 2026 — dodging debris or other spacecraft. Hugh Lewis, a space sustainability expert and professor of astronautics at the University of Birmingham, has warned that a collision involving an operational constellation satellite is increasingly likely despite every effort to avoid one.

This is the context that makes SSPICY more than a technology demo. The 3,000-plus dead, intact satellites in orbit are the largest single source of future debris: each one is a potential fragmentation event waiting for a micrometeoroid strike, a battery explosion, or a collision. Removing them — active debris removal — is widely seen as necessary to keep the situation from worsening. But you cannot remove what you cannot safely approach, and you cannot safely approach what you have never closely inspected. Inspection is the first rung of the ladder, and until now it was a rung NASA had never paid a commercial company to climb.

## The business taking shape around the mission

SSPICY is one contract in a portfolio that suggests Starfish Space is positioning Otter as the Swiss Army knife of orbital operations. The company's disclosed government business includes a $37.5 million U.S. Space Force contract for a satellite docking demonstration plus a $54.5 million follow-on mission, and a $52.5 million Space Development Agency contract for the disposal of military satellites. Commercially, the company has missions lined up for geostationary orbit — life-extension missions for satellites operated by Intelsat, among others — where the economics are most compelling: a communications satellite worth hundreds of millions of dollars can earn years of extra revenue if a small servicer nudges it back into its slot or takes over station-keeping when its own fuel runs low.

That mix — NASA inspection in low orbit, Space Force docking demos, SDA disposal, Intelsat life extension in geostationary orbit — traces the full arc of the servicing market as it is emerging: inspect first, then dock, then extend life or dispose. The inspection rung carries the smallest revenue, as industry analysts note, but it is the rung everything else stands on. No insurer, no operator, and no government customer will pay for a capture mission without confidence that the servicer can find and characterize its target. SSPICY is, in effect, NASA paying to de-risk the foundation of an industry.

The company is also candid that Otter is built for more than one job. SSPICY's Otter may take on additional missions during its operating lifetime, and the vehicle's design anticipates a future in which inspection, life extension, orbital transfer, and disposal are not separate spacecraft but separate tasks for the same platform. Space Systems Command's Colonel Joyce Bulson has described Otter's capabilities as going well beyond maneuvering — station keeping, life extension, orbital transfer, and, in her words, "responsible orbital disposal." Otter carries a docking mechanism designed to capture spacecraft that were never built with docking in mind — the Nautilus capture mechanism, with components contributed by Honeybee Robotics — though docking is explicitly not part of SSPICY's demonstration goals.

There is also a competitive dimension worth watching. Starfish is not alone in chasing the servicing market: Northrop Grumman's Mission Extension Vehicles have already docked with commercial satellites in geostationary orbit, Astroscale of Japan is pursuing debris removal with missions backed by European and British agencies, and ESA awarded ClearSpace a contract in January 2026 for a 2027 mission to test rendezvous and proximity operations. What distinguishes the American commercial approach, and SSPICY in particular, is the attempt to make the vehicles small and the business case scalable — an order of magnitude smaller than traditional servicing vehicles, built faster, launched on rideshares, and tasked across multiple missions in a single lifetime.

## What happens after the inspection

The most important question about SSPICY is the one the mission itself does not answer: what do you do with a dead satellite once you have looked at it?

The inspection data feeds directly into that question. A servicer that knows a target's tumble rate and axis can plan a capture; one that knows the surface is intact can choose a grapple point; one that finds the structure degraded can decide the object is a candidate only for a stand-off disposal nudge rather than a docking. NASA has said the technology demonstration and the inspection data will help the agency and industry better understand how spacecraft could inspect and eventually service, or assist with the disposal of, objects in orbit.

The disposal endgame matters because of the arithmetic of the debris environment. ESA's 2026 report flagged a structural problem: not enough satellites leave heavily congested orbits at the end of their lives, and the expected number of objects and collisions in low Earth orbit is rising in what the agency describes as a runaway effect. More than three intact satellites or rocket bodies reenter the atmosphere every day, while about ten new payloads launch daily — the inflow roughly keeping pace with, or exceeding, the outflow. In that environment, every dead satellite that can be inspected, understood, and eventually removed is one fewer lottery ticket in the fragmentation draw.

There is also a subtler payoff: anomaly investigation. Satellites fail for reasons their operators never fully understand, because the evidence stays in orbit. Close-range inspection can reveal whether a solar array drive seized, a micrometeoroid punctured a tank, or a thermal blanket came loose — forensic engineering at hundreds of yards. Over time, a library of such inspections could change how satellites are designed, steering manufacturers away from the failure modes that create the most dangerous debris. Bo Naasz's line about gathering "data on what may leave spacecraft stranded" points exactly here: SSPICY is as much a pathology mission as an inspection one.

## The road to 2027

For now, the mission is in its quietest phase. Commissioning a spacecraft — verifying power, communications, propulsion, sensors, and software in the unforgiving environment of orbit — takes months, and Starfish had published no public word on Otter's health in the days immediately after launch. That silence is normal; what matters is what happens when the checkout is complete and Otter begins its slow, autonomous tour of the graveyard.

The inspection targets themselves have not been publicly named, and NASA has described them only as inoperable objects of U.S. origin — satellites and rocket bodies. That constraint is partly legal and partly practical: inspecting another nation's dead satellites without permission would be a diplomatic incident, and inspecting your own is the responsible place to start. But the technologies being demonstrated are nationality-agnostic. A vehicle that can safely approach an American rocket body can approach anyone's — which is why inspection missions sit at the intersection of commerce, sustainability, and space security, and why every servicing demonstration is watched closely by more than its customer.

It is worth being clear-eyed about what SSPICY is not. It will not remove a single piece of debris. It will not repair anything. Four inspections, however successful, will not bend the curve of a debris population growing by thousands of tracked objects per year. What it can do is prove that the first step is possible — that a small, affordable, commercially built spacecraft can find the dead, study them up close, and come back for more — and hand the industry the dataset it needs to take the second step.

That is how infrastructure gets built: not with a single heroic cleanup, but with a boring, repeatable capability that makes the next capability possible. The railroads of orbit will not be laid by one mission. They will be laid by missions like this one, launched on rideshares, celebrated on Times Square billboards, and then quietly going to work in the dark — hundreds of yards from a dead satellite, cameras on, learning everything it can about how things end up there, so that the next visitor can do something about it.
