China has opened its space station to the rest of the world. On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, a Long March 2F rocket lifted off from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in northwestern China carrying the Shenzhou-24 spacecraft — and aboard it, Pakistani payload specialist Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, who became the first foreign national ever to visit China's Tiangong space station. About three and a half hours after liftoff, the spacecraft docked automatically with the station's Tianhe core module, and the China Manned Space Agency declared the launch a complete success.

It is a threshold moment for a programme that, for more than two decades, has flown only Chinese citizens. Since the first crewed Shenzhou flight in 2003, every astronaut to reach a Chinese orbital outpost has been Chinese. Shenzhou-24 breaks that pattern deliberately, and it does so with a mission architecture so unusual that the visitor will not even come home on the spacecraft that carried him up. Ali will spend about a week aboard Tiangong, run a set of joint China-Pakistan experiments, and then return to Earth aboard the Shenzhou-23 spacecraft alongside two members of the crew he is relieving — while the two Chinese astronauts he flew up with stay behind for roughly six months.

The flight is also a record-setter for its commander. Tang Hongbo, 51, is now the first astronaut to visit Tiangong three times, having previously flown on Shenzhou-12 in 2021 and Shenzhou-17 in 2023. He described himself at the pre-launch press briefing as a witness to two historic moments: the first Chinese crew's entry into China's own space station, and now the first foreign astronaut's visit to it. Between the commander's third visit, a rookie flight engineer on his first trip, and a guest astronaut carrying a new flag into orbit, Shenzhou-24 packs an unusual amount of history into a single launch.

## Liftoff from Jiuquan

The Long March 2F rocket, serial number Y24, lifted off from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in Gansu province at 8:18 a.m. Beijing time on Wednesday, November 25 — 00:18:52 UTC, or 07:18 WIB. The China Manned Space Agency said that about ten minutes after liftoff, the Shenzhou-24 spacecraft separated successfully from the carrier rocket and entered its designated orbit, and that the three astronauts were in good condition. The agency called the launch a complete success.

The timing was exact. The crew had been announced only the day before, on November 24, in keeping with China's practice of naming each crew one day ahead of launch. On launch morning the three astronauts departed the Wentian Pavilion crew quarters at Jiuquan and formally reported to the commander-in-chief in the pavilion's Yuanmeng — "dream fulfillment" — Garden Square before boarding the spacecraft. The China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation said the rocket and ground teams had made 12 technical improvements to enhance safety and reliability for this flight.

The launch also carried a quiet numerical milestone: it was the 680th flight of the Long March rocket series and the 24th flight of a Shenzhou spacecraft. Shenzhou-24 is China's 18th crewed spaceflight overall, and the eighth mission of the station's application and development phase — the phase in which Tiangong, completed in 2022, is operated as a working laboratory rather than assembled.

For readers following this mission from the start, our earlier preview laid out the plan and the stakes: <a href="/blog/shenzhou-24-pakistani-astronaut-tiangong-november-2026/">Shenzhou-24 Pakistani Astronaut Tiangong November 2026: China's First Foreign Visitor Heads to Orbit</a>. What was a plan on November 10 is now a completed launch and docking.

## Three and a half hours to Tianhe

After reaching orbit, Shenzhou-24 flew an autonomous fast rendezvous profile, the same approach China has refined across its recent station missions. At 11:56 a.m. Beijing time — 03:56 UTC, 10:56 WIB — the spacecraft docked with the forward port of the Tianhe core module. The whole docking process took approximately three and a half hours, the China Manned Space Agency said, and the spacecraft then formed a three-module, three-spacecraft assembly with the station.

The agency said the astronaut trio would then enter the Tianhe module, where the Shenzhou-23 crew was waiting to receive them. In the first official docking reports, the actual opening of the hatches and the crew's entry into the station had not yet been confirmed — the docking itself, however, was confirmed by multiple official outlets within the hour, with real-time imagery from the Beijing Aerospace Control Center showing the two spacecraft joined.

The fast rendezvous matters for more than convenience. A three-and-a-half-hour trip from launch pad to station door is the product of precise orbital phasing, a launch window timed to the station's pass over Jiuquan, and an automated guidance system trusted to do the final approach without a human hand on the controls. It is the same profile China has used for its recent crew rotations, and it worked again without a reported hitch.

## The crew: a veteran, a rookie, and a guest

The Shenzhou-24 crew is deliberately balanced: one spacecraft pilot, one flight engineer, and one payload specialist, each representing a different strand of the mission.

**Tang Hongbo, commander and spacecraft pilot, 51.** Tang was selected in China's second astronaut group in May 2010 after serving as a fighter pilot, and he hails from Hunan province. He flew on Shenzhou-12 in June 2021, a three-month mission that put one of the first crews aboard Tiangong, and he commanded Shenzhou-17 from October 2023 on a six-month stay. Shenzhou-24 is his third spaceflight and his third visit to Tiangong — a first for any astronaut. At the pre-launch briefing he framed his career in those terms, calling himself a witness to two historic moments: the first Chinese crew's entry into the station, and the arrival of its first foreign visitor.

**Wu Yi, flight engineer, 39.** Wu comes from Shaanxi province and was selected in China's third group of astronauts in September 2020. Shenzhou-24 is his first spaceflight. He said at the briefing that the crew had prepared comprehensively — in theory, skills, physiology and psychology — and had trained emergency responses until they were muscle memory. He also said that after six months of joint training in spacecraft operations, emergency procedures and scientific experiments, the three crewmates could anticipate one another's actions.

**Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, payload specialist, 40.** Ali is a former Pakistan Air Force pilot and the first Pakistani ever to reach orbit. He is one of the first foreign astronauts selected under a China-Pakistan cooperation agreement signed in February 2025, and he has been training since April at the China Astronaut Research and Training Center in Beijing alongside fellow Pakistani astronaut Khurram Daud. His role is science, not flying: as payload specialist he will look after a set of experiments during a stay of about five to ten days, then come home aboard the Shenzhou-23 capsule together with two of the three astronauts already living on the station.

The choice of a Pakistani as the first guest is not an accident. China's Foreign Ministry gave the mission its diplomatic seal days before launch, with spokesperson Guo Jiakun telling reporters at a regular Beijing press briefing that the flight would mark "another major milestone in China-Pakistan cooperation." The mission is the first joint flight involving Chinese and foreign astronauts in the history of China's manned space programme, and Beijing has presented it as the opening of Tiangong to international partners — a station it built alone, after being excluded from the International Space Station, now being offered as a shared workplace.

## What the crew will do in orbit

The Shenzhou-24 mission's main objectives are to complete an in-orbit crew rotation with the Shenzhou-23 crew, maintain routine station operations, conduct space science and application work, and carry out maintenance, public outreach and payload tests, the China Manned Space Agency said at the pre-launch press conference. A total of more than 90 scientific experiments and technology tests are planned, covering space life sciences, space material science, microgravity fluid physics and combustion science, fundamental physics, space medicine and new space technologies.

The science programme has several headline items. In space life sciences, researchers will build vascular chips and multi-type organ chips to explore how the space environment affects major physiological processes such as vascular aging, aiming to develop new strategies for preventing and treating age-related vascular diseases. In fundamental physics, the mission will explore new states of matter and new physical laws through optical-lattice quantum simulation and research on novel quantum states, as well as key technologies for inertial measurement based on the space cold-atom interferometer.

Ali's own work is the most closely watched part of the programme. Four China-Pakistan cooperative projects in the fields of biotechnology and materials will be conducted during the mission, and Ali will personally conduct an in-orbit experiment on photosynthetic cyanobacteria. He will also install experiment samples and units and carry out in-orbit experiments as scheduled, joining the crew's routine work while focusing on the joint science. The cyanobacteria experiment is a fitting choice for a first guest mission: photosynthetic microbes are a candidate technology for future life-support systems, and studying them in microgravity is directly relevant to the long-duration missions both countries aspire to.

The station itself is a working science platform now, not a construction site. Tiangong's recent research has included work at temperatures colder than anything in nature — our earlier report on the station's cold-atom physics is here: <a href="/blog/tiangong-9-7-picokelvin-coldest-atoms-november-2026/">Tiangong 9.7 Picokelvin Coldest Atoms: The Record Chill Made in Orbit</a>. Shenzhou-24's quantum-simulation and cold-atom work continues that thread, using the station's microgravity environment for physics that cannot be done on the ground.

## A week, then a swap

The most unusual feature of Shenzhou-24 is its split schedule. The mission is divided into a short-duration stay and a long-duration stay, and the two halves end on different spacecraft.

After completing his short joint flight, Ali will return to Earth aboard the Shenzhou-23 spacecraft together with Shenzhou-23 crew members Zhang Zhiyuan and Li Jiaying, who have been aboard Tiangong since May 2026. The Shenzhou-23 crew's six-month stay is drawing to an end; during it, the trio conducted experiments on brain function and sensory perception, using EEG equipment to monitor how the brain's networks change over time in space, and carried out only one spacewalk, when commander Zhu Yangzhu and Zhang Zhiyuan spent around five and a half hours outside the station on August 28, with Li Jiaying supporting from inside.

Once the crew handover is complete, Tang Hongbo, Wu Yi and Shenzhou-23 astronaut Zhu Yangzhu will remain on the station. Tang and Wu will stay for roughly six months. Zhu Yangzhu will stay on for a further six months and become China's first astronaut to spend a year in orbit in a single visit — the first long-duration stay experiment of that length by a Chinese astronaut.

The swap is possible because Shenzhou spacecraft stay docked at the station as lifeboats: each crew arrives in one and leaves in the one that was already there, so the station is never without a return vehicle. It is a routine piece of station operations, but the optics are new — a guest astronaut arriving on one nation's spacecraft and departing on another flight's vehicle, with a third crew member's year-long stay beginning in the same handover.

## Timeline: from announcement to docking

- **February 2025** — China and Pakistan sign a cooperation agreement under which Pakistani astronauts will train and fly to Tiangong.
- **April 2026** — Muhammad Zeeshan Ali and Khurram Daud begin training at the China Astronaut Research and Training Center in Beijing.
- **May 2026** — The Shenzhou-23 crew (Zhu Yangzhu, Zhang Zhiyuan, Li Jiaying) arrives at Tiangong for a six-month stay.
- **August 28, 2026** — Zhu Yangzhu and Zhang Zhiyuan conduct a spacewalk of about five and a half hours; Li Jiaying supports from inside.
- **November 7, 2026** — The China Manned Space Agency announces that a Pakistani citizen will fly as a payload specialist on Shenzhou-24.
- **November 9, 2026** — China's Foreign Ministry calls the flight "another major milestone in China-Pakistan cooperation."
- **November 24, 2026** — The Shenzhou-24 crew is formally announced at Jiuquan: Tang Hongbo, Wu Yi and Muhammad Zeeshan Ali.
- **November 25, 2026, 08:18 Beijing time** — Long March 2F lifts off from Jiuquan; Shenzhou-24 separates about ten minutes later and enters its designated orbit.
- **November 25, 2026, 11:56 Beijing time** — Shenzhou-24 docks with the Tianhe core module's forward port after a roughly three-and-a-half-hour autonomous rendezvous.
- **About a week later** — Ali is scheduled to return aboard Shenzhou-23 with Zhang Zhiyuan and Li Jiaying.
- **May 2027 (planned)** — Shenzhou-24 returns to Earth, landing in Inner Mongolia.

## How Shenzhou-24 compares with earlier Tiangong crews

| Mission | Launch | Commander | Notable first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shenzhou-12 | June 2021 | Nie Haisheng | First crew to live aboard Tiangong; Tang Hongbo's first flight, three-month stay |
| Shenzhou-17 | October 2023 | Tang Hongbo | Tang's first command; six-month stay |
| Shenzhou-23 | May 2026 | Zhu Yangzhu | Crew rotation now being completed; one spacewalk (Aug 28, ~5.5 hours) |
| Shenzhou-24 | November 2026 | Tang Hongbo | First foreign astronaut aboard Tiangong; first astronaut to visit the station three times; Zhu Yangzhu to reach a one-year stay |

The comparison shows how fast the programme has moved from construction to routine operations. Shenzhou-12 was a pathfinding mission: a three-month stay in a station that was barely more than its core module. Shenzhou-17 was a standard six-month rotation. Shenzhou-24 is something else — a six-month rotation that also carries a guest, runs more than 90 experiments, and sets up the first year-long Chinese stay. The station has gone from a destination to a workplace, and the crew manifest now reflects that: a commander on his third visit, a rookie engineer, and a foreign scientist who will be home within a fortnight.

It is also worth noting what Shenzhou-24 is not. It is not a tourist flight: Ali is a trained payload specialist flying a defined science programme under a government-to-government agreement, selected through the same kind of process as China's own astronauts. And it is not a one-off gesture without follow-through: China has said it will soon conduct a joint launch-site rehearsal of the Long March 10A carrier rocket and the Mengzhou crewed spacecraft at the Wenchang Satellite Launch Center, and will push forward with a technology-verification test flight of the Long March 10 carrier rocket and the first flight mission of the Lanyue lander — the hardware for its planned crewed Moon landing around 2030. ## Why this flight matters beyond the headlines

Three things make Shenzhou-24 significant, and they operate on different timescales.

First, the diplomatic signal is immediate. Tiangong was built after China was excluded from the International Space Station, and for years Beijing has said the station would be open to international partners. Shenzhou-24 is the first time that promise has been kept in orbit rather than on paper. Pakistan is a close partner — the two countries describe their relationship as an "all-weather" friendship — but the precedent matters more than the partner: the selection process, the training pipeline in Beijing, and the short-stay guest architecture are all now proven, and they can be repeated with other countries. China has already received applications and expressions of interest from other nations; the next guest will not be a first, which is precisely the point.

Second, the operational signal is about endurance. Zhu Yangzhu's coming year in orbit is the kind of mission that tests everything a long-duration programme needs: radiation exposure, bone and muscle loss, psychological strain, and the medical countermeasures that must work for a full year rather than six months. A year-long stay is a stepping stone to the Moon: China's lunar plan involves crews spending extended periods on the surface, and the only way to learn how a body handles a year in space is to fly a year in space. Shenzhou-24's handover sets that experiment in motion.

Third, the science signal is about what Tiangong is for. More than 90 experiments across life sciences, materials, fluid physics, combustion, fundamental physics and space medicine is a heavy manifest for a single increment, and the cold-atom and quantum-simulation work in particular uses the station as a laboratory that cannot be replicated on Earth. The cyanobacteria experiment Ali will run is small in itself, but it points at the same question the whole manifest asks: what can only be learned up there?

There are limits to what can be claimed on launch day. The hatches had not yet been confirmed open in the first official reports, Ali's experiments have not yet run, and the year-long stay has not yet begun. The launch and docking are confirmed; the rest is a plan being executed, and the reporting will follow the results. What is already certain is the fact that will define this mission in the history books: on November 25, 2026, a Pakistani astronaut joined a Chinese crew aboard a Chinese station, and the door of Tiangong opened to the world.

## What happens next

The next milestone is the crew's entry into the Tianhe module and the formal handover with the Shenzhou-23 crew — the moment Ali actually steps aboard the station as its first foreign visitor. After that comes a week of joint science, then the return flight: Ali, Zhang Zhiyuan and Li Jiaying riding Shenzhou-23 home while Tang, Wu and Zhu begin the long increment that will carry Zhu to a full year in orbit.

Watch also for the numbers that will follow. The mission's 90-plus experiments will produce results over months, not days, and the most interesting of them — the organ chips, the quantum simulation, the cyanobacteria — will be reported as they conclude. And watch the guest list: if Shenzhou-24's short-stay model works as planned, the question will quickly become who flies next, and from which country. The first foreign astronaut to visit Tiangong is a Pakistani pilot who trained for seven months in Beijing. The second will be a different story, and it will be written on the precedent this flight set.
