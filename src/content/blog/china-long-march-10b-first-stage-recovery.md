Six minutes after it fell away from a climbing rocket, the 760-ton first stage of a brand-new Chinese launch vehicle stopped falling the way rockets are supposed to stop falling. It did not splash into the ocean. It did not slam onto a concrete pad. It was *caught* — snagged by hooks on its own hull onto a grid of tensioned steel cables stretched across the deck of an autonomous ship steaming through the South China Sea, 430 kilometers from where it had lifted off. Smoke still curled off its engines when the world's cameras found it hanging there, suspended in a wire net like a prize catch.

On July 10, 2026, at 12:15 p.m. China Standard Time, China did something it had never done before: it recovered the first stage of an orbital-class rocket. And in doing so, it did something no one in the world had ever done: the first sea-based, net-capture recovery of a carrier rocket, according to the China National Space Administration. With that single flight of the Long March-10B Y1, China became only the second nation on Earth — after the United States — to bring an orbital booster home intact.

The milestone lands in a year when reusability has become the decisive currency of the global space race. SpaceX has stacked up more than 600 booster landings since its first in 2015. Blue Origin has spent the decade clawing its way toward routine reuse with New Shepard and, more recently, New Glenn. China, for all its relentless launch cadence — routinely 60-plus orbital launches a year — had never once recovered an orbital booster. Until Friday.

## A Maiden Flight With Two Missions

The Long March-10B Y1 lifted off from Commercial Launch Pad 2 at the Wenchang Commercial Space Launch Site on Hainan Island, China's southernmost spaceport. The vehicle stands about 63 meters tall and tips the scales at roughly 760 tonnes at liftoff, generating some 890 tonnes of thrust from seven YF-100K engines burning liquid oxygen and kerosene. The second stage is a different animal: a single YF-219 engine burning liquid oxygen and methane, the first methalox upper stage to fly on a major Chinese orbital rocket.

Ascent went exactly as the China Academy of Launch Vehicle Technology (CALT) had drawn it up. The seven YF-100Ks burned for roughly two and a half minutes before shutting down, and the stages parted. The second stage's YF-219 ignited to continue the push into orbit, and it would go on to do its job flawlessly: delivering its payload, an experimental satellite built by the China Satellite Network Group and designated CX-26, into a predetermined 800-kilometer low Earth orbit.

That payload matters more than its terse designation suggests. The China Satellite Network Group — China SatNet — is the state-owned operator behind Guowang, the planned 13,000-satellite broadband constellation meant to be China's answer to Starlink. CX-26 is widely understood to be a technology demonstrator for that constellation, and its delivery on a reusable rocket's maiden flight was a deliberate statement: this vehicle exists to loft China's satellite-internet ambitions cheaply and often.

But the second stage's success, while essential, was the less-watched half of the mission. All eyes were on what the first stage would do after separation. About six minutes later, it did what no Chinese booster had ever done: it came home.

## Anatomy of a Catch

The first stage's return was, by the standards of this strange art, textbook. After coasting and adjusting its attitude, the booster reignited three of its YF-100K engines for a braking burn, then throttled down to a single engine for the final, precise terminal descent. Its target was the *Linghangzhe* — "Navigator" — an autonomous recovery vessel holding station in the South China Sea.

*Linghangzhe* is no ordinary drone ship. The 144-meter-by-50-meter vessel, delivered by CALT in November 2025, displaces roughly 25,000 tonnes at full load and carries corner-mounted lidar units that track the incoming booster in real time. But its signature feature is the recovery rig itself: a cross-shaped array of pretensioned steel cables mounted on rail-guided sliders. As the booster descended, the sliders repositioned the cables to widen the effective capture window, compensating in real time for the last deviations in the booster's trajectory.

The booster, for its part, carries hooks on its exterior. It descended vertically into the wire grid, and the hooks snagged the tensioned cables — arresting the stage and leaving it suspended in midair. No landing legs. No concrete pad. No touchdown burn onto a barge deck, SpaceX-style. A net, a ship, and a set of hooks.

The catch took place about 430 kilometers downrange from Wenchang. CNSA called the launch a "complete success," and Chinese state media framed the recovery as "the world's first successful sea-based net-based recovery of a carrier rocket" — a claim that, on the available evidence, holds up. No one else has recovered an orbital-class booster this way.

## Why a Net? The Engineering Logic

To an American audience raised on SpaceX's tail-first touchdowns, the net looks exotic — almost whimsical. But the engineering logic is hard-headed. Landing legs are heavy. They add mass to the booster, and every kilogram of structure on a first stage is a kilogram of payload that cannot fly. They also demand precision: the booster must touch down within a tight envelope on a fixed deck, and the final landing burn must kill the vehicle's velocity to exactly zero at exactly deck height.

Chen Muye of CASC explained the trade-off to official media in plain terms: "net-based recovery simplifies the onboard structure, reduces weight, and boosts payload capacity," adding that the arrangement tolerates small landing deviations better than a legged touchdown.

In its reusable configuration, the Long March-10B is designed to deliver up to 16 tonnes to low Earth orbit — a figure that would make it the most capable partially reusable rocket China has ever fielded. The net is a direct contributor to that number: by offloading the landing hardware to the ship, the rocket carries more fuel and more payload. The five-meter-diameter common core of the Long March-10 family — one of the largest rocket diameters China has flown — exists to serve the same master: raw lift.

There is also a subtler point. A net catch does not require the booster to survive a touchdown transient on legs; the cable grid absorbs the last of the energy over a wider capture window. For a program still building its flight experience with recovered hardware, that margin is not a luxury. It is the design.

## The Gap That Closed — and the One That Remains

China's space officials framed the flight as filling a domestic gap, and they were not exaggerating. For a decade, the country's launch industry watched SpaceX turn booster reuse from a stunt into a production process: more than 600 successful recoveries since the first Falcon 9 landing in December 2015, individual boosters flying 30-plus times, launch costs driven down to levels expendable rockets cannot touch. China launched prolifically in that decade — dozens of orbital missions a year, lunar sample returns, a space station assembled in orbit — but every single orbital booster it flew was thrown away.

The Long March-10B changes the arithmetic. It is, in CALT's words, China's first operational reusable rocket — the country's first proven partially reusable launch vehicle. Chinese officials have said they plan to refly this very first stage before the end of the year, a turnaround that, if achieved, would demonstrate not just recovery but the beginning of genuine reuse.

But "recovery" and "reuse" are not the same word, and the distinction matters. SpaceX's dominance rests not on one catch but on a decade of iterative operations: refurbishment flows, fleet management, boosters flying their 30th and 35th missions. One net capture is a proof of concept; a reusable launch program is a production line. China has closed the recovery gap. The reuse gap — the hard, grinding work of flying the same hardware again and again — is still ahead.

The competition is not standing still, either. Blue Origin spent years and billions reaching orbital flight with New Glenn and continues to push toward routine booster recovery. Europe's Ariane 6, Japan's H3, and India's next-generation launchers are all watching the same cost curve. Reusability is no longer a differentiator. It is the table stakes of the 2020s launch business, and China has just bought in.

## The Road to the Moon Runs Through Reusability

The Long March-10B is not a one-off technology demonstrator. It is a member of a family — and the family has a destination: the Moon.

CALT describes the Long March-10 series with a neat formula: "one diameter, two types of engines, and three modules." One diameter: the 5.0-meter common core. Two engine families: the kerolox YF-100 line and the methalox YF-219 line. Three variants: the Long March-10A, a kerosene/liquid-oxygen two-stage vehicle designed to launch the Mengzhou crew spacecraft on human spaceflight missions; the Long March-10B, the kerolox-first-stage, methalox-second-stage variant that just debuted; and the Long March-10C, an all-methalox design that Chinese officials have positioned as the country's primary commercial launch workhorse.

The 10A is the moon rocket. China has committed to landing its astronauts on the lunar surface before the end of the decade, and the Long March-10 is the vehicle meant to get them there — lofting the Mengzhou crew capsule and the Lanyue lunar lander, with a possible first crewed test flight later this year. A reusable 10B, proven on commercial and constellation missions, de-risks the engines, the avionics, and the flight operations that the crewed program will depend on. Every net capture at sea is, in that sense, a rehearsal for the Moon.

That is why the maiden flight carried a China SatNet payload to 800 kilometers: the Long March-10B is being built as the truck that will deploy Guowang, China's 13,000-satellite answer to Starlink, at a launch cadence and cost that expendable rockets cannot sustain. Reusability is not a prestige project for Beijing. It is infrastructure — for the constellation, for the space station's future logistics, and for the lunar program.

## The Long Road to the Net

The *Linghangzhe*'s catch did not come out of nowhere. It was the third attempt at recovering an orbital-class Chinese booster — and the first to succeed. The road to that net is littered with instructive failures.

The first attempt belonged to the commercial sector. In December 2025, Beijing startup LandSpace flew its Zhuque-3 — a stainless-steel, methalox-fueled heavy launcher — for the first time. The rocket reached orbit as planned, but the first stage did not complete its controlled landing. It was, nonetheless, a historic flight: the first time any Chinese entity had attempted an orbital booster recovery. LandSpace's Zhuque program had been building toward that moment for years — the Zhuque-2 became the first liquid-oxygen-methane vehicle to reach orbit when it first flew in 2022, and went on to fly eight times.

Weeks after the Zhuque-3 attempt, the state program took its turn. The Long March-12A, a state-developed reusable-aspirant, also reached orbit — and also failed in its recovery. Two attempts, two boosters lost, in the span of about a month. The pattern was familiar to anyone who had watched SpaceX's early landing attempts in 2015 and 2016, when boosters toppled, exploded, or simply missed. The difference was that China was learning in public, on operational orbital missions, with the whole industry watching.

The suborbital groundwork had been laid earlier. In September 2024, commercial startup Deep Blue Aerospace flew its Nebula-1 vehicle on a high-altitude vertical recovery test in Inner Mongolia, practicing the powered-descent choreography — the flip, the braking burn, the terminal guidance — that an orbital recovery demands. The company has since pushed ahead with its Thunder-RS engine, a 130-tonne-class kerolox powerplant that completed a 100-second steady-state test firing, and is now developing the larger Nebula-2 with a capture system openly inspired by SpaceX's "chopsticks" tower arms.

The competitive field is crowded. Alongside LandSpace and Deep Blue, companies like Space Pioneer, i-Space, and Galactic Energy are all racing to field reusable vehicles, driven by the same prize: contracts to deploy China's low-Earth-orbit internet megaconstellations, Guowang and Qianfan. Reusability in China is not a single program's vanity project. It is an industry-wide bet, with state and commercial teams pursuing different architectures — nets at sea, legs on land, towers with arms — toward the same economic goal.

That is what makes the Long March-10B's success structurally significant rather than merely symbolic. It was not a startup's gamble or a one-off stunt. It was the state program — CALT, CASC, the institutional core of Chinese launch — demonstrating that the country's mainstream rocket engineering has internalized recovery. The net over the South China Sea is now the baseline against which every other Chinese approach will be measured.

## What Comes Next

Three days after the catch, China laid out more of its commercial strategy: the all-methalox Long March-10C will serve as the country's primary commercial workhorse, backed by a modular design philosophy and growing launch infrastructure on Hainan Island. The message was unmistakable — the net capture was not an experiment to be admired and filed away. It was the opening move in a campaign.

The immediate milestone is the refly. Chinese officials have said the recovered first stage will fly again before the end of the year. If that happens on schedule, China will have demonstrated the full loop — launch, recover, refurbish, relaunch — in under six months. SpaceX took years to compress that loop; the industry will be watching to see how fast China's learning curve runs.

Longer term, the questions multiply. Can CALT scale production of the YF-100K and YF-219 to support a reusable fleet? Can the net-capture system handle the turnaround tempo that constellation deployment demands? Will the 10C's methalox architecture deliver the cost targets that make Guowang's 13,000 satellites economically feasible? And how will the United States — which has treated space launch as a strategic industry since the NSSL program began — respond to a China that can now recover boosters too?

For now, though, the image endures: a smoking rocket stage hanging in a steel net above the South China Sea, 430 kilometers from where it launched, caught rather than crashed. On July 10, 2026, China stopped throwing its rockets away. The rest of the decade will show what it does with them.
