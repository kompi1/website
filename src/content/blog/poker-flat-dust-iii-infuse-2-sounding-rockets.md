Two small rockets are about to spend roughly fifteen minutes each in space, and that will be enough to tackle two of astronomy's biggest questions: how the universe builds the dust that becomes planets, and how dying stars scatter their remains across the cosmos.

The University of Alaska Fairbanks announced on October 16, 2026, that two sounding rocket missions are preparing to launch from Poker Flat Research Range in Alaska during a launch window that opens October 19 and runs through October 23. One is NASA's DUST III, a flying dust-formation laboratory on its third trip to space. The other is INFUSE-2, the Integral Field Ultraviolet Spectroscopic Experiment from the University of Colorado Boulder's Laboratory for Atmospheric and Space Physics (LASP), carrying the first far-ultraviolet integral field spectrograph built for astrophysics. Both payloads will fly on two-stage Black Brant IX rockets to an altitude of roughly 180 miles, fall back to Earth for recovery, and be done — in minutes.

Each mission is a follow-up to earlier flights from White Sands Missile Range in New Mexico. Bringing both to Poker Flat in a single coordinated campaign turns one of the world's busiest aurora research ranges into a proving ground for laboratory astrophysics, ultraviolet instrumentation, and the next generation of space scientists. And in at least one respect, the campaign is a study in opposites: DUST III manufactures something the universe makes everywhere, while INFUSE-2 tries to see something no instrument has ever seen in a particular kind of light.

## DUST III: Building Stardust From Scratch

The Determining Unknown yet Significant Traits mission — DUST — is the kind of experiment that sounds simple until you understand what it is really trying to do. Led by planetary scientist Joe Nuth of NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, DUST flies a small materials-science lab into space so that it can manufacture cosmic dust the way the universe does: by letting atoms released from dying stars collide and stick together in weightlessness.

Inside the payload, roughly the size of a small motorbike, a set of metal wires coated with magnesium silicates waits for launch. Once the rocket coasts through microgravity above the atmosphere, the wire is heated and atoms and molecules diffuse away. Some collide and stick, beginning to form dust grains; others never do. Using spectroscopy and other instruments, the team measures exactly when grains begin to grow and link into aggregates, noting the temperatures and densities at which they grow best. The payload then falls back to Earth under a parachute and is recovered for laboratory analysis.

Why go to all this trouble for dust? Nuth's answer has become one of the most quoted lines in planetary science. "Without cosmic dust, you don't have any terrestrial planets," he said. "Without dust, there's no life on Earth because there wouldn't be an Earth."

That is not rhetoric. Dust accounts for roughly two percent of the visible matter in the universe, but its influence is wildly disproportionate. Dust condenses into asteroids and planets. Giant dust clouds carry gas from dying stars and ferry it across space to fertilize new star systems. Dust surrounding young planets can keep them warm, providing surfaces where water can collect and organic molecules can form. But whether any of those things happens depends on the smallest details of how dust grains are constructed — their composition, their structure, and the conditions under which they assemble.

"Traditional astronomers — the people looking at galaxies and stars — they hate dust," Nuth once said. "It's the stuff that's in their way." He loves it, precisely because so much of cosmic history hides inside it.

DUST first flew on October 7, 2019, from White Sands, reaching an altitude of 210 miles during a roughly 14-minute flight. The team reported that everything went perfectly, and the hardware came back for analysis. The mission is a collaboration between NASA and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), and its science traces back to laboratory work by Nuth and colleagues on one of the thorniest problems in dust chemistry: dust grains can in principle form from any of the 92 naturally occurring elements, and each element behaves differently. Modeling all of them at once is, in Nuth's words, very tough to model.

His earlier experiments uncovered a surprising simplification. Some elements block one another inside a growing grain: if iron gets in, for instance, it tends to keep magnesium out. That interference means the chemistry of dust formation may be governed by just one or two materials rather than dozens — "much easier," Nuth noted, "if you only have to worry about one or two particular materials." The rocket flights test those laboratory findings in the one environment no lab can reproduce: genuine microgravity, where grains form without settling under gravity's pull.

Flying DUST a third time matters because cosmic dust science is a game of controlled repetition. Each flight refines the temperature and density curves at which grains begin to assemble, and each recovery brings home hardware that can be dissected on the ground. The jump from White Sands to Poker Flat also broadens the mission's operational envelope: NASA's Sounding Rockets Program, managed at the Wallops Flight Facility and funded by the agency's Heliophysics Division, uses multiple ranges so that science payloads are not tied to a single piece of geography. The Black Brant IX the missions will ride is the program's workhorse, capable of lifting instruments to altitudes of 180 to 200 miles and returning them for reuse.

For an experiment that fits inside a motorbike-sized can, the stakes are planetary — literally. Every grain DUST grows in those few minutes of weightlessness is a small-scale rehearsal of processes that, across billions of years, built every rocky world we know.

## INFUSE-2: A New Kind of Ultraviolet Vision

If DUST III is a laboratory, INFUSE-2 is an eye — and one tuned to a kind of light no astrophysics instrument has ever captured this way.

The mission's full name, the Integral Field Ultraviolet Spectroscopic Experiment, describes an instrument that does two things at once: it takes a picture of an astronomical object and records the spectrum — the rainbow of wavelengths — of every point in that picture simultaneously. Astronomers call this integral field spectroscopy, and it is one of the most powerful tools in modern observational astronomy because it hands scientists a complete map of an object's light instead of forcing them to choose between imaging and spectroscopy.

"What an integral field spectrograph does is combine the two," said Brian Fleming, the INFUSE principal investigator. "It gives you an image of an object at every wavelength, or color, of light. We developed INFUSE to bring this powerful tool to the ultraviolet, which has never been done before."

Bringing it to the ultraviolet is the hard part. Earth's atmosphere blocks far-ultraviolet light almost completely, so UV astronomy can only be done from space, and space telescopes are enormously expensive and slow to build. Sounding rockets offer a shortcut: a fifteen-minute flight above the atmosphere is long enough to point a telescope at a target and record data that cannot be obtained from the ground.

INFUSE-2's target is the Dumbbell Nebula, also catalogued as Messier 27. It is the glowing, expanding shell of gas thrown off by a dying star — a planetary nebula, one of the nearest and brightest of its kind, roughly 1,360 light-years away in the constellation Vulpecula. It is one of the most photographed nebulae in the visible spectrum, where it glows in familiar greens and reds. But in the far ultraviolet, M27 has never been imaged at all.

Scientists suspect that view could reveal an entirely different structure. Far-ultraviolet light traces the hottest, most energetic gas in the nebula — the material most recently expelled by the star's dying remnant — and mapping it point by point would show how a dying star distributes its material back into interstellar space. That material is the raw inventory of future star systems: the carbon, oxygen, and heavier elements forged in the star's interior, now on their way to become someone else's planets.

INFUSE-2 is the second flight of the instrument. INFUSE-1 launched from White Sands in October 2023 as the first far-ultraviolet integral field spectrograph to reach space, proving the concept worked. INFUSE-2 is designed to deliver roughly an order of magnitude better performance, and its data will feed directly into design decisions for future missions — including, the team says, NASA's Habitable Worlds Observatory, the next-generation flagship space telescope aimed at the late 2030s. A technology that flies for fifteen minutes on a sounding rocket today can become the instrument that images another Earth tomorrow.

## SUMO: Micromirrors Bound for Orbit

Flying alongside INFUSE-2 is a second payload with a different kind of ambition: SUMO, the Spectroscopic Ultraviolet Multi-Object Observatory technology demonstration.

"The point of the SUMO prototype is to demonstrate, in space for the first time, the use of a digital micromirror-based multi-object spectrograph," said Dmitry Vorobiev, the SUMO principal investigator and a research scientist at LASP.

The idea is disarmingly clever. Digital micromirror devices — arrays of millions of tiny, tiltable mirrors — are the same technology used in commercial video projectors. By pointing individual mirrors at selected targets in the sky, SUMO can feed the light of many astronomical objects into a spectrograph at once, without the fixed, expensive slits of traditional instruments. Because the hardware is available commercially, it dramatically reduces the cost of space instruments. SUMO will also test LASP-developed electronics and software that can autonomously select which objects to observe — technology that could one day fly on space telescopes, small satellites, and planetary science missions.

Like INFUSE, SUMO is as much about people as hardware. Students have been central to both payloads: graduate and undergraduate members of the Colorado Ultraviolet Spectroscopy Program contributed to instrument design, assembly, testing, calibration, software development, and mission operations. Several will travel to Alaska to support the launch and flight.

Among them is INFUSE experiment lead Alex Haughton, who recently defended his doctoral thesis and will begin a postdoctoral research position early next year with the Ultraviolet Explorer team at the University of California, Berkeley. Haughton worked on every part of the mission and has published three papers on the INFUSE instrument and its technical development. "Working on the INFUSE rocket program has taught me how to design, build, and lead space missions, and I'm excited to steer the telescope during the flight," he said.

His summary of why sounding rockets keep winning may be the campaign's best slogan: "An advantage of sounding rocket projects is that we get to launch multiple times and improve the technology on each flight." INFUSE-1 proved the far-ultraviolet integral field spectrograph was possible; INFUSE-2 demonstrates the performance leap; and whatever comes next — whether a small satellite or a flagship observatory instrument — will stand on both flights.

Vorobiev put the human dividend even more plainly: "These NASA grants are taxpayer investments, and one of the best ways to use that funding is to educate the science and technology workforce of the future. The students who worked on this mission are now trained engineers and scientists starting their careers, and I think that's a great outcome."

## Why Fifteen Minutes Is Enough

To an outside observer, the most surprising thing about the Poker Flat campaign may be how little time the science actually needs. A sounding rocket carries its instruments up, lets them work, and brings them home without ever entering orbit. NASA describes typical time in space as five to twenty minutes. That sounds like a handicap until you consider what it buys.

First, cost. Common launch hardware like the Black Brant IX, limited tracking requirements, and recoverable payloads keep sounding rocket missions orders of magnitude cheaper than orbital missions. Second, speed. An instrument team can design, build, fly, recover, modify, and fly again on timescales of years rather than decades — the exact cycle Haughton described. Third, focus. A short flight can answer one sharp question — when do dust grains start to grow? What does the Dumbbell Nebula look like in the far ultraviolet? — that would be diluted inside a flagship mission carrying dozens of competing instruments.

That is why the sounding rocket program sits, in New Space Economy's apt phrase, between ground testing and long-duration spaceflight. It is where risky ideas earn their flight heritage: a micromirror array proven in a fifteen-minute flight becomes credible for a satellite; a spectrograph demonstrated twice becomes a candidate for a flagship telescope. NASA's long tradition of using sounding rockets this way is one reason instruments keep maturing into the observatories of the next decade.

## Poker Flat: More Than an Aurora Range

The campaign also says something about Poker Flat itself. Located at Mile 30 of the Steese Highway north of Fairbanks, the range is owned by the University of Alaska Fairbanks Geophysical Institute and is supported by NASA's Sounding Rocket Program Office. Its reputation rests on auroral research — the shimmering northern lights that draw scientists to Alaska's high-latitude skies.

But neither DUST III nor INFUSE-2 needs the aurora. In fact, according to the university's campaign announcement, the rockets can launch as early as the first night of the window because neither mission requires clear skies or auroral activity to do its science. DUST manufactures its own dust regardless of the sky above, and INFUSE-2 looks straight out through the atmosphere it is trying to escape. That scheduling flexibility is exactly what makes Poker Flat valuable beyond aurora season: the infrastructure, tracking, and recovery expertise built for one kind of science serve many others.

The launch window runs nightly from 9:30 p.m. to 1:30 a.m. between October 19 and October 23. For those who want to follow along, the range posts updates and a live video feed on its Facebook page, and launch notifications are available by texting PFRRLAUNCHES to 866-485-7614. Two rockets, two nights at most, two teams of scientists and students holding their breath for fifteen minutes apiece.

## What Comes Next

Assuming the flights go as planned, both teams' real work begins after recovery. The DUST III payload will be trucked back for dissection, its newly grown grains analyzed grain by grain to extend the temperature and density curves Nuth's team has been building since 2019. The INFUSE-2 and SUMO teams will process their fifteen minutes of far-ultraviolet data — the first ever integral-field UV portrait of the Dumbbell Nebula, and the first space demonstration of micromirror spectroscopy — and feed the results into the instrument designs that may fly on the Habitable Worlds Observatory and the small satellites of the 2030s.

There is a pleasing symmetry to the pair. DUST studies how matter assembles itself from atoms into the seeds of planets; INFUSE studies how matter is scattered back into space when a star dies, completing the cycle. One experiment builds dust; the other watches the wreckage of the star that made it. Between them, they trace the full loop of the material universe: from dying star, to drifting grain, to new world.

Joe Nuth's line about dust bears repeating as these rockets rise over Alaska: without dust, there would be no terrestrial planets — no Earth, and no life on it. A motorbike-sized can full of heated wire, lofted for a quarter of an hour above the Arctic, is humanity's way of asking how the most ordinary stuff in the universe came to be. Fifteen minutes, it turns out, is plenty of time to begin an answer.
