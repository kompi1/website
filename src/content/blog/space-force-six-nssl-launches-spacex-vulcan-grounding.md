The Pentagon is done waiting. On Wednesday, October 21, the U.S. Space Force confirmed it is transferring six National Security Space Launch missions from United Launch Alliance to SpaceX, an unusually public reshuffling of America's most sensitive launch manifest that underscores just how long — and how consequentially — ULA's Vulcan Centaur rocket has been grounded.

The announcement, made by Space Systems Command commander Lt. Gen. Philip Garrant during an industry briefing, amounts to the largest single wave of reassignments since Vulcan's national security flights were suspended in February. It also marks the next chapter in a saga that began with a shower of debris over Cape Canaveral, deepened with a second engine anomaly at a rival company, and has now rippled through satellite constellations, NASA science missions, and even the rumored sale of ULA itself.

"The military remains confident in Vulcan's return to flight," Garrant said, even as he acknowledged that the ongoing technical investigation into a solid rocket booster anomaly has yet to establish an official return date.

## The Burn-Through That Started It

Vulcan Centaur V-005 lifted off from Space Launch Complex 41 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station at 4:22 a.m. EST on Thursday, February 12, 2026, carrying the classified USSF-87 payload into geosynchronous orbit. The flight was only Vulcan's fourth, and its first operational national security mission after two certification flights and a 2025 commercial flight that had flown without drama.

Seconds after liftoff, cameras caught what engineers feared: an anomalous plume erupting from one of the four Northrop Grumman GEM 63XL solid rocket boosters strapped to Vulcan's first stage. Post-flight analysis suggested the motor's exhaust had burned through the throat of its nozzle — a failure that produced asymmetric thrust and sent the rocket rolling more than intended during its climb.

"There was some asymmetric thrust when we had that solid motor fail, it reduced performance," Gary Wentz, ULA's vice president of Government and Commercial Programs, told Spaceflight Now in the days leading up to the Artemis 2 launch. "The BE-4s and our avionics system gimbaled to control that. We did see some roll, and the BE-4s were able to compensate to arrest that roll. We were well within our environments and limits, so it was nothing overly concerning for the BE-4s."

Both the Vulcan booster and the Centaur upper stage performed as designed for the rest of the flight, and the payload reached its intended geosynchronous orbit. The mission was a success — but the booster was not. Fragments of the damaged nozzle fell into waters likely too deep for recovery, complicating the forensic work that followed.

For the Space Force, a successful delivery was not enough. A joint Air Force–industry investigation board was stood up, and Space Systems Command suspended operational defense payloads on Vulcan pending a full root-cause determination. Vulcan has not flown a national security mission since.

## Two Anomalies in Four Flights

What made February's incident so difficult for ULA was that it was not the first. In October 2024, during Cert-2 — the second of two certification demonstration flights required before Vulcan could carry high-priority national security payloads — engineers watched one of the GEM 63XL nozzle's components fall away during the climb out of the lower atmosphere. That rocket, too, still reached orbit, and debris from that event was recovered for analysis.

Two nozzle-related irregularities in four flights was a pattern the Pentagon could not ignore. After extensive hot-fire testing in Utah in the wake of the Cert-2 anomaly, ULA and Northrop Grumman had certified fixes and returned the vehicle to flight. February's burn-through suggested those fixes had not addressed the underlying issue — or that a new failure mode had emerged in the same hardware.

The flight history tells the story of a rocket still early in its career: an inaugural launch on January 8, 2024, that sent Astrobotic's Peregrine lunar lander on its way to the Moon; the Cert-2 flight in October 2024 with its nozzle detachment; a nominal third flight in 2025; and then USSF-87 in February 2026. ULA once projected that Vulcan would be flying 20 to 30 times per year by now. It has flown four times in two years.

## The Road Back Is Long

To its credit, ULA and Northrop Grumman moved quickly on the engineering front. On April 15, 2026, Northrop Grumman performed a successful static fire of a GEM 63XL booster in Utah, a test ULA said was designed to "demonstrate nozzle design enhancements which were already in work and an advanced propellant technology for future solid rocket motors across their portfolio." A ULA spokesperson said the data, combined with the investigation's findings, would validate the analytical models supporting Vulcan's return to flight.

By May, Wentz was publicly projecting that "Vulcan's going to get back to flight by the end of the year," with Amazon — not the Space Force — as the likely return-to-flight customer. That same month, ULA hoisted the first Vulcan booster inside its newly completed Vertical Integration Facility–Amazon (VIF-A) at Cape Canaveral, giving the company two parallel stacking lanes for the first time and a physical platform for the sustained, high-cadence campaign Amazon's satellite constellation demands.

But the summer brought a second front in the battle. In May 2026, Blue Origin discovered what was described as an explosive anomaly tied to the main oxygen valve on a BE-4 engine — the same engine that powers Vulcan's first stage — and began modifying engines already installed on Vulcan vehicles at the Decatur, Alabama factory and at Cape Canaveral. On May 28, a New Glenn vehicle exploded on the pad at Launch Complex 36 during a prelaunch static fire, destroying a lightning tower and the transporter erector at Blue Origin's only orbital pad. Both vehicles use the same engine.

ULA said it aims to return Vulcan to flight before the end of 2026, and recent reporting placed a no-earlier-than date of October 29 on the return-to-flight mission, followed by an Amazon Leo mission before year's end. The investigation, however, remains formally open, with ULA and Northrop Grumman continuing structural analyses and hot-fire validations to verify booster nozzle integrity before Space Systems Command clears the vehicle for operational defense launches.

## Swapping Missions, Not Canceling Contracts

The six reassigned missions announced this week were not canceled — they were traded. Under Space Systems Command's contractual mission-trade mechanism, near-term, time-critical military payloads move to SpaceX's Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy, while ULA receives later national security missions to fly aboard Vulcan once the rocket is recertified. The approach protects launch schedules without punishing ULA's revenue stream.

There is precedent. After the February anomaly, the Space Force began moving individual missions off Vulcan. On March 20, 2026, Space Systems Command shifted GPS III satellite vehicle 10 to a Falcon 9 — reportedly the fourth such reassignment of a GPS III mission — and compensated ULA with the USSF-70 mission for summer 2028 to balance the Phase 2 allocation. The October announcement simply scales that same playbook to half a dozen payloads at once.

The trades have been one-directional because only one side of the market is standing still. GPS satellites cannot wait in storage indefinitely, and national security timelines are set by orbit mechanics and constellation health, not by engineering schedules. Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy, with their deep flight heritage and cadence measured in dozens of launches per year, are the only certified vehicles positioned to absorb the work.

## Ripple Effects Across the Manifest

The Vulcan grounding has not stayed confined to the Pentagon's manifest. In early October, NASA announced it was switching its SunRISE mission — six small satellites that will study the Sun's corona as an interferometer — from a Vulcan ride to a Falcon Heavy, where it will fly as a secondary payload sponsored by the Space Force. The launch had originally been targeted for the summer of 2026. As a secondary payload, SunRISE surrenders control over its exact launch date, and the next available Space Force Falcon Heavy flight is not expected until 2027.

The larger disruption sits with Amazon. ULA's order book holds 38 Vulcan launches for Amazon Leo — the broadband constellation formerly known as Project Kuiper — plus nine Atlas V flights, six of which had flown as of April 2026. Amazon entered 2026 far behind its Federal Communications Commission milestones: a July 2020 authorization required 1,618 satellites in orbit by July 30, 2026, and the company had only 231 production satellites aloft after an April Atlas V flight. The FCC granted an extension on June 5, 2026, keeping the full July 2029 deployment deadline but removing priority status for launches after July 31, 2026. Amazon cited rocket capacity — and Vulcan's grounding — among its reasons.

ULA has taken the commercial side of its recovery seriously. The company completed a $1.5 billion private bond placement in August 2026, upsized from an initial $500 million target, to fund expanded assembly lines and tooling at Decatur, modifications to its Florida and California pads for higher flight rates, and reserves for engine and motor purchases. Satellites are already stacked for the first Leo Vulcan missions, and Amazon Leo has said the integrated test configuration at the new VIF-A facility will help validate the procedures and ground systems for a sustained, high-cadence campaign.

## A Company in Transition

The grounding has arrived at an awkward moment in ULA's corporate history. The joint venture — owned 50-50 by Boeing and Lockheed Martin since 2006 — has been led by interim chief executive John Elbon since December 2025, after the prior CEO departed following nearly 12 years in the role. Reports on September 10, 2026 renewed long-running speculation that the two owners might move to divest the company; no sale has been confirmed, and both owners' first-quarter 2026 filings disclosed that the Vulcan suspension was hurting the venture, with Lockheed Martin warning of possible operating losses or impairment.

Any transaction would inherit a formidable but complicated portfolio: a backlog of more than 80 missions, a newly issued $1.5 billion debt stack, a production plan built around 25 launches per year that the company has not yet achieved, and — critically for the Pentagon — 19 missions won under the National Security Space Launch Phase 3 Lane 2 awards announced in April 2025. Those awards, covering roughly 54 of the nation's most demanding missions from 2027 through 2032, gave SpaceX 28 missions worth more than $5.9 billion, ULA 19 worth more than $5.3 billion, and Blue Origin seven worth nearly $2.4 billion.

The math of assured access is what makes this week's announcement sting. The Space Force pays a premium of roughly 30 percent to keep a second certified launch provider healthy — the Phase 3 Lane 2 ceilings imply an average of about $280 million per ULA mission against about $211 million for SpaceX. That premium buys resilience: the ability to keep flying national security payloads when one vehicle is grounded. This week, resilience meant flying them on the first provider instead.

## What Comes Next

The near-term calendar offers the first real test of ULA's recovery timeline. Reporting has pointed to a no-earlier-than date of October 29 for Vulcan's return-to-flight mission carrying Amazon Leo satellites — a commercial flight that would let ULA shake down its fixes before the Space Force hands back operational payloads. A second Leo mission could follow before year's end, offering the beginnings of the cadence the company promised.

But recertification for national security launches is a separate, more demanding gate, and the Space Force has not defined it publicly. The joint investigation board has not released interim findings, and Garrant declined to offer even an approximate return target. Until that clearance arrives, every near-term defense payload will flow to SpaceX under the trade mechanism — and each reassignment quietly extends the runway Falcon needs to cement its position as the default ride for the nation's most critical missions.

For the Space Force, the strategy is working exactly as designed: payloads are flying, contractors are not being punished, and Vulcan can return on engineering merit rather than schedule pressure. For ULA, the message is just as clear. The Pentagon will keep the seat warm — but it will not keep it empty.

## New Entrants Waiting in the Wings

One irony of Vulcan's grounding is that it is unfolding while the Space Force is actively trying to widen the national security launch market. Under Phase 3 of the National Security Space Launch program, new companies reach the government market through Lane 1 — a contract whose ceiling tripled to $17 billion on July 17, 2026, with the expected mission count rising from about 60 to about 170 over the 2025–2034 contract period.

The provider pool has grown to seven: SpaceX, ULA, and Blue Origin were on-ramped first, Rocket Lab and Stoke Space joined in March 2025, and Impulse Space and Relativity Federal were added on July 8, 2026. But the binding constraint is the first flight. Rocket Lab and Stoke Space each received a $5 million task order for a capabilities assessment and tailored mission assurance planning, and neither may compete for launch task orders until it completes a successful first launch — and both first flights have slipped toward 2027.

Rocket Lab's Neutron has not yet flown; a first-stage tank ruptured in a hydrostatic test on January 21, 2026, and by August the company said the window for an end-of-year debut was narrowing, with integrated static fire testing at its Virginia pad as the riskiest remaining milestone. Stoke Space announced an initial $1 billion tranche of a Series E round on September 8, 2026, bringing its total capital raised to $2.3 billion, yet its Nova Pathfinder first flight slipped from late 2026 to early 2027. Task orders awarded so far in Lane 1 have gone to SpaceX, with Blue Origin receiving its first on May 28, 2026 — hours before its New Glenn vehicle exploded during a static fire.

In other words, the cavalry is coming, but it is not here yet. The Lane 2 missions — roughly 54 of the most demanding payloads from 2027 through 2032 — remain the preserve of the same three providers that won them in April 2025. When one of those three is grounded, the burden falls entirely on the other two, and this week the Space Force made explicit which of the two is absorbing the load.

The dependency also runs deeper than the manifest. Vulcan's first stage burns the BE-4 engine built by Blue Origin — a direct competitor in both the commercial and national security markets — and its strap-on boosters come from Northrop Grumman, another defense prime. A vehicle whose two most critical propulsion elements are supplied by other companies concentrates risk in ways that are now visible in the manifest data. As one industry analysis put it, a buyer or lender will price that dependency before it prices anything else.

## The Test Ahead

If the reported October 29 return-to-flight target holds, the coming weeks will offer the first public evidence of whether ULA's engineering fixes have taken hold. A clean flight carrying Amazon Leo satellites would not recertify Vulcan for national security missions — that is a separate gate, governed by the investigation board's findings and Space Systems Command's clearance — but it would begin to rebuild the flight record the company needs.
