## A number from the first five minutes

There is a number that describes the universe when it was only a few minutes old, and astronomers have just measured it more precisely than ever before. The number is called the primordial helium abundance, written as Yp, and the new value is 0.2458, with an uncertainty of just 0.0013. In plain terms, about a quarter of the ordinary matter forged in the Big Bang ended up as helium, and researchers now know that fraction to a precision of 0.5 percent — roughly three times tighter than the best previous estimate.

The result, reported this month across a series of five papers in The Astrophysical Journal, is the product of more than 130 hours of observing time on the Large Binocular Telescope in Arizona and an international collaboration spanning the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, Ohio State University, Gonzaga University, Northwestern University, The University of Texas at Austin, Indiana University, the University of California Santa Cruz, the University of Illinois, the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, and TRIUMF. It is, in the words of University of Minnesota professor Evan Skillman, "a physics experiment on a grand scale and one of the biggest findings in my entire 40-year career."

"It is a fundamental number that tells us specifically about the conditions of our universe in its first five minutes," Skillman said. "It has diagnostic power that speaks directly to the Standard Model of Physics."

That claim is worth unpacking, because the number itself looks unglamorous. Yp = 0.2458 is a mass fraction: of all the ordinary matter — the baryonic matter that makes up stars, planets, and people — that emerged from the hot early universe, 24.58 percent of its mass was helium. The rest was overwhelmingly hydrogen, with only traces of deuterium and lithium. Measuring that fraction precisely is one of the few ways to test physics under conditions no laboratory on Earth can recreate: temperatures of billions of degrees, densities beyond imagination, and an entire universe expanding and cooling in minutes.

## The first three minutes, retold

Modern cosmology rests on three observational pillars: the expansion of the universe, the cosmic microwave background radiation, and the abundances of the lightest elements. Of the three, the helium pillar has historically been the least well measured, lagging far behind the exquisite precision achieved for the other two. The new measurement begins to close that gap.

The story starts about 13.8 billion years ago, when the universe expanded from an extremely high-density, high-temperature state. In its first second, the cosmos was a seething soup of protons, neutrons, electrons, photons, and neutrinos, too hot for atomic nuclei to hold together. As the universe expanded and cooled, protons and neutrons began to fuse. This era, known as Big Bang nucleosynthesis, lasted only a few minutes. In that brief window, nuclear reactions built the lightest elements: most protons remained as hydrogen nuclei, roughly a quarter of the mass became helium-4 nuclei, and tiny amounts of deuterium, helium-3, and lithium were produced.

Then the window closed. The universe had cooled too much, and its density had dropped too far, for fusion to continue. The composition was frozen in. Everything that came later — the first stars, the galaxies, the periodic table beyond lithium — was built from that starting mix, enriched over billions of years as stars fused heavier elements and scattered them in supernova explosions. To measure the primordial helium abundance today, astronomers must therefore find gas that has barely been touched by stars: material whose composition still resembles the young cosmos.

That is the central difficulty of the measurement, and the reason it took most of a century to refine. Almost every galaxy astronomers can observe has been chemically enriched by generations of stars, which manufacture fresh helium in their cores and return it to the interstellar gas. The primordial signal is buried under stellar contamination, and separating the two has been the field's long-standing challenge.

## Fifteen pristine galaxies

The new work attacks that problem by choosing its targets ruthlessly. Rather than surveying a huge number of galaxies and extrapolating backward to estimate the original helium abundance, the researchers selected just 15 unusually pristine, distant galaxies: compact systems that have experienced relatively little chemical enrichment. Their gas preserves a composition much closer to the one left behind by the young cosmos, which means the correction for stellar contamination is small — and small corrections carry small errors.

The observing campaign used more than 130 hours on the Large Binocular Telescope, a facility with twin 8.4-meter mirrors on Mount Graham in Arizona. With its MODS spectrographs, instruments developed at Ohio State University, the team measured more than 10 helium emission lines alongside 15 hydrogen lines in each galaxy's spectrum. Comparing those signals allowed the scientists to model subtle effects — including temperature, density, and other sources of systematic error — that become decisive when the target is sub-percent accuracy.

That attention to systematics is what separates the new measurement from its predecessors. Earlier estimates were limited not by the amount of data but by the difficulty of modeling the physical conditions inside the glowing gas clouds where the helium lines originate. If the temperature of the gas is misjudged, or its density misestimated, the inferred helium abundance shifts. By measuring many spectral lines simultaneously, the team could constrain those conditions from the data itself rather than assuming them, and the five-paper series documents the analysis in detail.

"We promised a half-percent uncertainty in our proposal, and we got there," Skillman said. "In the unpredictable world of science, that doesn't happen very often."

The collaboration's approach also matters for a second reason. A single galaxy, however pristine, can mislead; a single spectrograph can carry a subtle bias. Fifteen galaxies, analyzed through a consistent pipeline across five papers, provide a check on both. The result is a measurement whose uncertainty is dominated by well-understood statistics rather than by unknown systematics — the kind of uncertainty that can be quoted with confidence and, crucially, compared against theory.

## Why a decimal tests the Standard Model

The reason physicists care about a number like 0.2458 is that the amount of helium produced in the early fireball depends on conditions that no other measurement probes in quite the same way. Two stand out.

The first is the expansion rate of the early universe. In the first minutes, the competition between nuclear reactions and cosmic expansion set the outcome: a faster-expanding universe would have frozen the neutron-to-proton ratio earlier, when more neutrons survived, and produced more helium. A slower expansion would have produced less. The helium abundance is therefore a fossil record of how fast the universe was expanding when it was minutes old — an era far earlier than the cosmic microwave background, which dates from about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, can directly probe.

The second is the inventory of light particles. The expansion rate in the early universe was driven by the energy density of radiation, and that density depended on how many species of light particles existed — including the number of neutrino families. By measuring the helium abundance precisely, researchers can effectively count the neutrino families present in the early universe and check the answer against the three families known from particle accelerator experiments. Agreement between a cosmological count and a laboratory count, across such wildly different regimes, is one of the most stringent tests of the Standard Model.

That is what Skillman means by "diagnostic power." The Standard Model predicts a specific helium abundance given the measured cosmic baryon density from the microwave background. If the measured value had come out significantly different, something would have to give: either the standard picture of the first minutes is wrong, or new particles or new physics altered the expansion. The new value, 0.2458 plus or minus 0.0013, lands squarely in the range the standard picture expects, tightening the constraints on any exotic alternative.

It also sharpens a long-standing puzzle elsewhere in the light-element family. While helium and deuterium measurements have long agreed well with theory, the predicted abundance of lithium-7 has stubbornly exceeded what astronomers observe in old stars — the so-called lithium problem. A helium measurement at 0.5 percent precision does not solve that puzzle on its own, but it narrows the room for explanations: any proposed fix must now preserve an exquisitely measured helium value while addressing lithium, a much tighter needle to thread.

## Precision cosmology comes of age

The helium result arrives in a year when astronomy's reach into the early universe has been expanding on several fronts. The James Webb Space Telescope has been pushing observations to ever earlier epochs, including a supernova seen just 730 million years after the Big Bang, as covered in <a href="/blog/webb-earliest-supernova-grb-250314a-730-million-years/">Webb's earliest supernova GRB 250314A</a>. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope's deep surveys are mapping the young universe's structure, the subject of <a href="/blog/roman-extreme-deep-field-early-universe-survey/">Roman's extreme deep field survey of the early universe</a>. And studies of massive galaxy clusters continue to test the invisible scaffolding of dark matter, as in <a href="/blog/el-gordo-two-state-self-interacting-dark-matter/">El Gordo's two-state self-interacting dark matter</a>.

The helium measurement belongs to a different tradition from those observational triumphs, but it complements them. Telescopes like Webb and Roman show astronomers what the universe looked like hundreds of millions of years after the Big Bang. The helium abundance reaches back far further — to the first minutes — and it does so not with images but with spectroscopy: the careful reading of light's fingerprints to recover chemistry from the dawn of time.

There is a pleasing symmetry in the method. The helium lines measured in those 15 distant galaxies are produced by the same atomic physics that governs helium in the Sun and in laboratory discharge tubes. The spectrographs that captured them are descendants of instruments that have been refined for more than a century. The leap is not in new physics but in patience: 130 hours of telescope time, a dozen years of instrument development in the telling of some accounts, and a five-paper analysis that refuses to let any systematic error pass unexamined. Precision cosmology, in this case, is less about a single eureka moment than about the accumulation of care.

It is also a reminder of what ground-based astronomy can still do in an era dominated by space telescopes. The Large Binocular Telescope, with its twin 8.4-meter mirrors, gathers far more light than any space telescope of comparable cost, and its spectrographs can dissect that light with exquisite resolution. For work that demands many photons from faint, compact galaxies — exactly the kind of work this measurement required — a large ground-based telescope remains unmatched.

## What comes next

A measurement at 0.5 percent precision does not end the story; it raises the bar for everything around it. The most immediate consequence is for the cosmic baryon density, the total amount of ordinary matter in the universe. The helium abundance and the baryon density are linked through Big Bang nucleosynthesis theory, so a sharper helium value tightens the inferred baryon density independently of the microwave background. If the two independent determinations agree — and current indications are that they do — the standard cosmological model gains another passed test. If a future, even sharper measurement were to reveal tension, it would point to new physics in the first minutes.

The result also sets a target for the next generation of instruments. Extremely large telescopes now under construction, with mirrors of 30 meters and more, will gather far more light from even more distant and pristine galaxies, potentially pushing the helium uncertainty lower still. At some point, the limiting factor will no longer be telescope time but atomic physics: the precision with which helium's spectral lines themselves are understood. That, too, is a laboratory problem, and it means the measurement's future will be written jointly by astronomers and atomic physicists.

There is also the lithium problem, still unsolved. Some proposed solutions invoke new particles that decayed during nucleosynthesis, altering the lithium yield while leaving helium nearly untouched. The new helium measurement constrains such proposals more tightly than before: any exotic particle that changed the expansion rate or the neutron balance enough to fix lithium would also shift helium, and the window for doing one without the other has now narrowed. Other solutions look to stellar astrophysics, arguing that old stars have depleted their surface lithium over billions of years, hiding the primordial value. Distinguishing those explanations will require better models of stellar interiors and more observations of the oldest, most metal-poor stars.

For now, the headline stands on its own: a number from the first five minutes, measured three times more precisely than ever before, agreeing with the standard picture of the universe. In a field where the biggest questions concern invisible matter, invisible energy, and the first fractions of a second, there is something reassuring about a measurement that reaches back to the beginning and finds the theory intact.

"We promised a half-percent uncertainty in our proposal, and we got there," Skillman said. The universe, it turns out, keeps its promises too — at least the ones written in helium.

## The long road to half a percent

It is easy, reading a number like 0.2458 ± 0.0013, to miss the decades of work behind it. The primordial helium abundance has been pursued since the middle of the twentieth century, when George Gamow, Ralph Alpher, and Robert Herman first worked out that a hot Big Bang should leave a distinctive chemical signature. Early measurements, based on a handful of nearby gas clouds, carried uncertainties of ten percent or more — enough to confirm the broad picture but not enough to test it.

Each generation of the measurement has chipped away at the error. Better telescopes reached more distant, less enriched galaxies. Better spectrographs resolved more spectral lines. Better atomic data sharpened the conversion from observed line strengths to abundances. And better models of the glowing gas clouds — the H II regions where young stars ionize their surroundings — reduced the systematic biases that once dominated. The step from roughly 1.5 percent uncertainty to 0.5 percent, achieved in this work, is the largest single improvement in years, and it was earned not by one breakthrough but by doing everything at once: better targets, more lines, more hours, and a more honest accounting of errors.

The choice of targets deserves emphasis. The 15 galaxies in the sample are not random. They are compact, vigorously star-forming dwarf galaxies whose gas has been enriched by only a few generations of stars. Astronomers call them "pristine" in a relative sense — no galaxy is truly untouched — but relative to the Milky Way, whose gas has been recycled through countless stellar generations, they are close to the original recipe. Finding them required surveys that catalogued thousands of candidates; keeping only 15 required discipline. It is a strategy that trades quantity for quality, and the payoff is visible in the final uncertainty.

The spectrographs deserve emphasis too. MODS, the Multi-Object Double Spectrographs on the Large Binocular Telescope, were developed at Ohio State University to capture a wide wavelength range at once, so that helium and hydrogen lines could be measured simultaneously under identical conditions. That simultaneity matters: if helium and hydrogen are observed at different times, through different instruments, or under different skies, subtle calibration differences creep in. Measuring them together cancels many of those differences, and it is one reason the team could model temperature and density effects from the data rather than assuming them.

Finally, the five-paper format itself is a statement. Rather than compressing the analysis into a single letter, the collaboration spread it across five papers in The Astrophysical Journal, giving each element — target selection, observations, atomic physics, modeling, and cosmological interpretation — room to be scrutinized. In a measurement where the claim is precision, transparency is part of the result.

## Why the public should care about a decimal

It is a fair question: why should anyone outside cosmology care about the third decimal place of a number describing the universe's first minutes? The answer has two parts, one practical and one philosophical.

The practical part is that this measurement is a test of the Standard Model of particle physics performed at energies and densities no collider can reach. Every time the Standard Model survives such a test, it strengthens the foundation on which modern technology rests — from semiconductors to medical imaging to the satellite navigation that guides aircraft. Historically, fundamental physics experiments have led to technological breakthroughs in ways no one predicted at the time. A test that probes the universe's first minutes is not a luxury; it is the farthest frontier of the same experimental tradition.

The philosophical part is simpler. The helium abundance is one of the few numbers that connects the universe's beginning to its present. The helium in a birthday balloon, in the Sun, and in the most distant galaxy all trace back, in part, to those first minutes. Knowing that fraction to half a percent means knowing something concrete about an era that lasted less time than a coffee break and happened nearly 14 billion years ago. That a species of primates on a small planet can measure such a thing at all — by collecting photons that traveled for billions of years and reading their spectral fingerprints — is itself a remarkable fact about the universe: it is comprehensible, and its earliest moments left evidence that careful work can still recover.

The new measurement will not make headlines the way a Moon landing does. It has no spacecraft, no astronauts, and no images. It has a number, an uncertainty, and five papers. But numbers like this are the quiet infrastructure of cosmology. They are what turn a beautiful story — the Big Bang — into a tested theory, and they are what will eventually reveal, if anything is ever to reveal it, where the story breaks down.

For now, the story holds. The universe's first five minutes produced helium at a mass fraction of 0.2458, plus or minus 0.0013. The measurement is three times sharper than before, and the Standard Model survives another examination. Somewhere in the light of 15 small, pristine galaxies, the beginning of everything just came a little more clearly into focus.
