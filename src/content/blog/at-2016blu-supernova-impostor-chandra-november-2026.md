For fifteen years, a giant star thirty million light-years from Earth has been putting on the same spectacular show, over and over again. Every hundred and thirteen days or so, it flares up so violently that it looks, from a distance, exactly like a star blowing itself apart. Telescopes catch the flash, astronomers take note, and then — instead of fading into the expanding debris of a supernova — the star is still there, intact, waiting to do it all over again. It has now pulled off this trick at least twenty-seven times.

On Monday, 9 November 2026, NASA announced that the mystery behind the performance has finally been solved. Using the Chandra X-ray Observatory, astronomers have found what is triggering the repeated outbursts of the star known as AT 2016blu: a previously hidden companion object — either a stellar-mass black hole or a neutron star, the collapsed core of a dead massive star — that swings close to the giant star once every 113 days and pulls material away from it. The stolen gas falls onto the compact companion at nearly the speed of light, heats to millions of degrees, and blazes in X-rays. That blast of high-energy light, the researchers say, is what powers the optical flashes that have fooled observers for a decade and a half.

It is the first time astronomers have detected X-rays from this class of erupting star, and the first time any of these objects has been found with a black hole or neutron star companion. The result, published in the latest issue of *The Astrophysical Journal*, does more than explain one strange star. It suggests a link between two of astronomy's most puzzling phenomena — the so-called supernova impostors and the ultra-luminous X-ray sources — and it may help explain why some real stars seem to flicker and flare in the years before they genuinely explode.

## The star that refuses to die

AT 2016blu lives in the spiral galaxy NGC 4559, about 30 million light-years from Earth, sitting near the outer edge of the galaxy's dense spiral arms. It is a type of star known as a luminous blue variable, or LBV — a hot, massive star prone to dramatic changes in brightness. Astronomers estimate its mass at at least 33 times that of the Sun. By stellar standards, that is enormous. Stars this massive live fast and die young, burning through their nuclear fuel in a few million years rather than the billions enjoyed by a star like our Sun, and they tend to go out in spectacular fashion.

What makes AT 2016blu special, even among luminous blue variables, is the metronome regularity of its violence. Between 2012 and 2026, astronomers detected at least 27 bursts of optical light from the star, recurring roughly every 113 days — about three times a year. Each spike in brightness is so extreme that it resembles a supernova explosion, the cataclysm in which a massive star collapses onto itself and hurls its outer layers into space. But unlike a supernova, the star is not destroyed. It survives every outburst, dims back down, and then erupts again on schedule.

"For 15 years, astronomers have been watching this star get really bright and then dim again on a regular basis," said Mojgan Aghakhanloo of the University of Virginia in Charlottesville, who led the new study. "Until we were able to get Chandra data at the right time, we couldn't figure out what was making this happen."

That regularity was the first clue that something unusual was going on. A single star, left to its own devices, does not erupt on a precise 113-day schedule. Stars are chaotic systems; their outbursts are driven by instabilities deep inside them, and while those instabilities can recur, they do not keep time like a clock. A period that steady, repeated more than two dozen times over fourteen years, smells of an orbit. Something was coming around, again and again, on a fixed timetable — and whatever it was, it was doing something to the star each time it passed.

"This star's behavior was confusing at first because it kept acting like a supernova, but clearly it's not because it keeps surviving these outbursts about three times a year," said co-author Núria Torres-Albà, also of the University of Virginia. "To understand the cause of these eruptions, we realized we needed X-ray data, timed so that we'd observe the star when it was in an outburst."

## A 113-day heartbeat

The picture that has now emerged is a binary system with a dark secret at its heart. AT 2016blu, the brilliant blue giant, is not alone. It shares its patch of NGC 4559 with a compact companion — either a stellar-mass black hole or a neutron star — orbiting it on an elongated, oval-shaped path. Once every 113 days, the companion swings in close. At that closest approach, its gravity reaches out and pulls material away from the giant star. The stolen gas does not fall straight in. It spirals around the compact object, orbiting at very high speeds, close to the speed of light, and in the process it is heated to extraordinary temperatures. Matter that hot shines brilliantly in X-rays.

That is the engine of the outbursts. The X-ray blast heats the dense gas surrounding the system, and the heated gas then glows in visible light — the optical flash that telescopes on Earth have been recording, three times a year, since 2012.

"We suspected that the bright repeating flashes at optical wavelengths might actually be powered by periodic blasts of high-energy X-rays," said co-author Nathan Smith of the University of Arizona in Tucson. "The dense gas surrounding the system is heated by the X-rays and then glows in the optical. So, we needed to plan the Chandra X-ray observations carefully to be simultaneous with an optical outburst."

It is a neat piece of detective work, and it explains several features of the star's behavior at once. The strict 113-day period is simply the orbital period of the companion. The ferocity of each outburst reflects the violence of matter falling onto a black hole or neutron star — one of the most efficient energy-releasing processes in the universe. And the fact that the star survives each episode is no mystery at all: the explosions are not happening inside the star. They are happening beside it, in the gas being stripped from its surface and funneled onto its invisible partner.

## The X-ray fingerprint

Proving the idea required catching the star in the act — in X-rays, at the exact moment of an outburst. That is harder than it sounds. X-rays from space cannot be seen from the ground, because Earth's atmosphere absorbs them; only a space telescope can detect them. And the team needed not just any X-ray telescope, but the one instrument sharp enough to separate AT 2016blu from another bright X-ray source sitting nearby in the same patch of sky. Only Chandra, with its sub-arcsecond vision, could do that.

The team laid a trap. To collect the optical data, they used the Katzman Automatic Imaging Telescope, the Las Cumbres Observatory Global Supernova Project, and observations from the American Association of Variable Star Observers — including contributions from more than 30 amateur astronomers — to monitor the star's brightness night after night. When those observations showed that the star was entering an outburst in early 2026, Chandra observations were triggered and obtained in March 2026 as part of the observatory's Target of Opportunity program, which is designed to respond quickly to special events.

The result was decisive. Chandra data from March 2026 revealed that AT 2016blu was at least 100 times brighter in X-rays than in Chandra data from 2001 to 2002 — observations taken before the outbursts began. A star that had been essentially invisible in X-rays a quarter of a century ago was now blazing. The best explanation, the team concluded, is material from the giant star falling onto a neutron star or black hole during the closest approach of their oval orbit.

The timing mattered as much as the brightness. Had Chandra looked a few weeks earlier or later, when the companion was far from the giant star and no material was being transferred, the X-ray signal would have been far weaker — and the connection might have remained hidden for years more. The 113-day clock, once merely a curiosity, became the key to the whole investigation: it told the team exactly when to look.

This is the first X-ray detection of one of this class of erupting stars, and the first of these objects found with a black hole or neutron star companion. Both firsts matter. The first means that an entire class of stellar eruptions — the supernova impostors — now has an X-ray signature that astronomers can search for in other systems. The second means that the impostor phenomenon, long thought to be a quirk of single massive stars, can be driven by binary interaction: a dead star feeding off a living one.

## How the trap was set

The investigation behind the announcement was, in its own way, as remarkable as the result. AT 2016blu is not a bright or famous object. It is a faint point of light in a galaxy 30 million light-years away, and keeping watch over it for fourteen years required patience, coordination, and a surprising amount of help from people who do astronomy for love rather than a living.

The optical monitoring that made the Chandra observations possible came from three sources working together. The Katzman Automatic Imaging Telescope, a robotic telescope that scans the sky for supernovae, kept a regular watch. The Las Cumbres Observatory Global Supernova Project, a network of telescopes spread around the world, contributed its own measurements. And the American Association of Variable Star Observers — the AAVSO — supplied observations from more than 30 amateur astronomers, volunteers who pointed their own telescopes at a distant galaxy and carefully measured the brightness of a star most people will never see.

That amateur contribution deserves a moment's attention. Professional astronomy runs on large telescopes and large budgets, but the sky is vast and the night is long, and no single observatory can watch everything all the time. Networks like the AAVSO fill the gaps, and they have done so for more than a century. In this case, the volunteers' data helped pin down the timing of the outbursts, and the timing was everything: it told the Chandra team when to trigger the Target of Opportunity observations that caught the X-ray flare.

Chandra itself is no ordinary instrument. Launched on 23 July 1999 aboard the space shuttle Columbia on mission STS-93 — commanded by Eileen Collins, the first woman to command a shuttle flight — it is the third of NASA's four Great Observatories, alongside Hubble, the Compton Gamma Ray Observatory, and Spitzer. It carries the sharpest X-ray mirrors ever flown, with an angular resolution of about half an arcsecond, and it can detect sources far fainter than any X-ray telescope before it. More than 27 years after launch, and more than two decades past its five-year design life, it is still the only X-ray telescope with vision sharp enough to separate AT 2016blu from its bright neighbor. NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama, manages the Chandra program, while the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory's Chandra X-ray Center controls science operations from Cambridge, Massachusetts.

## Timeline: fifteen years of fake supernovae

- **2001–2002** — Chandra observes the location of AT 2016blu and finds it faint in X-rays. The outbursts have not yet begun, and nothing about the spot in the sky suggests anything extraordinary.
- **2012** — The first recorded optical outburst. The star flares dramatically, bright enough to be logged as a transient event — and then, instead of fading away like a supernova, it survives.
- **2012–2026** — At least 27 optical outbursts are detected, recurring roughly every 113 days, about three times a year. Each one resembles a supernova in brightness. Each time, the star endures.
- **Early 2026** — Optical monitoring by the Katzman Automatic Imaging Telescope, the Las Cumbres Observatory Global Supernova Project, and AAVSO observers — including more than 30 amateurs — shows the star entering another outburst.
- **March 2026** — Chandra Target of Opportunity observations catch the star during the outburst. It is at least 100 times brighter in X-rays than in 2001–2002.
- **9 November 2026** — The study by Mojgan Aghakhanloo and colleagues is published in *The Astrophysical Journal*, and NASA announces the discovery: a hidden black hole or neutron star companion is pulling material from AT 2016blu every 113 days.

## The family resemblance: Eta Carinae

To understand why astronomers care so much about one star's repeated eruptions, it helps to look at the most famous luminous blue variable of all: Eta Carinae, in our own Milky Way. Eta Carinae sits about 7,500 light-years away in the constellation Carina, and its primary star carries roughly 100 times the mass of the Sun, shining with a luminosity some four to five million times greater than our star. In 1837 it began to brighten, and by March 1843 it had become the second-brightest star in the night sky — despite its enormous distance — in an episode astronomers call the Great Eruption. Over roughly two decades, it threw off a huge amount of material, forming the hourglass-shaped Homunculus Nebula that Hubble has photographed in exquisite detail. And then, like AT 2016blu, it survived.

Eta Carinae is itself now known to be a binary system: its two stars orbit each other every 5.54 years on a highly eccentric path. The parallel with AT 2016blu is striking, and it is not a coincidence that NASA's announcement names Eta Carinae as the well-known example of the class. Luminous blue variables are stars living on the edge of stability. They shine so brightly that the pressure of their own light nearly overcomes their gravity — astronomers call this the Eddington limit — and the slightest disturbance can tip them into shedding enormous amounts of mass. A companion star passing close by, tugging at that barely-held-together outer envelope, is exactly the kind of disturbance that can set off an eruption.

What AT 2016blu adds to this picture is the mechanism, caught in the act and measured in X-rays. Eta Carinae's Great Eruption happened in the 1840s, long before X-ray astronomy existed; no one will ever know what its X-ray output looked like at the time. AT 2016blu's eruptions are happening now, on a 113-day schedule, under the watch of the sharpest X-ray telescope ever flown. For the first time, astronomers can see the engine, not just the exhaust.

There is also a darker possibility lurking in the comparison. Eta Carinae is expected to explode as a genuine supernova in the astronomically near future — perhaps within the next hundred thousand years. Luminous blue variables are, after all, massive stars near the ends of their lives. If some of them are in binary systems with compact companions, then the eruptions that precede the final explosion may themselves be shaped by that companion — and the flickering astronomers see in the years before a supernova may be telling them not just that a star is about to die, but that it has a partner helping it along.

## The ultra-luminous connection

The discovery also reaches into a second mystery. AT 2016blu is bright enough in X-rays to be classified as a candidate ultra-luminous X-ray source, or ULX. These are rare, bright X-ray sources found in other galaxies that astronomers have yet to fully understand. Their X-ray output exceeds what a single stellar-mass black hole or neutron star should be able to produce by steadily feeding — the Eddington limit again — and for years researchers have debated what powers them. The current thinking is that some ULXs contain neutron stars and others contain black holes, with the extra brightness coming from matter falling in at extraordinary rates, or from beams of radiation aimed in our direction.

The new observations suggest there could be a link between ULXs and the violent stellar eruptions seen in AT 2016blu, especially because many ULXs undergo large changes in X-ray brightness. If a ULX is, in some cases, a luminous blue variable with a compact companion — a system that flares in X-rays every time the companion swings close — then the variability that has puzzled astronomers for years may have a straightforward explanation: it is the same 113-day heartbeat, writ large.

SpaceNews has followed the ULX story before. Earlier this month, the James Webb Space Telescope peered at Holmberg II X-1, one of the best-studied ultra-luminous X-ray sources, and found a black hole wrapped in a glowing nebula — a system where a compact object is also feeding dramatically on its surroundings. Read that story in <a href="/blog/holmberg-ii-x-1-jwst-black-hole-nebula-november-2026/">Holmberg II X-1: Webb Sees a Black Hole Wrapped in a Glowing Nebula</a>. The AT 2016blu result adds a new member to this strange family, and a new mechanism for how such systems light up the X-ray sky.

Chandra's own history with these objects runs deep. The observatory has spent 27 years mapping the X-ray sky, from the supermassive black holes at the hearts of galaxy clusters — like the "Treble Clef" radio formation traced to a black hole outburst and a cluster collision, covered by SpaceNews in <a href="/blog/treble-clef-radio-phoenix-chandra-galaxy-clusters/">Treble Clef Radio Formation Traced to Black Hole Outburst and Cluster Collision</a> — to the hot gas of supernova remnants and the flickering of accreting compact objects. AT 2016blu is the latest reminder that the X-ray sky is not a static backdrop, but a place where things happen on human timescales: a star that erupts three times a year, every year, for fifteen years, because a dead star keeps coming back for more.

## Why it matters for the stars that really do explode

The most far-reaching implication of the discovery may be the one that reaches furthest into the future. Astronomers have long suspected that many stars change in brightness before they undergo supernova explosions — flickering, flaring, and shedding mass in the years or months before the final collapse. Understanding that pre-supernova behavior matters enormously, because it is one of the few ways to predict which stars are about to die, and because the material a star sheds before exploding shapes the supernova itself: the blast wave runs into the gas the star threw off, and the collision lights up the sky in ways that astronomers read like a forensic report.

The problem has always been that no one knew how much of that pre-explosion behavior came from the star itself, and how much came from a companion. AT 2016blu shows, concretely, that a companion can drive the whole show. The eruptions, the 113-day clock, the X-ray flares — all of it traces back to a black hole or neutron star that no telescope can see directly, betrayed only by the light of the gas it steals.

That has a direct bearing on how astronomers interpret real supernovae. When a genuine supernova goes off — like SN 2026ahvy, the recent stellar explosion in the galaxy NGC 1433 that SpaceNews covered in <a href="/blog/sn-2026ahvy-supernova-ngc-1433-2026/">SN 2026ahvy: A New Supernova Lights Up the Galaxy NGC 1433</a> — researchers work backward from the explosion to reconstruct what the star was doing in its final years. If some of those stars were in binary systems with compact companions, their pre-explosion flickering may have been driven by the same kind of periodic theft that powers AT 2016blu. The new result gives astronomers a template: a system where the mechanism is known, the period is measured, and the X-ray signature is recorded. When they see similar behavior in another star, they will know what to look for — and when to point Chandra.

## What comes next

The study leaves one big question open: is the companion a black hole or a neutron star? The X-ray data alone cannot yet tell. A neutron star is the collapsed core of a massive star, a city-sized ball of matter so dense that a teaspoon of it would weigh a billion tons. A stellar-mass black hole is what remains when an even more massive star collapses past the point of no return. Both can pull gas from a companion and heat it to X-ray brightness. Distinguishing between them will take more observations — more outbursts, more X-ray measurements, and perhaps observations at other wavelengths that can weigh the companion by its gravitational effect on the giant star.

The good news is that the system will cooperate. The 113-day clock keeps ticking, and the next outbursts are already predictable: the team knows when to look, and Chandra's Target of Opportunity program knows how to respond. Each new outburst is another chance to measure the X-ray flare in finer detail, to watch how it rises and falls, and to narrow down the nature of the hidden object.

There is also a wider hunt to begin. If AT 2016blu is the first supernova impostor found with a compact companion, it is unlikely to be the only one. Astronomers now have a recipe for finding more: look for erupting stars with suspiciously regular periods, watch them in visible light until an outburst begins, and then trigger X-ray observations at the peak. The archive may already hold candidates — stars whose outbursts were logged years ago and never explained, waiting for someone to check whether they, too, keep time.

For fifteen years, AT 2016blu has been the sky's most reliable fake — a star that dies three times a year and is always alive the next morning. Now we know why. It was never dying at all. It was being robbed, on schedule, by a dead star it cannot escape, and the light of that robbery was bright enough to look like the end of the world. The real end, when it comes, will be something else entirely — and thanks to this discovery, astronomers will be watching for it with a clearer idea of what to expect.
