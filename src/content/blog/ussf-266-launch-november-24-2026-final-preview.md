A Falcon 9 rocket stands ready at Space Launch Complex 4 East on Vandenberg Space Force Base, and if the schedule holds, Tuesday night in Jakarta will bring the third classified Space Force launch from that pad in four days. The USSF-266 mission is the quiet kind of launch that says a great deal about how the United States now puts military satellites into orbit: no livestream of the payload, no published satellite count, no post-launch deployment announcement — just a veteran booster, a four-hour window, and a classified payload heading south over the Pacific into polar orbit.

What makes USSF-266 worth watching is not only the secrecy. It is the third flight in a recognizable series, the latest chapter in a $1.6 billion rapid-acquisition experiment, and the 29th flight of a booster that has already carried astronauts, a NASA ocean-science observatory, and two previous missions in the very same series. It also caps a remarkable month for the Western Range: USSF-266 is the fifth government mission SpaceX has flown in November, and the third classified launch from Vandenberg's SLC-4E since Friday.

Liftoff is targeted for 16:08 UTC on Tuesday, November 24 — 08:08 a.m. local time in California and 23:08 WIB in Jakarta — inside a window that opens at 15:55 UTC (22:55 WIB) and runs for four hours, closing at 19:55 UTC. If the attempt slips, a backup opportunity is planned for Wednesday, November 25, at 7:46 a.m. PT. SpaceX will begin its webcast about ten minutes before liftoff, and Spaceflight Now will carry live coverage from about thirty minutes prior.

## A booster one flight from thirty

The star of the evening, in the practical sense, is the first stage. B1081 is flying for the 29th time, a figure that would have sounded like science fiction a decade ago and now barely raises an eyebrow at Vandenberg. The booster first flew on August 26, 2023, carrying NASA's Crew-7 mission to the International Space Station from Florida. Since then it has built one of the most varied resumes in the Falcon 9 fleet: the CRS-29 cargo resupply flight to the station, NASA's PACE ocean-and-atmosphere observatory, the Transporter-10 and Transporter-13 dedicated rideshares, the European EarthCARE climate satellite, the NROL-186 reconnaissance mission, the TRACERS heliophysics mission, Italy's COSMO-SkyMed Second Generation FM3 radar satellite, and no fewer than sixteen Starlink missions.

It has also flown for the Space Force before. USSF-153 was among its earlier government assignments, and its 28th flight — the one immediately before this one — was USSF-481 on October 15, the second mission in the same 'TH' series as USSF-266. In other words, the same booster that carried the second flight of this classified series is now carrying the third. Reuse is not a novelty in this programme; it is the operating model.

The numbers around B1081 put the flight in perspective. It is the seventh most-flown of the 23 active Falcon 9 boosters, behind fleet leader B1067 at 37 flights. SpaceX's booster-landing tally, if the recovery succeeds, would reach 681 landings to date, and the 236th landing on Of Course I Still Love You specifically. The droneship will be stationed roughly 574 kilometres downrange in the Pacific, and touchdown is expected about eight minutes and twenty-three seconds after liftoff. Because the landing happens far out at sea, no sonic booms from the returning booster are expected on land — although Vandenberg's launch alert notes a moderate possibility that residents of Santa Barbara, San Luis Obispo, and Ventura counties may hear one or more sonic booms during the launch itself.

Weather at the pad is a watch item rather than a threat. The forecast calls for overcast skies, a temperature of 46 degrees Fahrenheit (about 8 degrees Celsius), 95 percent cloud cover, and winds of around six miles per hour. It is a daytime launch in California, which means only observers within roughly 200 kilometres of the pad have a realistic chance of spotting the rocket in flight, with Bakersfield, Oxnard, and Santa Clarita among the better-placed cities.

## The 'TH' series: a pattern in plain sight

The Space Force will not say what USSF-266 carries, but the mission does not fly in isolation. According to Spaceflight Now, USSF-266 is the third in a series of missions carrying the 'TH' designation in public filings. The first was USSF-259, designated TH-1, which flew on September 16. The second was USSF-481, designated TH-2, which flew on October 15. Following each of those two missions, 17 satellites were added to the orbital tracking catalogue maintained by the Space Force.

That repeated number — seventeen satellites after each flight — is the most concrete public clue about the payload. Launch Library 2, the schedule database that underpins most public launch trackers, describes USSF-266 as possibly a batch of classified low Earth orbit satellites based on SpaceX's Starshield satellite bus, an assessment drawn from the locations of the ascent drop zones, which match those used by previous Space Development Agency Transport Layer launches. The same description was attached to the earlier 'TH' flights. It is an inference, not an official statement, and it should be treated as one — but it is an inference built on consistent, observable evidence across three missions.

The pattern matters because it suggests something deliberate: a steady cadence of small, classified satellite batches, launched roughly a month apart, on reused boosters, from the same pad. That is not how national-security launches used to work. The old model was a handful of large, expensive, one-off satellites on expendable rockets, scheduled years in advance. The new model looks a great deal more like the commercial Starlink machine — regular batches, standardised buses, rapid turnaround — applied to military missions.

## The $1.6 billion order behind the flight

Behind the 'TH' series sits a contract award that explains the pace. In late July 2026, the Space Force's Space Systems Command announced a $1.6 billion task order to SpaceX for 18 Falcon 9 launches on behalf of the Space Based Sensing and Targeting (SBST) portfolio. The award was made through the National Security Space Launch (NSSL) Phase 3 Lane 1 contract, a framework designed to let a wide range of launch providers bid on and receive task orders for payloads that can shoulder a higher risk tolerance than the most exquisite national-security spacecraft.

The speed of the award was, by the Space Force's own account, the point. "We have set an unprecedented two-month acquisition timeline under the Phase 3 Lane 1 contract from requirement identification to award," said Space Force Colonel Eric Zarybnisky, acting portfolio acquisition executive for Space Access at the time of the award. "This includes the one-month period for the launch service providers' proposal preparation. Ultimately, this timeline results in the speed our warfighter demands more than ever before."

Under the task order, SpaceX is expected to complete all 18 launches by the end of 2027. If the 'TH' missions are part of that order — as Spaceflight Now's reporting suggests — then USSF-266 is the third of eighteen, and the cadence of roughly one a month leaves more than a year of similar flights ahead. The NSSL Phase 3 Lane 1 structure matters here because it was built precisely for this: lower-risk-tolerance missions, commercial-style contracting, and competition among providers, in contrast to the Lane 2 track that handles the most demanding and least risk-tolerant payloads. USSF-266 is a test of whether that model can deliver classified capability at commercial speed, and so far the answer has been a launch a month without a public failure.

Since then the West Coast has become its home, and the October and November 'TH' flights are its latest assignments there.

## A November like no other at Vandenberg

USSF-266 does not arrive out of nowhere. It is the third classified launch from SLC-4E in four days, and the pace is the story of the month at Vandenberg. On Friday, November 20, USSF-317 lifted off from the same pad at 11:20:35 WIB, the sixth attempt after five scrubs, with booster B1100 landing on Of Course I Still Love You on its 11th flight. Thirty-two hours, three minutes, and twenty-one seconds later, NROL-136 followed from the same pad on Sunday, November 22, at 19:23 WIB — a pad-turnaround record — with booster B1097 landing at Landing Zone 4 on its 14th flight. Both payloads were classified; neither deployment was ever publicly confirmed.

The <a href="/blog/ussf-317-falcon-9-launch-result-november-21-2026/">USSF-317 Falcon 9 Launch Result November 21 2026: Sixth Attempt Flies as B1100 Lands Again</a> captured the first of those flights, and the <a href="/blog/nrol-136-launch-result-november-22-2026/">NROL-136 Launch Result November 22 2026: Falcon 9 Lands B1097 as Vandenberg Smashes Its Pad Record</a> covered the second. The <a href="/blog/spacex-ussf-481-classified-vandenberg-launch/">SpaceX Launches Classified USSF-481 Mission From Vandenberg as Booster B1081 Flies for the 28th Time</a> is the direct predecessor of this flight — the same booster, the same pad, the same series, five weeks earlier.

Taken together, the five government missions SpaceX has flown in November — four for the Space Force and one for the National Reconnaissance Office — amount to a demonstration of what the Western Range can now sustain. Two launches from one pad in 32 hours. Three classified launches in four days. A fourth government mission, USSF-380, is already scheduled for November 28 from Vandenberg, with a window opening at 8:39 p.m. PT and a booster on its 27th flight. The range that once measured its tempo in months now measures it in hours.

There is a second launch on Tuesday as well, on the other side of the world. Arianespace's Ariane 64 is scheduled to carry 36 Amazon Leo satellites — the LE-04 mission — from Kourou in French Guiana, with a window of 17:45–18:14 WIB, hours before USSF-266's window opens. Two different rockets, two different continents, two different customers, one evening: it is the kind of day that would once have defined a whole month of spaceflight, and now passes as a busy Tuesday.

## How to watch

SpaceX's webcast for USSF-266 begins about ten minutes before liftoff, at the mission's page on spacex.com. Spaceflight Now's live coverage starts about thirty minutes earlier. Because the payload is classified, viewers should expect the broadcast to end shortly after the booster's landing — SpaceX traditionally cuts its classified-mission webcasts after stage separation or booster recovery, and does not show the payload deploying. The most watchable moment, as ever, will be the landing: a booster returning for the 29th time, touching down on a droneship in the dark Pacific, eight minutes after leaving the pad.

For viewers in Indonesia, the targeted liftoff time of 23:08 WIB makes this one of the more civilised launches of the month to follow live — late evening rather than the small hours. The backup window on Wednesday evening, 21:46 WIB, is friendlier still, if it comes to that. Either way, the result article will follow in the morning edition.

The repetition is the point. Same pad, same rocket, same booster for the last two, the same number of catalogued satellites after each of the first two flights, and a monthly rhythm that looks less like a series of one-off launches and more like a production line. If USSF-266 follows the pattern, the Space Force's tracking catalogue should gain another batch of objects in the days after launch — and the open-source satellite-tracking community will be watching for them, just as it did after TH-1 and TH-2.

That community attention is worth noting, because it is part of how classified launches are understood at all. When the operator says nothing, the observers fill in the gaps: drop-zone notices filed with maritime authorities, the timing of stage separations, the objects that appear in the public catalogue, and the radio signals that amateur trackers pick up. None of it confirms what the satellites do. All of it confirms that something went up, that it went up on schedule, and that the system that put it there is working as designed.

## What "higher risk tolerance" actually buys

The NSSL Phase 3 Lane 1 framework deserves a closer look, because it is the contracting innovation that makes flights like USSF-266 possible at this pace. Under the older model, a national-security launch was a bespoke event: a single, often billion-dollar satellite, years of schedule, and a risk posture that treated any failure as unacceptable. Lane 1 deliberately inverts that logic for a defined class of payloads. It invites a wide range of providers to compete for task orders, accepts that individual missions carry more risk, and trades some of that caution for speed and price. The two-month timeline from requirement to award that Colonel Zarybnisky described — including a full month for providers to prepare proposals — would have been unthinkable under the old acquisition rhythm, where two years was fast.

The SBST portfolio is the first large-scale test of the idea. Eighteen launches by the end of 2027, awarded to a single provider in one order, is a bet that a proliferated sensing and targeting constellation can be built the way commercial constellations are built: in batches, on a production schedule, with the launch vehicle treated as a truck rather than a ceremony. If the 'TH' flights are indeed the first three of those eighteen, the evidence so far is encouraging — three launches in just over two months, on the same booster for the last two, with no public slip beyond the few days USSF-266 itself moved from its earlier November 18 and November 23 target dates.

There is a second, quieter benefit to the model. Because the satellites fly in batches and the constellation is designed to tolerate losses, a single launch failure — the kind of event that once grounded a programme for years — becomes a setback measured in months, not a catastrophe. That is the logic of proliferation applied to the launch manifest itself: no single flight carries the whole mission, so no single flight can lose it.

## Why it matters

USSF-266 will not make headlines the way a crewed launch or a Mars landing does, and that is precisely the measure of how far the launch business has come. A classified military satellite batch, flown on a booster making its 29th flight, from a pad that launched twice in 32 hours two days earlier, is now routine enough to be the third item on a single day's launch schedule. The remarkable thing is not that it is happening. The remarkable thing is how ordinary it has become.

But ordinary does not mean unimportant. The 'TH' series, the $1.6 billion SBST task order, the 18 launches due by the end of 2027, and the NSSL Phase 3 Lane 1 framework behind them all point in the same direction: the United States is rebuilding its military space architecture as a proliferated constellation of many smaller satellites, launched often and cheaply, rather than a small number of large, irreplaceable ones. That architecture is designed to be harder to disable, faster to replenish, and cheaper to expand — and its launch cadence is the visible evidence that the design is being executed, not merely announced.

Tonight's flight is one data point in that programme, and a modest one. If B1081 lands for the 29th time and the catalogue gains another batch of satellites in the coming days, it will confirm that the machine is working. If it slips to Wednesday, it will confirm something else that the last week has already shown: that Vandenberg's range, its pads, and its boosters can absorb a delay and try again the next day without missing a beat. Either way, the result will be in the morning edition — and the next 'TH' flight, whenever it comes, will probably not be far behind.
