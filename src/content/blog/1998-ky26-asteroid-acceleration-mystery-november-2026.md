Somewhere in the inner Solar System, about 299 million kilometres from Earth, a rock the size of a school bus is doing something it should not be able to do. Asteroid 1998 KY26 is accelerating. Not falling toward the Sun, not being tugged by a planet — accelerating sideways, in a direction no gravitational force can explain, as if some invisible engine were quietly pushing it along. Astronomers have tracked the anomaly for more than twenty-five years, and for most of that time they had a tidy explanation ready: the little world was a "dark comet", a rocky body with buried ice that vented gas as it warmed, nudging itself forward without ever growing the glowing tail of an ordinary comet.

That explanation has now been tested — and, according to a new peer-reviewed study, it fails. In November 2026, aerospace engineers at the Georgia Institute of Technology published detailed thermal simulations showing that 1998 KY26 is far too warm to hold on to the ice its acceleration would require. The result does not solve the mystery. It sharpens it. With the most natural answer weakened, researchers are left weighing two very different alternatives: a subtle push from sunlight and heat, or — in a theory that sounds like science fiction and is being argued seriously in the scientific literature — the possibility that the object is not an asteroid at all, but a spacecraft humanity lost nearly forty years ago.

The stakes are modest in the practical sense. NASA's administrator has said plainly that the agency sees no evidence the object is a threat to Earth, or anything other than an asteroid, and that it is being monitored with no reason for alarm. But the scientific stakes are real. Whatever is pushing 1998 KY26 is a force that acts on small bodies throughout the Solar System, and understanding it matters for predicting where asteroids will be decades from now. And in July 2031, Japan's Hayabusa2 spacecraft is scheduled to arrive at the object and look at it up close — an encounter that should finally settle what it is.

## A push that gravity cannot explain

Asteroids are supposed to be predictable. Once astronomers have measured a rock's orbit, Newton's laws and Einstein's refinements should tell them where it will be next year, next decade, next century, with only the gentle tugs of the Sun and the planets to account for. Comets are the exception: as they approach the Sun, their ice turns to gas, jets of vapour stream away, and the recoil pushes them off their purely gravitational paths. That "nongravitational acceleration" is expected for comets, and it is measurable. For an asteroid — a dry, rocky body with no ice to vent — it should not happen at all.

1998 KY26 breaks that rule. Long-term telescopic tracking compiled over more than twenty-five years shows the object drifting from the path gravity alone would predict. The extra push acts perpendicular to the plane of its orbit, tilting its motion in a way that the Sun and the planets cannot produce. The effect is small — the kind of deviation that only becomes obvious after years of precise measurement — but it is real, and it has survived every attempt to explain it away as a measurement error.

The object itself is unremarkable at first glance, and that is part of what makes it interesting. It measures roughly 11 metres across, about 36 feet, making it one of the smallest near-Earth objects astronomers track in any detail. It spins astonishingly fast, completing a full rotation every 5.35 minutes, which places it among the fastest-spinning asteroids ever discovered. A body that small, spinning that fast, is already near the limit of what a loose pile of rubble can survive without flying apart, which suggests it is a fairly solid chunk of rock rather than a loosely bound rubble pile. It was first spotted by telescopes in 1998, which is where its name comes from, and it has been watched on and off ever since — long enough for its slow, unexplained drift to emerge clearly from the data.

That drift is what turned an obscure catalogue entry into a scientific puzzle. If the acceleration is real, something is pushing the asteroid. The question that has occupied researchers ever since is what that something could be, when the object shows no coma, no tail, and no visible sign of the outgassing that normally explains such a push.

## The dark comet idea

The leading explanation, until this month, was that 1998 KY26 is a dark comet. The idea sounds contradictory, but it describes a real and increasingly recognised class of objects. A dark comet is a body that looks like an asteroid — small, rocky, no visible tail — but behaves partly like a comet, showing nongravitational acceleration driven by gas escaping from ice buried beneath its surface. The outgassing is too weak, or too deeply buried, to form the glowing cloud and tail that make ordinary comets obvious. The thrust is still there, however, quietly steering the body along a path that gravity alone cannot account for.

It was an attractive theory for 1998 KY26 because it explained the key observation without requiring anything exotic. The asteroid's acceleration is roughly the size and direction that a small amount of escaping water vapour could produce. Its orbit brings it close enough to the Sun for buried ice to warm and sublimate. And dark comets are now thought to be more common than astronomers once believed, with several candidates identified in recent years among the near-Earth population. If 1998 KY26 was one of them, its mystery was simply a matter of hidden ice doing hidden work.

The theory also carried a satisfying implication. If a dark comet could hide its ice so completely, then the line between asteroids and comets is blurrier than the textbooks suggest, and the population of icy bodies in the inner Solar System is larger than the visible comets alone would indicate. That matters for planetary science, because it changes estimates of how much water and volatile material was delivered to the early Earth, and for planetary defence, because an object that can quietly change its own orbit is harder to predict than one that follows gravity alone.

But the dark comet idea had a weakness from the start: it rested on an assumption nobody had tested. For the theory to work, water ice had to survive inside 1998 KY26 for a long time — long enough to still be venting gas today, after billions of years in the inner Solar System. Nobody had modelled the object's temperatures in detail to check whether that was actually possible. In November 2026, two engineers at Georgia Tech did exactly that.

## Too warm for ice

Korbin Hansen and Masatoshi Hirabayashi, aerospace engineers at the Georgia Institute of Technology, built a detailed thermal model of 1998 KY26 using its known physical characteristics: its size, its orbit, its reflectivity, and plausible assumptions about what it is made of. They ran the model thirteen times, varying the uncertain parameters, to see whether any plausible version of the asteroid could stay cold enough inside to preserve buried water ice over long periods.

The answer, in every run, was no. For buried water ice to survive over extended periods, temperatures need to remain below about 145 kelvin, or minus 128 degrees Celsius. But the simulations found that even the coldest regions of 1998 KY26 average at least 212 kelvin — about minus 61 degrees Celsius — over the course of its orbit around the Sun. No point on its surface, and no point in its interior, stayed cold enough to preserve ice in the long term. The object is, in the researchers' words, simply too warm.

The numbers make the problem concrete. Even if water ice had made up a generous one percent of the asteroid's total mass, the researchers calculated that the entire reservoir would disappear in just over 2,000 years at those temperatures. And the timescale becomes far shorter if escaping water vapour must also account for the observed acceleration: in that scenario, the outgassing needed to push the asteroid would exhaust the available ice in approximately 62 years. For a body that may be billions of years old, 62 years is nothing — a blink. The ice, if it ever existed, would be long gone.

"Our temperature model results indicate that the water outgassing rate on 1998 KY26 would be too high to maintain buried ice," Hansen and Hirabayashi wrote in their paper, published in the journal *Icarus* under the title "Thermally unfavorable conditions of volatile existence in near-Earth asteroid 1998 KY26, the Hayabusa extended mission's target". Their conclusion is blunt: the object is a dehydrated asteroid that lost whatever water it once had in the distant past, and thrust from sublimating ice cannot be responsible for its acceleration.

There is one caveat, and the authors state it plainly. Nobody knows how long 1998 KY26 has occupied its current orbit. If it arrived in the inner Solar System only recently — in cosmic terms — it might still carry some ice despite the hostile temperatures. The study narrows that possibility rather than closing it entirely. But combined with an earlier study that traced the object's reflected light to a composition consistent with a rocky asteroid likely originating in the inner part of the main asteroid belt, the case for a dry, rocky body is now considerably stronger than the case for a hidden comet.

## What could push it instead

If ice is out, what is left? The researchers themselves point to the most likely answer: sunlight. Two related effects, both well established in asteroid science, can push a small body without any gas at all.

The first is solar radiation pressure — the faint but relentless push of sunlight itself. Photons carry momentum, and when they strike an object and bounce off or are absorbed, they impart a tiny force. For a planet, the effect is negligible. For an object only 11 metres across, with a large surface area relative to its mass, it is not. Over years and decades, that gentle pressure adds up to a measurable change in trajectory, and its direction depends on the object's shape and reflectivity — which could explain why the push on 1998 KY26 acts perpendicular to its orbital plane.

The second is the Yarkovsky effect, a subtler thermal force. As an asteroid rotates, the Sun heats one side; that side later radiates the heat away as infrared light, and the recoil from that thermal radiation produces a small but steady thrust. For a fast spinner like 1998 KY26, rotating once every 5.35 minutes, the heating pattern is complex, but the principle holds: uneven heating means uneven radiation, and uneven radiation means a net push. The Georgia Tech team concluded that these thermal radiation forces are now the strongest explanation for the object's strange motion, and further modelling of exactly how sunlight and heat act on its particular shape and spin is the natural next step.

Both explanations are, in a sense, less dramatic than a dark comet — no hidden ice, no secret jets, just light and heat doing slow work over decades. But they are also harder to confirm from a distance, because they depend on details nobody can measure from Earth: the object's exact shape, its surface texture, how its reflectivity varies from one face to another. Those details are precisely what a visiting spacecraft can measure, which is why the coming rendezvous matters so much.

## The lost spacecraft theory

There is, however, a second explanation in play — one that the Georgia Tech study does not address, and that has nothing to do with ice or sunlight. A separate team of researchers has proposed that 1998 KY26 might not be an asteroid at all. It might be Phobos 1, a Soviet spacecraft launched toward Mars in 1988 that fell silent after a faulty command shut it down, and has been missing ever since.

The idea comes from Harvard astrophysicist Avi Loeb and researchers Adam Hibberd and Adam Crowl, who compared a simplified model of the lost spacecraft with observations of 1998 KY26. Their analysis, published this year in the journal *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, found that the model's pattern of reflected sunlight closely matched eight sets of observations of the object. More strikingly, they reported that sunlight acting on a spacecraft-shaped body — a cylinder with solar panels, rather than a roughly spherical rock — could better fit hundreds of telescope and radar observations of 1998 KY26 than a natural asteroid shape could. A spacecraft, in other words, would be pushed by sunlight in just the way this object is being pushed, because its flat panels and cylindrical body catch light differently from a tumbling rock.

It is a startling claim, and the authors are careful about how far it goes. They emphasised that their work does not establish that the object is Phobos 1, describing the possibility as "strongly plausible but presently unproven", and noting that their investigation cannot provide a definitive identification. The match is suggestive, not conclusive: many shapes can produce similar reflections, and the data are consistent with more than one interpretation.

The counter-evidence is also substantial. The same year, a study in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* examined the light reflected by 1998 KY26 and found that its apparent composition is consistent with a type of rocky asteroid, concluding that it likely originated in the inner part of the asteroid belt. A spacecraft built of metal and solar panels would not be expected to reflect light like a stony asteroid. And NASA's administrator, Jared Isaacman, has said the agency has no evidence that the object is a threat to Earth or anything other than an asteroid, adding that NASA is monitoring it but sees no reason for alarm.

The two pictures — rocky asteroid from the inner belt, or lost Soviet hardware — cannot both be right, and at present neither can be ruled out from Earth. That is what makes the object such a compelling target: it is small enough, and close enough, and strange enough that a spacecraft visit could settle the question outright. A camera that can see the object will know immediately whether it is looking at a cratered rock or at a cylinder with solar panels.

## The long road to 1998 KY26

- **1988** — The Soviet Union launches Phobos 1 toward Mars. A faulty command later shuts the unmanned spacecraft down, and contact is lost. The spacecraft is never found.
- **1998** — Telescopes first spot a small near-Earth object later designated 1998 KY26. It measures about 11 metres across and spins once every 5.35 minutes.
- **1998–2026** — More than twenty-five years of telescopic tracking reveal that the object is accelerating in a way gravity alone cannot explain, with the extra push acting perpendicular to its orbital plane.
- **2025** — A study of the light reflected by 1998 KY26 finds its composition consistent with a rocky asteroid, likely from the inner main asteroid belt. A shape model of the object is also published.
- **November 2026** — Georgia Tech researchers publish thermal simulations in *Icarus* showing the object is too warm to retain buried ice, challenging the dark comet explanation. Separately, the Phobos 1 hypothesis is debated in the scientific literature and in the press.
- **2028** — 1998 KY26 is projected to pass about 32 million miles from Earth, its closest approach in the near term.
- **July 2031** — Japan's Hayabusa2 spacecraft is scheduled to rendezvous with 1998 KY26, ending years of speculation with direct close-up observations.
- **2039** — The object is projected to draw closer still, to about 10 million miles from Earth.

## Why the answer matters

It is fair to ask why a rock 11 metres wide, hundreds of millions of kilometres away, deserves this much attention. Part of the answer is that 1998 KY26 is a test case for a much larger problem. Astronomers track tens of thousands of near-Earth objects, and predicting their orbits decades ahead is the foundation of planetary defence. If small bodies can be pushed by forces that are hard to model from Earth — sunlight on an unusual shape, heat radiating unevenly from a fast spinner — then long-term predictions carry uncertainties that must be understood, not assumed away. Every object like 1998 KY26 that is studied in detail makes the predictions for all the others a little more reliable.

The object also matters because of what it may reveal about the population of dark comets. If 1998 KY26, once a leading dark comet candidate, turns out to be a dry rock pushed by sunlight, then the dark comet category shrinks, and astronomers must be more careful about which accelerating objects they assign to it. If it turns out to be something else entirely, the implications run in the opposite direction. Either way, the answer refines a classification that is still new and still contested.

And then there is Hayabusa2 itself. The Japanese spacecraft has already visited the asteroid Ryugu and returned samples to Earth, and its extended mission has carried it onward through the Solar System. Its <a href="/blog/hayabusa2-torifune-flyby/">flyby of asteroid Torifune</a> showed what the spacecraft can do at close range, threading past a small world at five kilometres per second and revealing it to be a two-lobed contact binary. The rendezvous with 1998 KY26 in July 2031 will be a different kind of encounter — a slow approach and a long stay, rather than a high-speed flyby — and it will give scientists their first direct look at the surface, the shape, and the composition of an object that has resisted explanation from afar.

That context is also why the parallel story of asteroid exploration matters. While Hayabusa2 cruises toward its target, ESA's Hera spacecraft is already conducting close-up science at another asteroid system, as seen in the <a href="/blog/hera-didymos-close-up-60-km-november-2026/">Hera Didymos close-up 60 km image</a> and the <a href="/blog/hera-first-image-didymos-dimorphos-2026/">Hera first image of Didymos and Dimorphos</a>. Hera is studying the crater left by NASA's DART impact, a mission that deliberately changed an asteroid's orbit. Together, these missions are building a new kind of planetary science: not just observing asteroids from afar, but visiting them, touching them, and in DART's case, shoving them — and learning, each time, how small bodies actually behave when forces act on them.

## What happens next

For now, the mystery remains open, and the next few years will be a waiting game punctuated by opportunities. In 2028, 1998 KY26 will pass about 32 million miles from Earth — close enough, in astronomical terms, for telescopes to refine their measurements of its motion and its reflected light. A further approach in 2039 will bring it to about 10 million miles. Each pass is a chance to test the competing explanations against fresh data, and to narrow the range of shapes and surfaces that could produce the observed acceleration.

The decisive moment, however, is already scheduled. In July 2031, Hayabusa2 will rendezvous with 1998 KY26 and examine it at close range, measuring its shape, its surface, and its composition directly. If the object is a rocky asteroid, the spacecraft's cameras and instruments will show a cratered, irregular body, and the thermal measurements will confirm whether sunlight and the Yarkovsky effect can account for its motion. If it is Phobos 1, the spacecraft will find something no natural process can produce: flat panels, straight edges, and the unmistakable geometry of human engineering, drifting silently through space since 1988.

Either outcome would be remarkable. A dehydrated asteroid pushed by sunlight would confirm that even the smallest bodies in the Solar System are subject to forces that astronomers are only beginning to model properly — and it would close the book on one of the most persistent small-body mysteries of the last quarter-century. A lost spacecraft, found again after forty-three years, would be one of the strangest rediscoveries in the history of spaceflight, and a reminder that the Solar System still holds objects humanity launched and then forgot.

Until then, the little rock keeps moving — a school-bus-sized world, spinning once every five minutes, accelerating gently in a direction no one can fully explain, carrying its secret with it as it circles the Sun. The explanation, whatever it turns out to be, is already on its way to meet it.
