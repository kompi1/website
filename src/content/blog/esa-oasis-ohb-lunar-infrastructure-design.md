BREMEN — Every spacecraft Europe has ever sent to the Moon has been an island: a self-contained machine carrying its own power, its own radio, its own brain, expected to do its science and die. On October 19, 2026, the European Space Agency took a formal step toward a different model — one in which the Moon gets infrastructure first, and missions simply plug in.

ESA has awarded OHB System AG, the Bremen-based subsidiary of the OHB SE space and technology group, the feasibility and preliminary design studies for OASIS — the Outpost for Advanced Scientific Investigation Station. The concept: a shared surface installation that would provide core services — power supply, communications, and data storage — to multiple payloads at once, and keep them alive through the lunar night, which lasts roughly two Earth weeks.

"With OASIS, we are taking lunar exploration a step further," said Marco Fuchs, Chief Executive Officer of OHB SE, in the announcement. "Europe is not only looking at individual missions to the Moon, but at the infrastructure that will enable long-term scientific research and technological innovation in the first place."

And then, in the line that may matter most: "With OASIS, we are creating the conditions that will allow science and technology activities to be conducted on the lunar surface sustainably and beyond the scope of individual missions."

## A utility company for the lunar surface

The idea behind OASIS is deliberately terrestrial. On Earth, a research station does not ask every scientist to bring their own power plant, satellite dish, and hard drives. It offers shared infrastructure — electricity, connectivity, storage — as central services, so researchers can focus on the research. OASIS applies that same logic to the Moon.

Under the concept, future lunar payloads would no longer need to develop and transport their own power, communications, and data systems for every mission. Instead, they would draw on a common platform that handles those chores centrally. Energy generated during the roughly two-week lunar day would be stored to sustain payload operations through the roughly two-week-long lunar night — one of the most brutal engineering constraints on the Moon, where temperatures swing hundreds of degrees between sunlight and shadow.

By sharing central infrastructure, the announcement notes, the complexity, mass, and cost of future lunar missions could be significantly reduced. That is the economic engine of the whole idea: every kilogram a lander doesn't have to dedicate to batteries and radios is a kilogram it can dedicate to science — or a kilogram of launch mass Europe doesn't have to pay to fly.

For now, the award covers studies — feasibility and preliminary design — not hardware. But the direction of travel is unmistakable: ESA is no longer only asking how Europe gets *to* the Moon. It is asking what Europe does once it stays.

## Why the Moon is a terrible place to be an island

The logic of OASIS becomes clear the moment you tally what a standalone lunar mission must survive on its own.

A lunar day is not 24 hours. Because the Moon rotates so slowly, one full day-night cycle takes about 29.5 Earth days — roughly two weeks of continuous sunlight followed by roughly two weeks of total darkness. During the night, temperatures at the lunar equator can plunge toward minus 170 degrees Celsius; surviving that demands either nuclear power or enormous batteries, both of which are heavy, expensive, and politically complicated. Many landers simply accept the alternative: they do their work during the day and freeze to death when the Sun goes down.

Communications are just as punishing. The Moon has no atmosphere to bounce signals through, no cell towers, and on the far side no line of sight to Earth at all. A mission must either carry a powerful direct-to-Earth radio and point it home, or relay through an orbiter — and most of today's orbiters were designed for someone else's mission, not yours.

Data storage and relay compound the problem: a lander that generates more data than it can transmit during its brief contact windows has to carry the memory and the power to hold it until the next pass.

OASIS attacks all of these at once. A shared power system can be sized for a whole site rather than one payload, with storage amortized across many users. A shared communications hub can carry the big antenna and the relay equipment, freeing individual payloads to bring modest radios. A shared data store can buffer measurements until Earth is reachable. The payload designers — the people who actually want to drill, measure, photograph, and experiment — can stop re-engineering survival and start engineering science.

## One piece of a much larger European lunar machine

OASIS did not arrive in a vacuum. It lands inside the most aggressive lunar build-out in European history — a layered program that is taking shape mission by mission.

**Moonlight** is the orbital half of the puzzle. ESA's planned lunar communications and navigation service is designed as a scalable five-satellite solution — one communications satellite and four navigation satellites on highly elliptical orbits — offering continuous connectivity and navigation for the lunar south pole, surface positioning precision of up to three metres, and bandwidth sufficient for smooth Earth-Moon video calls. ESA frames the stakes in stark numbers: as many as 400 missions are scheduled to launch for the Moon in the next decade, many of them launched by, or in partnership with, ESA. The Lunar Pathfinder project is already meant to provide initial communications services to early missions, including a navigation payload demonstrator that would allow positioning in lunar orbit using GPS and Galileo systems for the first time, with a launch from 2025 onward.

**Argonaut** is the surface-delivery half. ESA has signed a contract with Thales Alenia Space in Italy to lead a European consortium of aerospace industries in building the Argonaut Lunar Descent Element — ESA's first lunar lander, and a cornerstone of the agency's lunar exploration strategy. The rest of the core team includes Thales Alenia Space in the United Kingdom and France, plus OHB — the same company now tasked with OASIS. From the start of the next decade, Argonaut missions are expected to launch regularly, delivering infrastructure, scientific instruments, rovers, technology demonstrators, and vital resources for astronauts on the surface, such as food, water, and air. The lander is designed to survive the harsh lunar night and days for five years, and to work seamlessly with ESA's Lunar Link on the Gateway and the Moonlight communication and navigation systems. The consortium is expected to deliver the Lunar Descent Element in 2030 for the first operational mission, ArgoNET, expected in 2031.

"The Argonaut contract signature is a pivotal moment for Europe's lunar exploration ambitions," said Daniel Neuenschwander, ESA's Director for Human and Robotic Exploration. "Argonaut will enable Europe to contribute meaningfully to international partnerships, while paving the way for a sustainable human presence on the Moon. Europe is on its journey to the Moon and has broken the ground towards European autonomy in Exploration."

**Gateway** is the orbital staging point. With ESA and other partners, NASA intends to build the lunar Gateway — an outpost in orbit around the Moon that will serve as the staging point for both robotic and crewed exploration of the lunar south pole. ESA is supplying habitat and refuelling elements plus a communications module that will pave the way for Moonlight.

And underneath all of it, Europe is already flying: every future Artemis lunar mission will include an ESA European Service Module — the propulsion and life-support backbone of NASA's Orion spacecraft.

Read as a system, the European lunar strategy has a shape: Argonaut lands the mass, Gateway stages the crews, Moonlight connects them, and OASIS — the newest addition — powers and serves the science once it is all on the ground. It is infrastructure thinking from orbit to surface.

## OHB is betting the company on the Moon's plumbing

For OHB, the OASIS studies are not a one-off contract. They are the latest move in a sustained campaign to make the Bremen group Europe's prime contractor for lunar infrastructure — the plumbing of the new lunar economy.

The company recently founded the European Moonport Company to drive that agenda, developing with Munich Airport International an initial concept for a central launch and landing facility on the lunar surface — a Moonport that would serve as a reliable hub for logistics flights and crewed spacecraft, and a key enabler for a future lunar economic sphere. Establishing infrastructure on the Moon, the company argues, lays the foundation for that economic sphere, and it requires not only independent access to the Moon but key technologies for launch and landing pads, energy, oxygen and propellant supply systems, and navigation and communication capabilities.

"With the founding of the European Moonport Company, we are supporting Europe's ambition to establish a long-term presence on the Moon under its own power and to create the foundation for future scientific missions and economic activities," Fuchs said at the time. "For us as a company, the Moon has always been a fascinating destination. This long-standing enthusiasm is now becoming tangible: We aim not only to accompany Europe's journey to the Moon, but to actively help shape it."

That message resonates with ESA's own stated position: "For ESA, it is essential that Europe develops the capabilities to be permanently, sustainably, and independently present on the Moon — as a reliable and active partner in an international lunar architecture."

OHB's lunar résumé runs deeper than press releases. A team led by OHB Italia has worked on the definition of a technology demonstrator payload for in-situ resource utilization on the Moon — a plant, carried on a lander, that would use a robotic arm to collect lunar regolith and chemically process it at temperatures around 900 degrees Celsius to extract oxygen, producing water or oxygen from non-Earth materials for the first time. The same group is also inside the Argonaut consortium, sits on ESA's European-led orbital outpost studies, and builds hardware across Earth observation, communications, and launch services.

The timing is not accidental. On September 29, 2026, ESA awarded OHB — alongside a consortium led by Airbus Defence and Space — parallel preliminary studies for a future European-led outpost in low Earth orbit, assessing the technical and programmatic feasibility of an infrastructure capable of supporting human spaceflight, scientific research, and technology development. Those studies, initiated in June 2026 under ESA's European Exploration Envelope Programme, run for about nine months. Together with OASIS, they position OHB at the center of both of ESA's great infrastructure bets of the decade: the post-ISS orbital future and the permanent lunar surface.

## The two-week night that decides everything

To appreciate why OASIS's headline feature — surviving the lunar night — matters so much, consider what the night actually demands.

Solar panels, the cheapest and most proven power source in space, produce nothing for two weeks. Radioisotope heaters and generators work beautifully but draw political and financial costs. Batteries capable of carrying a payload through 14 days of darkness at cryogenic temperatures are massive. So the default outcome for most lunar missions has been the same: land in the morning, work furiously for two weeks, and accept that the night will probably kill you.

That is acceptable for a technology demonstrator. It is fatal for a scientific program. Geology doesn't pause for the dark. A seismic network, a radiation monitor, a regolith chemistry lab, a telescope — any instrument that wants continuous data — needs power it cannot generate itself. So does any commercial customer: resource prospectors, communications operators, and the eventual crews of a surface base all need the night handled.

OASIS is Europe's answer to that gap: generate during the day, store at scale, and meter it out to everyone on site through the night. Do it once, do it well, and let every payload — ESA's, a member state's, a university's, a startup's — buy into the service instead of solving the night alone.

## Shared stations are how humans explore — on Earth, anyway

There is a precedent for what Europe is attempting, and it sits at the bottom of the Earth.

Antarctica's research stations — McMurdo, Concordia, Halley — operate on exactly the OASIS model. The station provides heat, power, communications, data links, logistics, and survival support as shared services. The scientists bring experiments. Nobody asks a glaciologist to also be a power-plant engineer.

The International Space Station did the same in orbit: European experiments aboard Columbus plug into the station's power, thermal control, and data buses. Nobody ships a battery bank to the ISS. The European Service Modules now flying on NASA's Artemis missions are infrastructure in the same sense — a standardized propulsion and life-support unit that any Orion capsule can rely on.

OASIS is the extension of that philosophy to the lunar surface, and its ambition is the same as Antarctica's: turn an expedition into an occupation. Missions visit; stations persist.

## What happens next

Feasibility and preliminary design studies are the beginning of a long road, not the end of one. The studies OHB System AG has won will define what OASIS could look like, how it would be built, and what it would cost — the groundwork on which any future development decision rests. ESA has not announced a deployment timeline, and the history of space infrastructure is full of brilliant concepts that never left the study phase.

The industrial base behind the bid is not betting on paper alone. The announcement, issued from Bremen on the morning of October 19, 2026, comes from a company embedded in a region that has made space a state economic strategy: Bavaria's leadership describes the state as a "Space Valley," generating roughly 12 billion euros in aerospace revenue and employing some 38,000 people, with the state government planning to invest over one billion euros in aerospace alone by 2030 — universities, start-up support, and settlement incentives included — under its Hightech Agenda Bavaria. OHB's Moonport concept work with Munich Airport International grew out of exactly that ecosystem. Studies like OASIS are how industrial strategy converts into flight hardware: first the feasibility contract, then the design, then — if the ministers agree — the metal.

But the context around OASIS makes it harder to dismiss as paper engineering. Argonaut has an industrial consortium and a delivery date. Moonlight has an architecture and a market. Gateway has hardware under construction. The European Moonport Company exists. And the orbital-outpost studies awarded three weeks earlier show ESA is running the same infrastructure playbook in low Earth orbit simultaneously.

Seen from Bremen today, the pattern is the point. Europe spent half a century learning to be an indispensable partner — building the service module that powers Orion, the laboratory that anchors the ISS, the navigation constellation that guides the continent. OASIS is what comes after partnership: the first piece of ground Europe would own, not just visit, on another world.

The Moon is about to get its first utilities. Europe wants to be the one that built them.
