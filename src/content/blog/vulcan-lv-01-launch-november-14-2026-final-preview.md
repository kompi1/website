United Launch Alliance has one more night to wait. After a scrub on Friday morning stopped the countdown a little more than four hours before liftoff, the company's Vulcan rocket is now set to make its second attempt at returning to flight on Saturday afternoon in Florida — which means just after midnight on Sunday for readers in Indonesia. The stakes could hardly be higher: 40 Amazon Leo broadband satellites are stacked on top of the most powerful Vulcan ever flown, a nine-month grounding hangs over the vehicle, and the United States Space Force is watching every second of the flight before it decides whether the rocket can carry national security payloads again.

The mission is Amazon Leo LV-01, known in ULA's naming as Vulcan Leo 1. It is the first of 38 Vulcan launches Amazon has booked to build its satellite internet constellation, and it carries the largest batch of Leo satellites ever sent up at once. If the flight goes as planned, the constellation will grow to 432 satellites in orbit, and ULA will finally be able to start the rapid launch cadence it has been promising for years. If it does not, the questions that have dogged Vulcan since February will only get louder.

## The New Target: Saturday in Florida, Sunday After Midnight in Jakarta

Liftoff is now scheduled for 12:32:30 p.m. Eastern Standard Time on Saturday, November 14, from Space Launch Complex 41 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. That is 17:32:30 UTC, and 00:32:30 WIB on Sunday, November 15, in Jakarta. The launch window lasts 29 minutes and closes at 1:01 p.m. EST, or 01:01 WIB. The rocket will fly a northeasterly trajectory, climbing out over the Atlantic before its Centaur upper stage takes the satellites the rest of the way to orbit.

The new time is about 22 minutes earlier than Friday's original target of 12:54 p.m. EST, a small shift that reflects the way launch windows move with the orbit the satellites need to reach. What matters is not the clock but the fact that the attempt is happening at all, barely a day after the scrub. ULA said the rocket and all 40 satellites remained healthy after Friday's halt, and the team turned the mission around in a single day — a pace that would have been unthinkable for the company's older rockets and a pace Amazon needs ULA to sustain, because 37 more Vulcan launches are waiting behind this one.

For viewers, the timing is almost ideal. The United States Space Force's 45th Weather Squadron is forecasting a zero percent chance of rain at the launch site on Saturday, with winds of 10 to 15 knots — about as clean a forecast as a Florida launch ever gets. ULA has published a visibility map showing the ascent should be visible across much of Florida and up the East Coast of the United States, and the midday timing means the rocket's climb will be easy to follow with the naked eye from the Space Coast. Florida Today will run live coverage from about 90 minutes before liftoff, and ULA's own webcast begins about 20 minutes before the countdown reaches zero. Spaceflight Now will carry live coverage starting 30 minutes before liftoff.

The satellites themselves will not reach their working positions for some time. After two burns of the Centaur upper stage, the 40 spacecraft are to deploy in 21 separate events over about 25 minutes and 40 seconds, with the first release coming roughly 1 hour and 17 minutes after liftoff — between about 01:49 and 02:15 WIB on Sunday morning. They will be released into a circular parking orbit 450 kilometres above Earth, inclined 51.9 degrees to the equator, and will then raise themselves to their operational altitude. Centaur will coast for another 219 seconds after the last deployment and then fire its engine once more for a deorbit burn, nearly two hours after liftoff, so that the spent stage does not stay in orbit as debris.

## What Friday's Scrub Really Was

Friday's attempt never got close to the pad's final minutes. ULA announced the delay at 8:41 a.m. EST, a little more than four hours before the scheduled liftoff, while crews were already loading liquid oxygen into the Centaur upper stage. The cause was not the rocket at all. A power interruption at the Air Liquide gaseous nitrogen plant cut power to the facility's high-pressure pumps, and the plant could not keep supplying the nitrogen the launch complex needs. ULA described the problem as a commodity flow issue with a supplier, and both the rocket and the spacecraft stayed healthy through the halt.

That distinction matters, because the nitrogen plant is not part of Vulcan. Gaseous nitrogen is used across the launch complex to purge lines and tanks, keeping oxygen out of places where it does not belong and keeping propellant systems safe while they are loaded. Lose the nitrogen supply in the middle of fuelling, and the safe choice is to stop. The rocket did nothing wrong on Friday, and nothing about the scrub suggests a problem with the vehicle. It was, in the plainest sense, a ground-support failure — the kind of mundane infrastructure problem that has scrubbed rockets since the beginning of the space age, and the kind that is usually fixed in hours rather than weeks.

The speed of the turnaround is part of the story. Within a day of the scrub, ULA had a new target, a new window, and a forecast with no rain in it. That is the tempo Amazon is paying for. The company has built a dedicated integration facility and a new mobile launch platform at Cape Canaveral precisely so that Vulcan launches can be turned around in days or weeks rather than months, and LV-01 is the first mission to use that new infrastructure end to end.

Readers who want the full account of the scrubbed attempt can find it in our earlier report, <a href="/blog/vulcan-leo-1-scrubbed-november-13/">Vulcan Leo 1 Scrubbed November 13: Nitrogen Plant Failure Delays ULA's Six-Booster Comeback to Saturday</a>, and the countdown that led up to it is covered in <a href="/blog/vulcan-leo-1-launch-countdown-november-13/">Vulcan Leo 1 Launch Countdown November 13: ULA's Six-Booster Comeback Faces Its Final Hours</a>.

## The Rocket: Vulcan at Full Strength

LV-01 flies in the configuration ULA calls VC6L — the most powerful version of Vulcan ever assembled. The letters say it all: V for Vulcan, C for the Centaur upper stage, 6 for six solid rocket boosters, and L for the long, extended payload fairing that swallows the stack of 40 satellites. The rocket stands 65 metres tall, about 215 feet, and can carry up to 27,200 kilograms to low Earth orbit. At liftoff, its two Blue Origin BE-4 main engines and six Northrop Grumman GEM-63XL solid boosters will generate 17.2 meganewtons of thrust — roughly the push of 17 million newtons, or the weight of about 1,750 tonnes pressing the vehicle skyward.

The BE-4 engines burn liquid oxygen and methane, a combination chosen for performance and for the way it leaves almost no soot in the engine, which is meant to make the engines easier to reuse one day. The GEM-63XL boosters are stretched versions of the GEM-63 motors that flew successfully on ULA's Delta IV Medium and Atlas V rockets, and they are the largest monolithic solid rocket motors ever flown. Each one is a single, enormous tube of propellant with a nozzle at the bottom, and together the six of them provide most of the thrust that gets Vulcan off the pad.

There is also a quieter first on this flight. LV-01 is the first mission to fly a shorter, optimised version of ULA's Centaur V upper stage, tailored for missions to low Earth orbit. The standard Centaur V is a long, wide stage built for heavy payloads headed far from Earth; the shorter variant trims mass the mission does not need, which lets the rocket carry more satellites to low orbit. Amazon says the first several Leo-Vulcan missions will each debut new flight hardware as the vehicle matures, and the satellite count will grow as the upgrades add performance. The first missions carry 40 satellites each; later ones are already being planned with larger configurations.

## The Payload: 40 Satellites and a Constellation on a Deadline

Amazon Leo — the satellite internet network formerly known as Project Kuiper, renamed in November 2025 — is Amazon's answer to SpaceX's Starlink: thousands of satellites in low Earth orbit beaming broadband to small antennas on the ground. The first-generation network is designed for up to 3,236 satellites, and the company says it needs 578 of them in a circular orbit at 630 kilometres altitude before it can start initial regional service. LV-01 is a significant step toward that number. As of early November, 368 Leo satellites were in their operational orbit, with six having reentered the atmosphere. Once the 40 new satellites are deployed, the constellation will number 432 spacecraft in orbit.

The scale of the production behind those numbers is easy to miss. Amazon completed its 1,000th satellite in early September, a milestone that makes Leo one of the largest satellite manufacturing operations in the world, and the program now has hundreds of flight-ready satellites waiting for rockets. Nearly 400 satellites are already in orbit, delivered on 15 launches by Atlas V, Falcon 9, and Ariane 6. Vulcan is Amazon's second heavy-lift launch vehicle to come online, after Ariane 6, and the company has booked more than 100 missions across its launch portfolio, with providers including Arianespace, Blue Origin, SpaceX, and ULA. The strategy is deliberate: by flying with multiple providers, Amazon can keep deploying even when one rocket is grounded — which is exactly what happened to Vulcan this year.

The 38 Vulcan launches Amazon has contracted will deliver more than 1,500 first-generation Leo satellites — more than any other rocket in the program — and Melissa Wuerl, Amazon Leo's director of launch systems, has called Vulcan the constellation's primary workhorse over the next several years. "Larger rockets and more frequent launches are critical to scaling our constellation and getting service to customers, and the Vulcan program is designed to deliver on both dimensions," Wuerl said ahead of the mission. "Vulcan will launch close to half our first-generation constellation, making it our primary workhorse over the next several years, and we're looking forward to kicking off the sprint with our partners at ULA."

There is also a clock running. Amazon obtained an extension from the United States Federal Communications Commission on June 5 this year, waiving the requirement to have half of its satellites flying by July 30, 2026. The company now has until July 30, 2029, to complete the constellation; if it is not complete by then, the FCC could limit Amazon to using only the satellites already launched. That deadline is one reason the Vulcan manifest matters so much. Every month Vulcan sits on the ground is a month of constellation growth lost, and every successful launch is a step toward a service that already has signed customers — Delta Air Lines, JetBlue, Crane Worldwide Logistics, and Hunt Energy among them — waiting for capacity. Amazon says it expects to expand service to more customers following an initial service rollout later this year.

## A Nine-Month Grounding, and the Nozzle That Has to Prove Itself

Nothing about LV-01 can be understood without the nine months that came before it. Vulcan has not flown since February 12, when the USSF-87 mission for the Space Force reached orbit successfully but not cleanly. Sixty-nine seconds after liftoff, engineers saw an "observation" on one of the GEM-63XL solid rocket boosters — a nozzle problem, similar in kind to one seen before. The rocket compensated for the resulting asymmetry and loss of thrust and completed its mission, just as it had the first time. But a rocket that can survive a nozzle failure is not the same as a rocket cleared to fly again, and the Space Force suspended Vulcan launches while the problem was investigated.

The February incident was the second nozzle failure on Vulcan. The first came on the rocket's second flight, in October 2024, when a nozzle "liberation" event struck one booster 37 seconds after liftoff. Again the rocket compensated and reached orbit, carrying an inert demonstration payload and experiments. Northrop Grumman, which builds the GEM-63XL, investigated, and ULA's then-chief executive Tory Bruno said the failure originated in insulators bonded to the inside of the shell that later became the nozzle. A modified booster was successfully test-fired at Northrop Grumman's facility in Promontory, Utah, in February 2025.

Vulcan returned to flight in August 2025 with the USSF-106 mission, flying in the four-booster VC4S configuration, and that flight was fully successful, with no observations on the GEM-63XL motors. For six months it looked as though the nozzle problem had been solved. Then USSF-87, also a VC4S flight, showed that it had not — or at least, not completely.

The investigation that followed identified three credible causes of the nozzle liberation issue, and Northrop Grumman applied fixes to mitigate each of them, covering both design and manufacturing. In April this year, the company successfully test-fired a GEM-63XL fitted with an enhanced nozzle at Promontory. Those changes are what LV-01 will test in flight. It is worth being precise about what that means: ground tests can prove a nozzle survives a firing on a stand, but only a real launch reproduces the vibration, the aerodynamic loads, the temperature swings, and the sheer violence of six boosters burning together on a vehicle climbing through the atmosphere. NASA, the Space Force, and ULA will all be reading the flight data from LV-01, and any sign of another nozzle problem will almost certainly delay future Vulcan flights while engineers go back to the data.

### The road to LV-01

- **October 2024** — Vulcan's second flight suffers a GEM-63XL nozzle liberation 37 seconds after liftoff; the rocket compensates and reaches orbit.
- **February 2025** — Northrop Grumman successfully test-fires a modified GEM-63XL at Promontory, Utah.
- **August 2025** — Vulcan returns to flight with USSF-106 in the VC4S configuration; the flight is fully successful.
- **February 12, 2026** — USSF-87 reaches orbit, but a nozzle observation occurs 69 seconds after liftoff; the Space Force suspends Vulcan launches.
- **April 15, 2026** — Northrop Grumman test-fires a GEM-63XL with an enhanced nozzle at Promontory as part of the return-to-flight effort.
- **Summer 2026** — The LV-01 core stage, known as V-012, rolls out to SLC-41 for a wet dress rehearsal, then returns for flight preparations.
- **November 11, 2026** — The launch readiness review clears LV-01 for launch.
- **November 12, 2026** — The fully stacked vehicle rolls out to the pad on Amazon's new Vulcan Launch Platform.
- **November 13, 2026** — Friday's attempt is scrubbed at 8:41 a.m. EST after a power interruption at the Air Liquide nitrogen plant.
- **November 14, 2026** — LV-01 is retargeted for 12:32:30 p.m. EST, with a zero percent chance of rain forecast.

## Who Is Watching: The Space Force, Starliner, and the Launch Business

The audience for this launch extends well beyond Amazon. The Space Force has told ULA it wants the first commercial Vulcan flight completed and its data reviewed before it decides whether to lift the hold on national security missions, and ULA president and chief executive Mark Peller has confirmed the pause remains in effect. The government's hold applies to national security missions, not to commercial flights like LV-01, which is precisely why this mission matters so much to the certification case: it is the flight that generates the evidence.

The stakes are visible in what has happened while Vulcan sat on the ground. The Space Force moved six launches to SpaceX as the grounding dragged on, as we reported in <a href="/blog/space-force-six-nssl-launches-spacex-vulcan-grounding/">Space Force Moves Six Launches to SpaceX as ULA's Vulcan Stays Grounded</a> — work and revenue that would otherwise have flown on ULA's newest rocket. Every month of delay has been a month in which the country's other heavy-lift provider picked up missions Vulcan was built to fly. NASA is watching too: Boeing's Starliner crewed spacecraft is planned to launch on Vulcan in the future, which means the rocket's reliability record will eventually carry astronauts. A clean LV-01 does not by itself restore full confidence — certification is a process, not a single launch — but a troubled one would set that process back a long way.

For ULA itself, the flight is a test of the business model it has been building toward for a decade. Vulcan was designed to replace both the Atlas V and the Delta IV Heavy, consolidating ULA's fleet into a single rocket that could serve commercial, civil, and national security customers. The company is preparing to ramp up its flight cadence sharply if LV-01 goes well: the LV-02 mission for Amazon Leo is already being processed in the original integration building, VIF-G, while LV-01 heads to the pad, and Amazon says it should fly within weeks of a nominal LV-01 mission. The two facilities working in parallel — the new Amazon-funded VIF-A and the original VIF-G — are what make that pace possible, allowing two rockets to be prepared at the same time.

That parallel processing is also the answer to the question Amazon's competitors will be asking. SpaceX's Starlink already operates thousands of satellites and serves customers around the world; Amazon is racing to close a gap measured not just in satellites but in launch slots. Vulcan's 38 contracted launches are the backbone of that plan, and the manifest behind them — more than 100 missions in total across the launch portfolio — is what turns a satellite factory into a working network. If ULA can fly Vulcan every few weeks, as it plans, the constellation's growth curve steepens dramatically. If it cannot, Amazon's other providers — Ariane 6, whose next Leo mission, LE-04, is targeting no earlier than November 23 from Kourou in French Guiana, and New Glenn, which also flies BE-4 engines — will have to carry more of the load.

## How to Watch

For readers in Indonesia, the arithmetic is simple: the launch happens at 00:32 WIB on Sunday morning, November 15, and the satellites begin deploying at about 01:49 WIB, with the last of them released at roughly 02:15 WIB. ULA's webcast begins about 20 minutes before liftoff, so the stream should be live from around 00:12 WIB. Spaceflight Now's live coverage starts 30 minutes earlier still. Florida Today's space team will be updating its live page from about 90 minutes before the target time.

What to look for in the first minutes is straightforward. The six solid boosters do most of the work early, and they burn out and separate in the first two minutes of flight, leaving the two BE-4 engines to carry Vulcan the rest of the way to orbit. The nozzle question — the reason this flight exists in its current form — is a matter for the first 90 seconds. The first nozzle failure came at 37 seconds; the second at 69 seconds. If Vulcan passes two minutes with all six boosters performing normally, the hardest part of the vehicle's return to flight will be behind it, even though the mission will still have an hour of Centaur coast and 21 satellite deployments ahead of it.

The rest of the flight is quieter but no less important. Centaur's two burns, the long coast, and the deployment sequence are where the mission becomes a constellation launch rather than a rocket test, and they are the moments Amazon's engineers will be watching most closely. A rocket can fly perfectly and still fail to deliver its payload if the deployment sequence goes wrong, and 21 separate release events leave 21 chances for something to stick. Confirmation of the deployments — not the liftoff — is what will tell the world whether LV-01 has succeeded, and that confirmation is expected in the early hours of Sunday morning, Jakarta time.

## What Happens Next

If LV-01 flies cleanly, the tempo changes immediately. LV-02 is already being prepared in VIF-G and is expected to launch within weeks, and Amazon says its investment in Cape Canaveral infrastructure will let the turnaround between launches shrink to days or weeks. The next Ariane 6 mission for the constellation, Leo Europe 4, is targeting no earlier than November 23 from Europe's Spaceport in Kourou. With more than 100 missions on the manifest, the constellation's growth from 432 satellites toward the 578 needed for initial service — and eventually toward the full first-generation fleet of more than 3,200 — becomes a matter of execution rather than of waiting.

If the launch scrubs again on Saturday, the picture is different but not dire. ULA has a rocket and a payload that are both healthy, a ground-support problem that has already been worked once, and a forecast with no rain in it. A second scrub would cost days, not months, and the mission would still be the same mission. But a second scrub would also be a reminder that launch operations are a chain, and that a rocket is only as reliable as the humblest piece of equipment on its ground — a nitrogen plant, a pump, a power feed — allows it to be.

And if the flight itself goes wrong, the consequences would be measured in months. Another nozzle event would reopen the investigation, extend the Space Force's hold, and force ULA and Northrop Grumman back to the test stand at Promontory. Amazon would lean harder on Ariane 6, Falcon 9, and New Glenn while Vulcan was grounded again, and the 2029 FCC deadline would start to look less comfortable. None of that is the expected outcome — the ground tests passed, the readiness review cleared the flight, and the weather is perfect — but it is the outcome everyone involved has spent nine months trying to make impossible.

Saturday afternoon in Florida, Sunday after midnight in Jakarta, a white-and-red rocket 65 metres tall will light six boosters and two methane engines and climb northeast over the Atlantic, carrying 40 satellites and the weight of a company's constellation plan, a rocket's reputation, and a certification case that has been waiting nine months for its evidence. The countdown that was stopped on Friday morning starts again. This time, the only thing standing between Vulcan and orbit is the rocket itself.
