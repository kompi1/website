At 8:37 on a humid Friday morning, a Falcon 9 rocket lifted off from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, carrying 29 Starlink internet satellites toward low Earth orbit. Less than an hour later, thousands of miles away in New York, another kind of launch took place: shares of SpaceX began trading on the Nasdaq stock exchange under the ticker symbol SPCX.

In the span of a single morning, Elon Musk's rocket company added satellites to its megaconstellation and rewrote the record books of American finance. SpaceX priced 555.56 million shares at $135 apiece the night before, raising approximately $75 billion — the largest initial public offering in the history of Wall Street, more than two and a half times the $29.4 billion Saudi Aramco raised in its 2019 debut. The implied valuation at listing: $1.77 trillion.

By the closing bell, the numbers were even more staggering. SPCX opened at $150, climbed as high as $176.52 during the session, and finished its first day at $160.95 — a 19.2 percent pop that pushed the company's market capitalization to roughly $2.1 trillion, making SpaceX the sixth most valuable U.S.-listed company on the planet after a single day of trading. The offering was reportedly more than four times oversubscribed, and in a break with Wall Street convention, retail investors were allocated roughly 20 percent of the available shares — far above the typical 5 to 10 percent reserved for individuals in most major IPOs.

Twenty-four years after Musk founded the company with the stated goal of making humanity multiplanetary, SpaceX had become a public company. And its first day as one was, fittingly, business as usual: by the time traders went home, the morning's Falcon 9 booster — veteran B1080, flying for the 27th time — had already landed itself on the droneship *A Shortfall of Gravitas* in the Atlantic Ocean, and the 29 satellites were spreading out in orbit. It was the 68th Falcon 9 mission of 2026.

## The numbers behind history

To appreciate the scale of what happened on June 12, it helps to line SpaceX's debut against the biggest public offerings that came before it. Saudi Aramco's $29.4 billion listing in Riyadh was the previous record-holder. Alibaba's $25 billion New York debut in 2014, once the largest in U.S. history, suddenly looked modest. SoftBank's $23.5 billion Arm listing in 2023 — the biggest tech IPO of the decade — was barely a third of SpaceX's raise.

But the raise was only half the story. The valuation was the other half, and it was the valuation that made June 12 feel like a hinge point in economic history. At $1.77 trillion, SpaceX went public worth more than Tesla — and more than all but a handful of American companies. On the first trading day it briefly pushed past $2 trillion in market value, a threshold only a few firms have ever crossed.

The structure of the deal was engineered for maximum public participation. On May 15, SpaceX completed a 5-for-1 stock split, cutting the per-share price from $526.59 to $105.32 — a move designed to make the shares accessible to ordinary investors whose brokers don't offer fractional-share trading. The company then ran a compressed timeline: the S-1 prospectus went public in late May after the Securities and Exchange Commission completed its review ahead of schedule, the investor roadshow began on June 4, and pricing was set on June 11. A syndicate of 23 financial institutions — led by Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Bank of America, Citigroup, and JPMorgan — managed the offering.

Not everyone was cheering. In the weeks before the debut, pension chiefs from New York and California pushed back on SpaceX's proposed governance structure, which critics described as unusually management-friendly for a company of this scale — super-voting shares for insiders and restrictions on shareholder lawsuits among the provisions drawing fire. SpaceX did not publicly answer the objections, and the market, in the end, voted with its wallet: demand overwhelmed supply.

The company also secured a quiet regulatory win that mattered enormously to index investors: a Nasdaq rule change allowing SPCX early inclusion in the Nasdaq-100 index — though not, notably, in the S&P 500. Inclusion in the Nasdaq-100 guarantees a steady stream of passive investment from the index funds and ETFs that track it, effectively building a floor of institutional demand beneath the stock.

## What investors actually bought

Strip away the ticker symbol and the trading-day theatrics, and the question every investor had to answer was the same one SpaceX's private-market backers had wrestled with for years: what exactly is this company, and what is it worth?

The prospectus painted SpaceX as four businesses wearing one logo. The first and most lucrative is Starlink, the satellite internet constellation that has become the financial engine of the entire enterprise. SpaceX disclosed that its connectivity business generated roughly $2 billion in revenue in 2024 and $4.4 billion in 2025. The company reported 10 million active subscribers as of February 2026 — up from 4 million in September 2024 — across 160 countries and territories. By the morning of the IPO, the constellation counted more than 10,600 active satellites, according to tracker Jonathan McDowell, providing broadband from remote villages to airliners in flight, plus direct-to-cell service through select carrier partners.

The second business is the launch operation itself: the Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets that have made SpaceX the dominant launch provider on Earth, flying national-security payloads, NASA science missions, commercial satellites, and the company's own Starlink spacecraft. Reusability — landing and reflying boosters — turned launch from a bespoke engineering project into something approaching an assembly line. The IPO-day mission was the 157th landing on the droneship *A Shortfall of Gravitas* and the latest demonstration of a cadence no competitor has matched.

The third is Starship, the giant fully reusable vehicle SpaceX is developing in Texas — 33 engines on the booster, a ship designed to be caught out of the sky — intended to loft the next generation of Starlink satellites by the hundreds, carry NASA astronauts to the lunar surface, and, in Musk's telling, eventually carry settlers to Mars.

The fourth, and the one that most complicates any valuation model, is artificial intelligence. In February 2026, SpaceX merged with xAI, Musk's AI startup — the combined entity was valued at about $1.25 trillion at the time — folding together the Grok AI models, the X social media platform, and SpaceX's launch and satellite infrastructure. The prospectus said the $75 billion raise would fund long-term ambitions including building orbital AI infrastructure: data centers in space to meet the computing needs of xAI.

That last element is precisely what made independent analysts queasy. Morningstar, the investment research firm, valued SpaceX at $780 billion before the listing — less than half the $1.77 trillion offer price — and flagged losses absorbed through the xAI merger as a structural concern. The gap between Morningstar's math and the market's enthusiasm captured a genuine disagreement: is SpaceX a launch company with a great broadband business, or is it the indispensable infrastructure layer for the AI age, with rockets as merely the delivery mechanism?

## The trillionaire question

No discussion of the IPO could avoid the number attached to its founder. Elon Musk's retained stake in SpaceX, multiplied by the debut-day valuation, quickly made him the world's first trillionaire — at least on paper. The milestone was reported widely on June 12 and was, characteristically, controversial: wealth measured in unrealized shareholdings of a company that had been public for a few hours is a fragile thing, and critics noted the circularity of valuing the fortune by the very offering Musk's own company had engineered.

Musk himself has long treated net-worth rankings as a sideshow, but the trillionaire label carried symbolic weight beyond one man's balance sheet. It was the market's verdict on an idea Musk has preached since 2002: that a private company could fund the conquest of space by selling internet access from orbit, and that the economics of reusability would compound into something no incumbent could match. On June 12, the market agreed to the tune of two trillion dollars.

## The fine print Wall Street is watching

Beneath the celebration, the paperwork told a more complicated story — and professional investors were reading it closely.

On June 1, SpaceX filed an amendment to its S-1 that added a new warning to the risk-factors section: the company said it may issue "significant equity" in connection with future acquisitions, partnerships, or other transactions. Dilution warnings are boilerplate in IPO filings, but the specific phrasing stood out, because it landed in the middle of persistent speculation that Musk ultimately intends to combine Tesla and SpaceX under a common corporate structure. The company had merged with xAI only four months earlier; investors were left to wonder whether the IPO was, in part, about creating a publicly traded currency for the next deal rather than simply raising cash.

The governance fight was sharper. Pension officials from New York and California argued, in a letter reviewed by Reuters, that SpaceX was bringing to market one of the most management-friendly governance structures ever attempted at this scale — super-voting shares concentrating control with insiders, plus provisions restricting shareholders' ability to sue. SpaceX never publicly answered the letter. The four-times oversubscription suggested the market had heard the objection and bought anyway, but governance terms do not expire with the IPO pop. They will govern every shareholder vote, every board fight, and every related-party transaction for as long as the company is public.

Then there is the calendar. Insider shares are locked up for 180 days, until December 9 — the first moment early employees and investors can sell. Lockup expirations are the classic stress test of an IPO: they reveal how much of the debut-day demand was durable conviction and how much was scarcity. Between now and then, SpaceX must do something it has never done before — report quarterly earnings, host analyst calls, and explain a quarter in which a rocket exploded or a Starship test slipped, in the language of guidance and margins rather than engineering postmortems.

The valuation debate will be re-litigated every one of those quarters. Morningstar's $780 billion assessment against the market's $2 trillion is not a rounding error; it is a fundamental disagreement about what SpaceX is. If the company is a launch provider with a superb broadband business, the skeptics have a point. If it is the company that will own orbital access, orbital communications, and eventually orbital computing — the infrastructure layer for the AI economy — the believers might, improbably, be right. Both sides will get their evidence soon enough, delivered one quarter at a time.

## One company, two launches

If there was a single image that captured the strangeness of the day, it was the split screen: in Florida, a rocket climbing through humid morning air; in Manhattan, SpaceX's chief operating officer Gwynne Shotwell preparing to ring the Nasdaq opening bell.

"Today we make history again. We have a history of making history," Shotwell said ahead of the bell ceremony. "We did open this morning in a rather exciting way. We launched a Falcon 9 and Starlink satellites to orbit. So what company would do such a thing on the day they open in the public market? SpaceX would."

The launch itself was routine only by SpaceX's standards. Liftoff of the Starlink 10-54 mission came at 8:37 a.m. EDT, sending 29 Starlink V2 Mini Optimized satellites — the latest iteration of the spacecraft, each heavier and more capable than its predecessors — on a northeasterly trajectory paralleling the U.S. East Coast. About eight and a half minutes later, the first stage touched down on the droneship. The satellites were confirmed deployed roughly an hour into the flight, at just about the moment SPCX began trading.

The booster that did the flying deserved its own footnote. B1080 was on its 27th mission — a veteran whose logbook included the Axiom-2 and Axiom-3 private crew flights, the European Space Agency's Euclid space telescope, and a NASA cargo resupply run. Its landing on *A Shortfall of Gravitas* was the 157th touchdown on that vessel. Reusability, the technology that made the company's economics work, was on full display on the morning the company went public: the rocket that opened the trading day had already been to space and back 26 times before.

There was poetry in the timing, and SpaceX knew it. The company's launch webcast and social channels leaned into the double meaning of the word "launch" all morning. But there was also a harder-edged message: the cadence doesn't stop for Wall Street. Rockets fly on the schedule the manifest demands, and the manifest in 2026 demanded a Falcon 9 roughly every three days.

## What comes next

The morning after the biggest IPO in history, the questions shifted from what SpaceX is worth to what it will do with $75 billion in fresh capital — and what being public will do to SpaceX.

The immediate calendar is unforgiving. Insider shares are locked up for 180 days, with the lockup expiring on December 9 — the date when early employees and investors can first sell, and the first real test of whether the debut-day enthusiasm has durable support. The company will now report quarterly earnings, face analyst calls, and explain itself to shareholders who measure progress in 90-day increments rather than Mars transfer windows.

The spending plan, as sketched in the prospectus, is dominated by two enormous bets. The first is Starship: scaling production, increasing flight rate, and proving the vehicle can do the jobs the business plan requires — deploying Starlink's next-generation satellites by the hundreds, refueling in orbit, and landing on the Moon under NASA's Artemis program. The second is orbital AI infrastructure: turning the company's dominance in space access into a platform for computing in orbit, where solar power is abundant and cooling is free.

Both bets are genuinely audacious, and both will be judged by a market that is less patient than the private investors who funded SpaceX for two decades. The governance questions that pension funds raised before the IPO will not disappear; they will resurface at the first shareholder meeting. The valuation debate — Morningstar's $780 billion versus the market's $2 trillion — will be re-litigated every quarter.

But on the morning of June 12, none of that had happened yet. There was only a rocket rising over the Atlantic and a ticker symbol blinking to life in Times Square, and the strange, exhilarating sense that the company that taught the world to expect the impossible had just done it again — twice, before lunch. The bell rang. The booster landed. The satellites deployed. And the largest IPO in history was, for SpaceX, just another Friday.
