A Long March 12 rocket climbed away from China's southern coast in the small hours of Monday morning, carrying nine more satellites for GuoWang, the state-backed broadband constellation that Beijing is assembling at a pace few other space programmes can match. The flight was the third GuoWang launch group in a single month — and the tenth flight of a rocket that has never failed.

The mission, designated Long March 12 Y11, lifted off from Commercial Launch Complex 2 at the Wenchang Space Launch Site on Hainan Island at 04:24 local time on Monday, November 9 — 20:24 UTC on Sunday, November 8, or 03:24 in Western Indonesian Time. The flight was confirmed a success: all nine satellites reached their planned orbit, taking the number of GuoWang spacecraft in space past 240. The launch had been moved to this date after slipping from its original target of Thursday, November 5.

## What flew

The payload was a batch of nine low Earth orbit communications satellites for the SatNet constellation, operated by the state-owned China Satellite Network Group. SatNet is the operating name for the network better known outside China as GuoWang — literally "national network" — and it is the centrepiece of Beijing's answer to SpaceX's Starlink. The full constellation is planned to grow to 13,000 satellites, a scale that would make it one of the largest satellite networks ever attempted.

The nine spacecraft were bound for a low Earth orbit at an altitude of about 550 kilometres, inclined at 28.5 degrees to the equator, according to Orbit-deck's mission data. That orbital geometry is consistent with the previous GuoWang groups: a shell of satellites circling the equator-leaning band of low Earth orbit, from which the constellation will eventually provide broadband coverage around the globe. Trackers had put the weather violation risk for the window at just 10 percent, and the coastal weather at Wenchang cooperated.

This flight is now confirmed as the constellation's 29th group. The nine satellites were produced by the China Academy of Space Technology and use the constellation's small satellite platform, each weighing about 695 kilograms. After their deployment, 240 GuoWang satellites are in space, functioning in and heading up to their operational orbits.

## A rocket that has never missed

The Long March 12 is a relative newcomer to China's rocket fleet, but it has compiled a flawless record since its debut. Developed by the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology, the 62.6-metre-tall, 3.8-metre-diameter vehicle burns kerosene and liquid oxygen and can deliver up to 12,000 kilograms to low Earth orbit. According to launch records compiled by Space Launch Live, the rocket had flown nine times with a 100 percent success rate before this mission — and the tenth flight kept the record intact.

The numbers now behind the vehicle are striking. This was the tenth launch of a Long March 12, the 12th launch of the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology-supported Long March 12 series, the 282nd Long March vehicle from the Shanghai Academy, and the 676th launch of the Long March series overall. It was also the 80th launch from China in 2026.

That reliability matters because the Long March 12 has become one of the workhorses of the GuoWang deployment. Its most recent flight before this one, on October 9, 2026, carried the constellation's 27th group of nine satellites into orbit from the same Wenchang commercial complex. Specialist trackers identified that flight as Long March 12 Y10 — the ninth flight of the type and the 671st launch of the Long March series overall. The satellites on that mission, each weighing about 695 kilograms and built by the Innovation Academy for Microsatellites of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, were designated Hulianwang Digui 27 A through I.

The numbers behind the programme are large and growing. After the October 9 launch, estimates of how many GuoWang satellites were in orbit ranged from 220, counted by the blog Behind The Black, to 222, counted by the specialist outlet china-in-space.com. With nine more spacecraft now added, the count stands at 240. The constellation is planned to reach 310 satellites by the end of 2026 and 900 in 2027, with a target of 3,600 launches per year thereafter on the road to 13,000 in the 2030s. The October 9 mission was also the 71st Chinese space launch of 2026, a reminder that GuoWang is only one strand of a national launch tempo that now rivals the United States in sheer volume.

## Three groups in a month

The November 9 flight continues a brisk cadence. Just sixteen days earlier, on October 25, a Long March 8A lifted off from Commercial Launch Pad 1 at Wenchang carrying the constellation's 28th group of nine satellites, as covered in <a href="/blog/china-long-march-8a-guowang-group-28-launch/">China's GuoWang Network Grows Again as Long March 8A Launches Nine More Internet Satellites</a>. Before that, the 27th group flew on October 9 aboard the Long March 12 itself. Three groups in a month, 27 satellites in orbit, and no sign of a pause.

That tempo is the story of GuoWang in 2026. The constellation is being deployed in steady, nine-satellite increments, using two different rockets — the Long March 12 and the Long March 8A — from two commercial pads at the same coastal spaceport. It is a deliberately industrial approach: standardised satellites, standardised batches, standardised launch slots, with the Wenchang commercial site functioning less like a test range and more like a production line.

The choice of Wenchang is no accident. The spaceport on Hainan Island, China's southernmost province, sits at a low latitude that gives rockets a helpful boost from Earth's rotation, and its coastal location lets spent stages fall harmlessly into the sea. The commercial launch complex there, with its LC-1 and LC-2 pads, was built specifically to serve the new generation of Chinese commercial and state-backed missions — and GuoWang, a state-owned constellation flown on state-built rockets, has become its most regular customer.

The pace is accelerating. This was the Long March 12's fourth flight in four months, following GuoWang deployments in August, September and October. The China Aerospace Science and Technology Commercial Launch Vehicle Group, the state-owned launch sales firm, said after the mission that the rocket has been flying at a "high density" rate since August and will continue to do so. Another launch may follow before November ends, depending on hardware and payload readiness, and one of the vehicle's last missions of the year could come around mid-December before efforts shift towards 2027.

## How the flight unfolded

The Long March 12 is a single-use, expendable vehicle — there was no booster recovery on this mission, and the first stage followed an ocean trajectory after separation. According to Orbit-deck's staging sequence, the terminal count began its autonomous phase fifteen minutes before liftoff, with the flight computer taking full control three minutes out. Main engine ignition and liftoff came at T-0, and the first stage burned for about two minutes and forty seconds before main engine cut-off and stage separation.

From there, the high-energy, re-ignitable upper stage took over, carrying the nine satellites toward their 550-kilometre orbit. Payload deployment came around T-plus 45 minutes, when the spacecraft were released into their target orbit. It was a brisk, efficient profile: under an hour from ignition on a Hainan beach to nine new satellites in orbit.

That speed is part of the point. The Long March 12 was designed for exactly this kind of work — a medium-lift vehicle, sized between China's small solid-fuelled rockets and its heavy-lift Long March 5, able to loft a meaningful batch of satellites in a single flight without the cost and complexity of a heavy launcher. Its 12,000-kilogram capacity to low Earth orbit leaves ample margin for nine satellites in the 700-kilogram class, with room for the dispenser hardware that releases them one by one. If the programme moves to larger batches in future, the same rocket has the performance to carry them.

The launch also showed how fast the ground operation has become. In their post-launch statement, the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology and the Commercial Launch Vehicle Group said their teams had continued to optimise pre-flight processing, enabling the vehicle to fly within three days of being brought to the pad and shortening launch-day processes to nine hours. Four YF-100K engines power the first stage, generating a combined thrust of 510 tons, while two YF-115 engines power the second stage with 36 tons of thrust. Fully fuelled, the rocket is believed to weigh 433,000 kilograms.

## Why the world is watching a routine launch

A launch of nine identical communications satellites would once have been a footnote. Today it is a data point in one of the defining competitions of the decade: the race to own the orbital layer of the internet. SpaceX's Starlink, with thousands of satellites already in orbit, proved that a low Earth orbit broadband network could work at scale — and proved, too, that the country which controls such a network controls a piece of strategic infrastructure. China's answer is GuoWang, and the Long March 12 is one of the vehicles carrying it into the sky.

The stakes are visible in the numbers. A constellation of 13,000 satellites would require more than 1,400 launches at nine satellites per flight — an impossible pace, which is why the deployment is expected to accelerate with larger batches, new rockets and higher flight rates in the coming years. The 310-by-year-end target for 2026 and 900 in 2027 suggest the programme is still in its early, methodical phase, building the first shell of the network while the industrial base behind it scales up.

There is also a quieter signal in the schedule. The original target date for this flight was Thursday, November 5; it slipped three days to Sunday, November 8, according to Next Launch Today's schedule data. Delays of a few days are unremarkable in spaceflight — weather, range availability and final checks routinely shuffle the calendar — but the fact that the mission was re-targeted within days rather than weeks speaks to the maturity of the Wenchang operation. The rocket was ready, the pad was ready, and the window moved by days, not months.

For observers outside China, the frustration is familiar: Beijing says little about GuoWang in advance. State media typically confirm a launch only after the satellites are in orbit, and the payloads are described in generic terms — a "group of low-orbit internet satellites" — without technical details. What is known comes from tracking data, maritime advisories and the patient counting of independent analysts. That is why the launch trackers matter: they turn a silent countdown into a public schedule, and their agreement on this window — 20:17 to 20:47 UTC, nine satellites, Wenchang LC-2 — was about as firm a signal as this programme ever gives in advance.

## A tale of two constellations

GuoWang is not China's only satellite internet project. The Shanghai-based Qianfan constellation — sometimes called "Thousand Sails" — is being built in parallel by a different consortium, and the two networks together represent Beijing's two-track bet on orbital broadband. Qianfan's satellites fly on a variety of rockets, including the Long March 12's reusable sibling, the Long March 12B, whose surprise maiden flight in June 2026 delivered two Qianfan satellites to polar orbit — a story told in <a href="/blog/long-march-12b-maiden-flight/">The Rocket That Appeared From Nowhere: China's Long March 12B Makes a Surprise Maiden Flight</a>.

The distinction matters because it shows how China's space industry is organising itself. GuoWang is the state constellation, operated by the China Satellite Network Group and flown largely on state rockets from the Wenchang commercial complex. Qianfan is the commercial challenger, flying on whatever vehicles are available. Both are racing the same clock: the International Telecommunication Union's filing deadlines, which require a large fraction of a registered constellation to be in orbit within a set number of years or the spectrum rights lapse. That regulatory pressure, as much as any commercial logic, explains the relentless nine-at-a-time cadence.

The competition is not only with Starlink. Amazon's Project Kuiper, Europe's IRIS² and several smaller networks are all pursuing the same orbital real estate, and the low Earth orbit shells they covet are finite. Every group of nine satellites GuoWang puts up is a claim staked — a set of orbital slots occupied, a set of frequencies used, a piece of the future internet's infrastructure secured. The rockets may be routine, but the arithmetic is not.

## The week ahead

The Long March 12 flight was the first of several closely watched launches this week. On Tuesday, November 10, a SpaceX Falcon 9 is due to lift off from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California carrying the classified USSF-627 mission for the United States Space Force, with a four-hour window opening at 10:03 WIB — a mission whose predecessor flight SpaceNews covered in <a href="/blog/ussf-432-launch-success-november-7-2026/">USSF-432 Falcon 9 Launch November 7 2026: Classified Space Force Payload Reaches Orbit</a>, which flew on November 7 aboard booster B1095. And on Friday, November 13, SpaceX is targeting the 15th integrated flight test of Starship, a mission expected to attempt the first-ever catch of the Starship upper stage with the launch tower's arms, alongside a catch of the Super Heavy booster.

It is a crowded week, and it underlines a shift that has been building all year: launches that once felt like events now arrive in clusters, several a week, from three continents. The Long March 12 flight in the small hours of Monday morning is part of that new normal — a routine launch, carrying a routine batch of satellites, for a constellation that is anything but routine in its ambition.

## What comes next

With the group designation confirmed as the 29th, the count to watch is now 240 — and whether the launch rate steps up in December, as the 310-by-year-end target implies it must. The Long March 12 has kept its perfect record through a tenth flight, and the commercial launch vehicle group says the high-density tempo will continue. Another GuoWang launch may come before November ends, and one of the vehicle's last missions of the year could fly around mid-December.

The questions that follow are the interesting ones. Will the next group confirm the programme's push toward 310 satellites before the year closes? Will larger batches or new rockets join the deployment in 2027, when the target jumps to 900? And how long can a rocket keep a perfect record when it is flying every few weeks? For a vehicle that has never failed, the tenth launch is a milestone worth noting — not because failure was expected, but because in spaceflight, every flight is a fresh test of the same unforgiving physics.
