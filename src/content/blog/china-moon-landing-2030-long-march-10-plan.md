China has spent years describing its crewed lunar ambition in broad strokes: a landing before 2030, a new rocket, a new spacecraft, a new lander. On October 31, 2026, at the seventh Manned Space Symposium in Beijing, deputy chief designer Wang Xiang filled in the missing middle of that story. Speaking as both deputy chief designer of China's human spaceflight program and chief designer of the China Lunar Exploration Program, Wang laid out a three-step roadmap that turns a deadline into a sequence: first an uncrewed lander proves it can land and lift off alone, then the full two-rocket architecture rehearses itself without a crew, and only then do astronauts ride the same path to the surface. It was the most detailed public account yet of how China intends to put its first taikonauts on the Moon, and it arrived wrapped in a week of symbolism — the symposium marked the 70th anniversary of the founding of China's space program and served as a showcase for the country's ambitions under its 15th five-year plan, Beijing's blueprint for the years 2026 to 2030.

The plan, as Wang described it, is simple to state and hard to execute. One Long March 10 rocket will send the Mengzhou crew spacecraft toward the Moon. A second Long March 10 will send the Lanyue lander. The two craft will meet and dock in lunar orbit, and two of the three taikonauts aboard Mengzhou will transfer into Lanyue for the descent to the surface. After their surface stay, the pair will lift off again in Lanyue, dock once more with Mengzhou, and ride the crew capsule back to Earth, while the third crew member waits in lunar orbit. It is the same basic choreography Apollo used more than half a century ago — lunar orbit rendezvous — but executed with two heavy rockets instead of one Saturn V, and with a lander designed from the start to carry a rover.

## The announcement in Beijing

The venue mattered as much as the message. The Manned Space Symposium is not a press conference; it is a gathering of the engineers and managers who actually build China's crewed spacecraft, and Wang's remarks were carried by state media including CGTN. The Register, reporting on the speech on November 4, noted that Chinese state media posted video of Wang discussing the two-rocket plan, and that he framed the coming years as a deliberate sequence of tests rather than a race to a single launch date. First, he said, China plans near-Earth orbit test missions for both new vehicles, followed by an uncrewed visit to the Moon. If all goes well, the crewed landing attempt follows before 2030.

That phrasing — "before 2030" rather than a specific year — is worth noting, because it is the only date China has formally committed to. Reports on the speech stayed with the architecture and the test record: no crew was named, no exact launch date was set, and no landing site was announced. Readers should treat any specific landing date beyond "before 2030" as unconfirmed. What the symposium did confirm is the order of operations, and in a program as complex as a crewed lunar landing, the order of operations is the plan.

The timing of the announcement also fits a wider pattern in China's space program this year. In May 2026, the China Manned Space Agency folded its crewed lunar effort, its robotic Chang'e missions, and even the work of the Tiangong space station into a single integrated moon exploration project, in language that spoke of mobilizing all resources for the 2030 goal — a consolidation covered in our earlier report on <a href="/blog/china-moon-program-2030/">China's integrated moon exploration program</a>. The October symposium speech reads as the engineering sequel to that organizational decision: the structure was unified in May, and by late October the chief designer was explaining, step by step, how the unified program will actually fly.

## Two rockets, one rendezvous

The workhorse of the whole architecture is the Long March 10, a three-stage super heavy-lift rocket built by the China Academy of Launch Vehicle Technology. The standard crewed variant stands 92.5 meters tall — roughly the height of a 30-story building — and weighs about 2,189 tonnes at liftoff. Its first stage and two side boosters share a common five-meter-diameter design, each powered by seven YF-100K kerosene-fueled engines, for 21 engines firing at liftoff. A hydrolox third stage with three YF-75E engines performs the trans-lunar injection burn that sends the payload toward the Moon. The published performance figures are 70 tonnes to low Earth orbit and at least 27 tonnes on a trans-lunar trajectory — enough, in each of two launches, for one half of the landing system.

That two-launch split is the defining trade of China's approach. The United States' Apollo program used a single Saturn V to send the command module and lunar module together; NASA's Artemis program uses the Space Launch System for the crew capsule and relies on separately launched commercial landers. China has chosen a middle path: two launches of the same rocket, one for the crew ship and one for the lander, meeting in lunar orbit. The advantage is commonality — one rocket design, one production line, one set of ground systems — and the disadvantage is the rendezvous itself. Both launches must succeed, both spacecraft must reach lunar orbit healthy, and the docking must work the first time it truly matters, because there is no backup lander waiting in lunar orbit.

The medium-lift siblings of the rocket family show how China is thinking about reuse and cost at the same time. The Long March 10A and 10B variants drop the boosters and third stage and are designed to recover their first stage by vertical landing on a floating platform, with a capacity of at least 14 tonnes to low Earth orbit. They are intended to take over crew and cargo transport to the Tiangong station, superseding the Long March 2F and Long March 7 respectively. A <a href="/blog/china-long-march-10b-first-stage-recovery/">Long March 10B first-stage recovery test</a> earlier this year demonstrated the recovery concept in flight, and in February 2026 a Long March 10 first-stage booster performed a controlled splashdown just 200 meters from its landing platform in the South China Sea — close enough to prove the guidance, far enough from the deck to show how much work remains.

## The three-step roadmap

Wang's roadmap, as detailed in the days after the symposium, has three distinct steps, and each one retires a different risk before astronauts are allowed to take it on.

Step one is a solo flight of the lander. A stand-alone, uncrewed Lanyue will launch on a tri-core Long March 10 and attempt a fully autonomous landing on the Moon, then ascend back into lunar orbit. This step tests the hardest part of the mission in isolation: the descent, the touchdown on unprepared terrain, and the ascent engine restart that must work perfectly because there is no second chance and no rescue. If the lander cannot land and lift off by itself, nothing else in the architecture matters, so China will prove that first.

Step two is the full dress rehearsal, still without a crew. Two Long March 10 rockets will launch an uncrewed Mengzhou spacecraft and an uncrewed Lanyue lander separately. The pair will autonomously dock in lunar orbit, Lanyue will attempt an uncrewed lunar landing followed by an ascent, and then it will dock autonomously with Mengzhou once again before the crew capsule returns safely to Earth. This is the complete mission flown end to end, minus the people — every rendezvous, every docking, every engine burn, every return trajectory, all validated against real lunar conditions rather than simulations. Only after that rehearsal does step three put a crew aboard: the same architecture, the same sequence, this time with two taikonauts descending to the surface and a third remaining in lunar orbit.

The discipline of that sequence is the real news in Wang's speech. Many lunar programs have announced a landing date and then worked backward; China has announced a sequence and let the date follow from it. The uncrewed steps also explain why the near-term test program emphasizes near-Earth orbit flights for both vehicles first: Mengzhou must prove itself as a crew transport in Earth orbit, and Lanyue must prove its landing and takeoff systems, before either is committed to a translunar trajectory. The Long March 10A's maiden orbital flight, carrying an uncrewed Mengzhou 1, is planned for late 2026 — a test that, if it flies on schedule, will close out the year with the crew capsule's first orbital flight already in the books.

## Meet the hardware: Mengzhou and Lanyue

Mengzhou, whose name means "dream vessel," is the crew spacecraft at the heart of the plan. It carries the astronauts from Earth to lunar orbit, hosts the dockings with Lanyue, and brings the crew home through Earth's atmosphere at the end of the mission. Its test record so far centers on the moments when a crew's life depends on hardware working instantly: zero-height abort tests and maximum-dynamic-pressure abort tests, both designed to prove that the capsule can escape a failing rocket on the pad and during the most stressful part of ascent. Those tests, completed before the symposium, are the unglamorous prerequisites that make a crewed flight possible at all.

Lanyue — "embracing the Moon" — is the lander, built by the China Academy of Space Technology for the China Manned Space Agency. It consists of two parts: a propulsion stage and a landing and ascent stage. Unlike Apollo's lunar module, which carried its descent stage all the way to the surface, Lanyue will jettison its propulsion stage before landing, shedding dead weight for the final descent. The combined spacecraft, propulsion stage included, has an estimated launch mass of about 26,000 kilograms, and the crew-carrying segment is designed for two astronauts. Its main propulsion system is described as four 7,500-newton engines, with the propulsion module powered by a single larger engine, all burning storable propellants — the kind of reliable, long-storable chemistry that suits a spacecraft which may have to wait in lunar orbit before its crew arrives.

The lander is also designed to carry its own transport. A stowed lunar rover capable of carrying two astronauts is part of the design, along with docking mechanisms for meeting Mengzhou, a crew hatch for surface excursions, a ladder attached to one of the landing legs, two solar arrays, and the antennas and sensors needed to navigate to a precise landing. The rover matters because it signals intent: this is not a flags-and-footprints visit but the opening move of a surface program, in which mobility extends what two astronauts can explore and sample during a short stay. An integrated landing and takeoff test of the lander hardware was completed in August 2025, a full year before Wang's roadmap speech, which suggests the lander's riskiest maneuvers have already been exercised on the ground.

## The tests already done

Taken together, the test record China brought to the symposium is substantial, and it helps explain the confidence of the roadmap. The Long March 10 has completed low-altitude validation flights and tethered ignition tests. The February 2026 booster splashdown test showed the first stage's guidance working to within 200 meters of a floating platform. Mengzhou has passed its abort tests. Lanyue has passed its integrated landing and takeoff test. The Long March 10A's maiden flight with Mengzhou 1 is targeted for late 2026. None of these is a lunar landing, but each one is a box that must be ticked before a lunar landing can be attempted, and the list of unticked boxes is visibly shrinking.

That is also why the symposium's timing, days before the end of October, reads as a deliberate checkpoint. China was able to present a program that has already survived its early hardware tests and can now describe, in public, the exact order of the flights that remain. The contrast with a program that announces a date first and discovers its risks later is the point: Wang's three steps are, in effect, a promise that the crewed flight will not happen until the uncrewed flights have already done the same mission twice over.

## The robotic scout that stumbled

There is, however, a reminder in the same week's news that even careful programs meet setbacks. China called off its last robotic moonshot in August 2026, when authorities decided that the Chang'e 7 ice-hunting mission "failed to meet launch criteria" just one day before its planned launch. The mission is aimed at the lunar south pole, where water ice in permanently shadowed craters is one of the most valuable resources in the solar system — and where the orbit required to reach the south pole is not always accessible, because the geometry of the transfer window opens and closes with the Moon's motion. China plans to try again next year, when the window reopens.

The Chang'e 7 delay matters to the crewed program in two ways. Scientifically, the south pole is the leading candidate region for a crewed landing precisely because of that ice, which could one day supply water, oxygen, and even propellant; a robotic scout that maps the ice and tests the terrain is the natural precursor to boots on the same ground. Programmatically, the scrub is a case study in the same discipline Wang preached at the symposium: a launch criterion was not met, and the launch did not happen, even at the cost of a year's delay. A program willing to stand down a robotic mission one day before launch is a program whose crewed schedule can be trusted to slip rather than to gamble — and for the astronauts who will eventually ride Lanyue, that is the more reassuring signal of the two.

## The quiet paperwork: seventeen new standards

Less dramatic, but just as telling, is the bureaucratic news from the same week. The China Manned Space Engineering Office published seventeen new standards for the crewed program last week, covering the unglamorous details that decide whether a complex mission works. One defines the architecture for Ethernet and Wi-Fi networks on China's space station and other orbiters — the internal data plumbing that lets spacecraft, stations, and ground systems talk to each other reliably. Another governs signage used in orbit, in the hope that a standard means taikonauts will not make mistakes when reading labels under stress. A couple outline procedures for extravehicular activities and for the use of a robotic arm to assist taikonauts on such missions. Others explain which materials designers can choose from when building spacecraft.

Standards sound dull until you consider what they replace: ad-hoc decisions, made separately by each contractor, that have to be reconciled in orbit. A crewed lunar mission is, among other things, an exercise in interfaces — the lander must dock with the crew ship, the rover must fit in the lander, the suits must work with the hatch, the arm must handle the tools. Publishing standards for networks, signage, spacewalks, robotic arms, and materials is how a program makes those interfaces routine before they become emergencies. It is also a sign of a program moving from development into operations: you write standards when you expect to build more than one of something, and to fly it more than once.

## The race context: two programs, one Moon

China's roadmap does not exist in a vacuum. NASA's Artemis program, reshaped by an October 23, 2026 acceleration update at Kennedy Space Center, now aims for a crewed landing in 2028 on the Artemis IV mission, following an Artemis III Earth-orbit test flight in 2027 — a sequence covered in our report on <a href="/blog/nasa-artemis-acceleration-2028-moon-landing-update/">NASA's accelerated Artemis plan</a>. On paper, that puts the American landing roughly one to two years ahead of China's "before 2030" goal. On the calendar, both programs face the same physics: lunar windows, hardware readiness, and the unforgiving arithmetic of test flights that cannot be skipped.

The comparison is instructive precisely because the architectures differ so much. NASA's plan pairs the Space Launch System with a commercial lander and a lunar-orbit staging strategy; China's pairs two Long March 10s in direct lunar-orbit rendezvous. NASA's program has already flown a crewed lunar flyby, Artemis II, in April 2026; China's has not yet flown its crew capsule to orbit, though the late-2026 Mengzhou 1 flight is meant to change that. What China has, instead, is a simpler stack — one rocket family doing both jobs — and a test sequence that rehearses the entire mission uncrewed before committing a crew. Which approach reaches the surface first will depend less on announced dates than on which program's next three flights go as planned, and both programs have now published roadmaps detailed enough to be judged against.

There is also a deeper difference in what each landing is for. NASA's Artemis program is explicitly building toward a sustained lunar presence, with the lunar Gateway station and reusable landers as long-term infrastructure. China's program, with its integrated moon project, its Tiangong station experience, and its south-pole robotic scouts, is pointed at the same destination by a different route: a crewed landing as the opening of a surface program, with a rover in the lander from the first mission. The two programs are not racing to the same finish line so much as racing to establish the facts on the ground — or rather, on the regolith — that will shape the next fifty years of lunar exploration.

## What to watch next

The next twelve months will test whether the roadmap survives contact with reality. The first milestone is the Long March 10A's maiden orbital flight carrying Mengzhou 1, targeted for late 2026: a successful flight would put China's new crew capsule in orbit for the first time and validate the medium-lift branch of the rocket family. The second is Chang'e 7's retry, planned for next year, which will show whether the south-pole window problem was a scheduling hiccup or a deeper constraint. The third, further out, is the stand-alone uncrewed Lanyue landing — the step-one flight that must succeed before anything else in the roadmap can proceed, and the flight that will tell the world whether China's lander can do the one thing no test on Earth can fully simulate.

Watch, too, for the quieter signals. The seventeen new standards will start showing up in hardware descriptions and test reports, as contractors build to common interfaces. The near-Earth orbit test missions Wang mentioned for both vehicles will appear on launch manifests, probably with little fanfare, as incremental flights rather than headline missions. And the crew — unnamed so far — will eventually be assigned, trained, and introduced, at which point the program's abstraction will acquire faces. Until then, the roadmap is the story: three steps, two rockets, one rendezvous, and a deadline that China has now explained how it intends to meet.

Seventy years after its space program began, China stood up at its own symposium and described, in unusual detail, how it will send people to the Moon. The plan asks for patience — an uncrewed lander first, a full dress rehearsal second, a crew only third — and it asks for trust that the sequence will hold when the hardware meets the Moon. If it does, the decade will end with two nations having returned people to the lunar surface, by two very different roads, within a few years of each other. If it does not, the world will know exactly which step failed, because China has now told us the order in which it intends to take them. Either way, the race to the Moon has acquired something it lacked for years: a second roadmap detailed enough to follow, flight by flight, all the way to the surface.
