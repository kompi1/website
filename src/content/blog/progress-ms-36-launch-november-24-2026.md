A Soyuz rocket climbed away from the Kazakh steppe on Tuesday carrying the fourth and final Russian cargo ship of 2026, and with it a quiet admission: the International Space Station is changing the way it works because one of its oldest modules can no longer be trusted to hold air.

Progress MS-36 lifted off from the Baikonur Cosmodrome at 10:17 UTC on November 24, 2026, riding a Soyuz-2.1b launcher from Site 31/6 into the same orbital plane as the station. About nine minutes later the uncrewed freighter was in orbit, beginning a two-day chase that is scheduled to end with docking at the Russian segment's Rassvet module at 12:11 UTC on Thursday, November 26 — Thanksgiving Day in the United States.

The flight itself was, by the standards of a spacecraft family that has been flying since 1978, routine. What makes it historic is what happens after docking. For the first time, a Progress freighter will be unloaded at one port and then moved to another before it does its second job — raising the station's orbit. The change is not an experiment. It is the new standard, forced by the permanent closure of a leaking compartment aboard the Zvezda service module, a decision the station's partner nations took in June 2026 after years of chasing a leak they could not stop.

Progress MS-36 is the 189th flight of the Progress series, which began with the Salyut 6 station nearly half a century ago. It is also, in its own way, a marker of how the station's Russian segment is adapting to old age — improvising around failing hardware while the clock ticks toward a planned retirement in 2031, a date that NASA's new leadership has already begun to question.

## A Textbook Launch From Baikonur

The Soyuz-2.1b left Pad 6 at Site 31 in Baikonur at 13:17:46 Moscow time, heading eastward on a ground track inclined 51.67 degrees to the equator — the same inclination as the space station it was chasing. The three-stage, kerosene-and-liquid-oxygen launcher is a direct descendant of the R-7 intercontinental ballistic missile that put Sputnik into orbit, and it remains the workhorse of Russia's human and cargo spaceflight programme.

Tuesday's flight was the 10th Soyuz 2.1b launch of 2026 and the 109th mission overall for the rocket variant, which can deliver up to 8,700 kilograms to low Earth orbit. The Progress spacecraft it carried weighs 7,280 kilograms fully loaded, and the launcher placed it precisely into the station's orbital plane, setting up the standard roughly 48-hour rendezvous profile that Progress missions have used for decades.

The launch came at the end of a long and troubled road to the pad. Progress MS-36 was originally expected to fly on August 17, 2026, then slipped to September 9. The delays traced back to a serious incident at Site 31/6 during the launch of the crewed Soyuz MS-28 mission in November 2025, when the pad's service platform collapsed. The damage shut the site down for months and rippled through Russia's entire launch manifest. Technicians eventually installed a replacement mobile service platform built during Soviet times, which allowed flights from the site to resume with the Progress MS-33 mission on March 22, 2026.

Coverage of the launch was notably sparse. Roscosmos has dramatically reduced its public coverage of its launch campaigns, and NASA's ISS Orbit File Data list of major station events has also disappeared for now. Observers believe the curtailment is linked to recent developments in the war in Ukraine. What is known about the mission comes largely from Russian space programme trackers, NASA's own mission designations, and specialist reporting — a thinner information diet than Progress launches once enjoyed.

Progress MS-36 is identified by NASA as Progress 97P, and it carries the Russian production number 466. Its crew — there is none, of course — is replaced by cargo, and the spacecraft will serve Expedition 75, the crew currently living and working aboard the station.

## What Is Aboard: 2.4 Tonnes of Cargo

The freighter's manifest is a small inventory of what it takes to keep seven people alive and working 400 kilometres above Earth for months at a time. RussianSpaceWeb, which tracks the Russian programme in detail, lists a total cargo mass of 2,427 kilograms, while NASA Spaceflight puts the figure at roughly 2,500 kilograms. The difference reflects different ways of counting the same load, but the contents are consistent: food, water, fuel, air, clothing, medicine, and the hardware that keeps the station's systems running.

| Cargo category | Mass | Contents |
|---|---:|---|
| Pressurised cargo | 1,206 kg | Maintenance and upgrade hardware (327 kg), science equipment (303 kg), food (261 kg), clothing and hygiene items (247 kg), medical supplies (41 kg), crew-support items (27 kg) |
| Propellant | 750 kg | Fuel for station reboost burns and attitude control |
| Water | 420 kg | Drinking water and life-support supplies |
| Gases | 51 kg | Oxygen and nitrogen for the station atmosphere |
| **Total** | **2,427 kg** | |

NASA Spaceflight's account of the manifest puts the dry cargo figure slightly higher, at about 1,400 kilograms, and notes that the load includes a robot among the scientific equipment, along with the same 420 kilograms of water and the fuel and gases for reboost and life support. The spacecraft is also flying a modified propellant tank isolation valve, a piece of hardware being validated in flight for use in future spacecraft — a small reminder that even a routine resupply flight doubles as a test bed.

The pressurised cargo tells its own story about station life. More than a quarter of a tonne of food. Nearly as much again in clothing and hygiene supplies — the unglamorous logistics of a crew that cannot do laundry in orbit and must throw away its clothes. More than 300 kilograms of scientific equipment and materials for experiments, the reason the station exists at all. And 327 kilograms of maintenance and upgrade hardware, the spare parts and tools for a machine that has been flying for more than a quarter of a century and needs constant attention.

Once the freighter docks, the crew will spend days unloading it, item by item, through the hatch. Then, as with every Progress, the empty spacecraft will be reloaded with rubbish — the trash of months of orbital living — before it is sent to burn up in the atmosphere. Progress MS-34, currently docked at Zvezda's aft port, is already loaded with trash and scheduled to depart and deorbit in the near future, clearing the way for the new arrival's second act.

## The New Two-Port Dance

The most consequential thing about Progress MS-36 is not what it carries but where it docks — and where it goes next. Under the new procedure, the freighter will dock at the Rassvet module, where the crew will unload it. Then, once empty, it will undock, fly around the station, and re-dock at the aft port of the Zvezda service module. Only there, at the back of the station and aligned with its main axis, will it fire its engines to raise the station's orbit.

That two-step choreography is new, and it will be the standard process for all Progress spacecraft from now on. The reason is simple and stark: the transfer chamber at the back of Zvezda — known as the PrK, the small compartment between Zvezda's pressurised hull and its aft docking port — is permanently closed.

The old way and the new way differ in one respect that matters enormously to the crew: where they can open the hatch.

| | Old procedure | New procedure (from MS-36) |
|---|---|---|
| Docking port | Zvezda aft port | Rassvet module |
| Crew unloading | Through the PrK transfer chamber | Through open hatches of the Rassvet module |
| Reboost burns | From Zvezda aft, on the station's main axis | Freighter relocates to Zvezda aft after unloading |
| Number of dockings | One | Two |
| PrK status | In use as a passageway | Permanently closed |

The change was forced by a problem that has haunted the Russian segment for years: a chronic air leak in the PrK. The compartment has developed microcracks over years of thermal cycling and structural stress, and the leak has been growing. Numerous attempts to isolate it did not work. In May and June 2026, the leaks grew larger still.

The station's partner nations decided in June 2026 to close the PrK for good. Roscosmos initially wanted to attempt a structural repair, which would have involved cutting a bracket inside the compartment. NASA, assessing the risk, ordered its astronauts to take shelter in a Crew Dragon spacecraft if the repair went ahead. The repair was never performed.

A NASA risk matrix rated the PrK leaks at the highest possible level: a red five-by-five, five for likelihood and five for consequence. That is the language of a risk that is both certain and potentially catastrophic, and it explains why the partners chose the conservative path — sealing the compartment and redesigning how the station's most frequent visitor does its work.

There is a certain irony in the solution. The aft port of Zvezda remains the best place in the entire station from which to fire a freighter's engines, because it sits on the station's main axis and pushes the whole structure cleanly forward. So the new procedure keeps the reboost burns exactly where they were. What changes is only the unloading: the crew now empties the freighter at Rassvet, and the spacecraft then makes a second, short flight to Zvezda's tail. The station's logistics have been re-plumbed around a door that will never open again.

The change was not quite as simple as the first announcements suggested. Initial information had indicated that Progress MS-36 would dock with the Poisk module, with the current resident of Poisk — Progress MS-35 — moving over to Zvezda's aft port to free the space. The final plan, confirmed in reporting updated after launch, has MS-36 docking at Rassvet instead, with MS-35 remaining at Poisk and MS-34 holding the Zvezda aft port until it departs. The shuffle of three freighters around four ports is itself a measure of how carefully the traffic must now be managed.

## The Leak That Changed the Rules

To understand why a small compartment matters so much, it helps to understand what Zvezda is. The service module is the heart of the Russian segment — its living quarters, its flight control centre, its life-support hub. It was launched in 2000, and its design is based on the core module of the Mir space station, the base block that flew for 15 years, from 1986 to 2001, before being deorbited. It was, at the time, the longest any module of its type had spent in orbit. Zvezda has now been in space for more than 26 years — eleven years longer than its Mir ancestor lasted.

The PrK is a small transfer chamber at the aft end of Zvezda, a vestibule between the module's main pressurised volume and its rear docking port. For years it served as a passageway: cosmonauts and cargo passed through it to reach visiting spacecraft docked at the back of the station. But the compartment sits at a structural junction, and over decades of heating and cooling as the station passes in and out of sunlight every 90 minutes, tiny cracks opened in its hull.

The leak was first noticed years ago and managed for a long time as a slow, acceptable loss of air — the station's reserves and resupply flights could keep up with it. But leaks in a pressurised structure rarely get better. They grow. By the spring of 2026 the rate of loss was increasing, and the June decision to close the compartment permanently was, in effect, an admission that the station's managers could no longer guarantee the PrK would hold.

The closure is one of the clearest examples yet of the station showing its age. The International Space Station was designed for a 15-year life. It has now been continuously occupied for more than 26 years, and its oldest modules are flying well beyond anything their designers planned for. The PrK is not the only ageing system — the station's solar arrays, radiators, and life-support equipment all require constant maintenance — but it is the one that has forced the most visible change in how the station operates day to day.

NASA's current administrator, Jared Isaacman, has hinted that the station's planned retirement in 2031 may not be the final word, saying he does not want a gap between the International Space Station and the commercial stations meant to replace it. Whether that means an extension, and whether the Russian segment's ageing hardware can support one, are questions that the PrK saga makes harder to answer with confidence.

## A Long Road to the Pad

| Date | Milestone |
|---|---|
| November 2025 | Service platform collapses at Site 31/6 during the Soyuz MS-28 launch, shutting the pad down for months |
| March 22, 2026 | Flights from Site 31 resume with Progress MS-33, using a replacement Soviet-era mobile service platform |
| June 2026 | Station partners decide to permanently close Zvezda's leaking PrK transfer chamber |
| August 17, 2026 | Progress MS-36 originally scheduled to launch — date slips |
| September 9, 2026 | Revised launch target — slips again |
| November 24, 2026, 10:17 UTC | Progress MS-36 lifts off from Site 31/6 on Soyuz-2.1b |
| November 26, 2026, 12:11 UTC | Docking planned at the Rassvet module |
| 2027 | Undocking and deorbit planned after unloading, relocation, and reboost duties |

The timeline of Progress MS-36 is also a timeline of Baikonur's recovery from one of the worst pad accidents in the cosmodrome's recent history. The collapse of the service platform at Site 31 during the Soyuz MS-28 launch in November 2025 did more than damage a structure: it closed Russia's busiest launch pad for months and forced a cascade of delays across the manifest. The replacement platform — a mobile service structure built in Soviet times — was pressed into service, and flights resumed only in March 2026.

MS-36 was one of the casualties of that cascade, sliding from August to September and then to late November. In the meantime, the station's other cargo traffic kept moving: American Dragon freighters and the Japanese HTV-X have taken up some of the slack in recent years, and the station's logistics are now genuinely international in a way they were not when Progress flew alone.

The delay also meant that MS-36 arrived at the station in a particularly busy week. The <a href="/blog/spacex-crs-35-dragon-docking/">SpaceX Dragon on the CRS-35 mission</a> undocked and splashed down earlier in the same week, and <a href="/blog/northrop-grumman-crs-25-cygnus-xl-launch-november-29/">Northrop Grumman's Cygnus XL freighter</a> — the S.S. James A. "Jim" Lovell, named for the Apollo 13 commander — is scheduled to launch on a Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral on Sunday, November 29, on mission CRS NG-25, arriving at the station on December 1. For a few days, the station's visiting-vehicle traffic reads like a timetable: a Dragon leaving, a Progress arriving, a Cygnus on its way.

## A Busy Week at the Station

That traffic is worth pausing on, because it shows how the station's supply chain has changed. When Progress MS-36's predecessors first flew, the Russian freighter was the station's lifeline. Today it is one of several. The American commercial cargo programme — SpaceX's Dragon and Northrop Grumman's Cygnus — now carries a large share of the load, and Japan's HTV-X has joined the rotation as well.

The current Russian segment traffic illustrates the choreography. Progress MS-35 occupies the Poisk module. Progress MS-34 is docked at Zvezda's aft port, loaded with trash and awaiting its departure and destructive re-entry. The crewed Soyuz MS-29 is docked to the Prichal module, which is itself attached to the Nauka science module. Into this crowded parking lot, MS-36 must now find its slot at Rassvet — and then, days later, move to Zvezda's tail once MS-34 has gone.

The crew that will do all this unloading is Expedition 75, the current long-duration crew aboard the station. For them, the arrival of a Progress is both a delivery and a workload: every kilogram of cargo must be unpacked, stowed, and logged, and every kilogram of trash must be packed back into the freighter for disposal. It is the orbital equivalent of moving house, performed in weightlessness, and it is one of the least glamorous and most essential jobs in human spaceflight.

The new two-port procedure adds a complication to that routine. The relocation manoeuvre — undocking from Rassvet, flying to a holding position, and re-docking at Zvezda — is not new in itself; Progress freighters have relocated between ports before, and the spacecraft is designed for it. But it is the first time the relocation has been the plan from the start rather than an exception, and it will be repeated on every Progress flight from now on. What was once an occasional manoeuvre is now the standard operating procedure for the station's most frequent visitor.

## An Aging Station, an Uncertain Future

Progress MS-36's mission is, in the end, a story about a station growing old gracefully — or at least, growing old while still working. The International Space Station is supposed to be <a href="/blog/goodbye-iss-deorbit-commercial-stations/">retired by 2031</a>, deliberately deorbited into a remote stretch of ocean. Between now and then, its managers must keep a machine built from modules launched between 1998 and 2021 flying safely, and the PrK is a reminder that the oldest parts of the machine are the ones most likely to surprise them.

The pattern is familiar to anyone who has maintained an old house or an old car: the failures are rarely dramatic. They are slow, cumulative, and expensive. A seal that no longer seals. A crack that grows a fraction of a millimetre a year. A compartment that must be closed because the cost of fixing it — in risk, if not in money — has become too high. The station's response to each of these is the same: work around it. Close the compartment. Move the docking. Change the procedure. Keep flying.

That adaptability is one of the station's genuine achievements. A spacecraft designed in the 1990s, assembled in orbit by crews from five space agencies, is still being reconfigured in 2026 to cope with problems its designers never anticipated. The two-port Progress procedure is a small piece of orbital improvisation, but it is the kind of improvisation that has kept the station alive for a decade past its design life.

Whether the station gets more time is now an open question. Administrator Isaacman's remarks about avoiding a gap before commercial stations are ready suggest that the 2031 retirement date may be revisited, and the commercial stations themselves — the successors that are supposed to take over low Earth orbit — are still years from flying crews. If the International Space Station does fly beyond 2031, it will do so with more closed compartments, more workarounds, and more procedures like the one Progress MS-36 is pioneering. The freighter now chasing the station is, in that sense, a preview of the station's future: still flying, still supplied, and increasingly held together by ingenuity rather than by its original design.

For now, though, the mission ahead is simpler. Dock on Thursday. Unload. Relocate. Raise the orbit. Burn up. It is the unglamorous, indispensable rhythm of keeping people alive in space — a rhythm that has now been running, in one form or another, since the first Progress docked with Salyut 6 in 1978. Progress MS-36, the 189th of its line, keeps that rhythm going, and it does so with a new step in the dance, learned the hard way, from a leak that would not stop.

## What Happens Next

Docking is planned for 12:11 UTC on Thursday, November 26. After the crew opens the hatches and confirms a hard dock, the unloading will begin — days of careful work, moving food, water, hardware, and science equipment into the station's stowage. The freighter will remain docked for months, as Progress missions do, serving first as a pantry and then as a rubbish bin. Its undocking is expected in 2027.

The relocation to Zvezda's aft port will come after unloading is complete and Progress MS-34 has departed. From there, MS-36's engines will perform the periodic reboost burns that counteract the drag of the thin upper atmosphere, keeping the station at its operating altitude. It is a job that has become more important as the station ages: the lower the orbit sinks, the more drag it encounters, and the more fuel it takes to climb back up.

Meanwhile, the station's traffic continues. The Cygnus XL freighter launches on November 29 and is due to be captured by the station's robotic arm on December 1. The crew of Expedition 75 will have barely finished unpacking one freighter before the next one arrives. That, too, is the rhythm of the station: a delivery every few weeks, a constant flow of supplies from Earth, and a crew that keeps the whole improbable machine flying — one closed compartment, one new procedure, and one freighter at a time.
