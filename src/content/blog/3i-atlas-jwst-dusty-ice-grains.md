A comet from another star system has just handed astronomers one of the most revealing clues yet about how planets are built — and the clue is almost invisibly small. In a study published on October 26, 2026, in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, researchers using NASA's James Webb Space Telescope report that the ice streaming off interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS is not the clean, relatively pure ice seen in most comets of our own solar system. Instead, it comes in tiny grains, about a micron across or smaller, in which water ice is intimately mixed with a large amount of rocky dust. That dusty recipe, the team found, looks strikingly like the ice on some of the frozen worlds orbiting beyond Neptune in our own solar system — a resemblance that hints the raw material of planets may be assembled in much the same way around very different stars.

The finding does not overturn what astronomers already knew about 3I/ATLAS. It refines it, in the way that only a careful look at a spectrum can. The comet remains the third interstellar object ever confirmed passing through our solar system, after 1I/'Oumuamua in 2017 and 2I/Borisov in 2019. It was discovered on July 1, 2025, by the ATLAS survey telescope in Chile, and it swung past the Sun on October 29, 2025, before heading back out toward interstellar space, never to return. What is new is the level of detail with which scientists can now describe the individual grains it shed along the way — grains so small that dozens of them could fit across the width of a human hair, yet each one carrying a chemical fingerprint of the distant disk of gas and dust in which the comet was born.

The study was led by Dr. Silvia Protopapa of the Southwest Research Institute, with Dr. Martin Cordiner of NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center and the Catholic University of America as principal investigator of the Webb observing program and a co-author of the paper. Its full title, "JWST Reveals Refractory-rich Water Ice in Interstellar Comet 3I/ATLAS: Evidence for a Continuum of Grain Properties across Protoplanetary Disks," states the conclusion almost as plainly as the press release does. "Refractory-rich" is the technical term for what the rest of us would call dusty: the ice grains contain a substantial fraction of refractory material, the rocky, heat-resistant stuff that does not evaporate easily, mixed in with the frozen water. The second half of the title carries the broader claim — that grains like these may form a continuum, a smooth range from relatively pure ice to ice heavily laden with dust, across the planet-forming disks of many different stars, with 3I/ATLAS sitting toward the dusty end of that range.

## Three looks, before and after the Sun

The strength of the new result lies in the way the observations were made. Rather than a single snapshot, the Webb program observed 3I/ATLAS three times between August 2025 and April 2026, bracketing the comet's closest approach to the Sun. The instrument was Webb's Near-Infrared Spectrograph, known as NIRSpec, used in its prism mode, which splits incoming light into its component wavelengths like a prism splitting sunlight into a rainbow. Each wavelength carries information about the material that emitted or absorbed the light, and the resulting spectrum is, in effect, a chemical fingerprint.

Cordiner explained that the program was deliberately designed to be ready the moment an active interstellar comet appeared. "We designed this program to be ready the moment an active interstellar comet was found and to follow it over time," he said in the Southwest Research Institute announcement. "Our goal was to characterize the ice, dust and gas that the comet releases, and so learn about the chemistry and formation of planetesimals — the building blocks of planets — around other stars." That readiness mattered. Interstellar objects arrive without warning and leave on hyperbolic trajectories that never bring them back. A program that had to be designed from scratch after discovery would have missed the comet's most active months. Instead, Webb was watching in August 2025, while the comet was still inbound, and kept watching through April 2026, months after perihelion, as it receded into the dark.

As 3I/ATLAS warmed on its approach to the Sun, gas escaping from its interior carried ice and dust grains outward into the coma, the glowing cloud that surrounds a comet's nucleus. "As 3I/ATLAS warms up, gas escaping from its interior carries ice and dust grains into the surrounding cloud, or coma," Protopapa said. "Those grains hold clues to the conditions where the comet was born." That is the central idea behind the whole study, and behind much of interstellar-object science in general. A comet is not just a beautiful visitor. It is a preserved sample of the disk in which it formed, delivered free of charge to our telescopes. Unlike an exoplanet, whose composition must be inferred from light-years away, an interstellar comet brings the actual material of another planetary system into our neighborhood, where instruments like Webb can examine it grain by grain.

## The fingerprint that did not fit

Water ice leaves a distinctive signature in infrared light. Astronomers look for three absorption bands — places in the spectrum where ice absorbs light, creating a dip — near wavelengths of 3 microns, 1.5 microns, and 2.0 microns. In most icy comets, all three are present: a deep, strong band near 3 microns, driven by the fundamental vibration of the oxygen-hydrogen bond in water molecules, plus two weaker but clearly detectable bands at the shorter wavelengths. The relative strengths of those bands encode information about the size of the ice grains and how pure they are, because light interacts differently with a large, clean crystal of ice than it does with a tiny, dirty one.

In 3I/ATLAS, the pattern broke. The 3-micron band was strong, exactly as expected for water ice in the coma. But the 1.5- and 2.0-micron bands were very weak or missing entirely, unlike in most icy comets, which show all three. "That combination was a puzzle," Protopapa said. "The strong 3-micron band points to water ice in the coma. But the weak or absent shorter-wavelength bands suggest the ice is not in the form we usually see in solar system comets."

A puzzle, in spectroscopy, is a gift. It means the data are good enough to rule out the obvious explanation, and the team had to work out what kind of grain could produce exactly this combination of a strong long-wavelength band and vanished short-wavelength bands. The answer came from detailed modeling of how light scatters off grains of different sizes and compositions. The models that fit the observations best pointed to grains about a micron or smaller — roughly a millionth of a meter — made of water ice mixed with dust. In grains that small and that dusty, light travels only a short distance through the ice before it is scattered or absorbed by the dust mixed into it. That short path is enough to produce the deep 3-micron absorption, which is intrinsically very strong, but not enough to build up the weaker 1.5- and 2.0-micron bands, which need light to travel farther through pure ice before they become prominent. In other words, the missing bands were not evidence that water ice was absent. They were evidence that the ice was dirty, and that the dirt was doing the talking.

That distinction matters, because it is easy to misread a spectrum. A weaker band can mean less of a substance, or it can mean the same substance in a different physical form. Here, the modeling showed it was the form, not the amount, that had changed. Ice grains in other comets are similarly small, the team noted, but they are thought to be relatively pure, perhaps with only a small amount of dust mixed in. 3I/ATLAS, by contrast, carries its dust proudly. "The grains of 3I/ATLAS are about the same size as those in our own comets," Protopapa said. "What seems to set them apart is how much dust is mixed into the ice."

## A comet that changed as it flew

The three-epoch design of the Webb program paid off a second time, because the grains did not stay the same throughout the observations. By the final observation in April 2026, as the comet was moving away from the Sun, the data favored an additional population of ice-rich grains — still around a micron in size, but larger than those inferred from the earlier observations, and richer in ice relative to dust. The coma, in other words, evolved. The material streaming off the comet after its solar encounter was not identical to the material it shed on the way in.

The team is careful not to over-interpret that change. Dr. Michael Kelley of the University of Maryland, the paper's second author, laid out the two competing explanations plainly. "We can't yet tell whether the changes over time are the result of the Sun heating the grains differently at each observation, or of new material exposed as the comet's surface eroded near the Sun," Kelley said. "Both are possible, and telling them apart is one of the open questions we aim to address." That honesty is part of what makes the result trustworthy. A comet near the Sun is a violent, changing environment: its surface erodes, fresh layers are exposed, and the heating conditions shift as its distance from the Sun changes. Any of those effects could alter the grain population that Webb sees. Disentangling them will require more observations, more modeling, and probably more interstellar comets to compare against.

The evolution also connects this study to the broader story of 3I/ATLAS that has unfolded over the past year. Earlier studies, including work published in October 2026, showed the comet shedding dust at a furious rate around perihelion, its surface apparently stripped by the Sun's heat — a story told in detail in <a href="/blog/3i-atlas-interstellar-comet-one-year-later/">One Year After Its Solar Plunge, Interstellar Comet 3I/ATLAS Is Fading Into the Dark — But Its Secrets Keep Surfacing</a>. The new Webb result adds a microscopic chapter to that macroscopic story: not just how much material the comet lost, but what that material was made of, grain by grain, and how its composition shifted as the encounter progressed. The two lines of evidence point in the same direction. This was a comet being actively reworked by sunlight, and Webb caught the reworking in progress.

## The surprise resemblance: worlds beyond Neptune

The most far-reaching part of the study came when the team stopped comparing 3I/ATLAS to other comets and started comparing it to something else entirely: trans-Neptunian objects, the icy bodies that orbit beyond Neptune and are thought to preserve material from the earliest days of our solar system. Webb has observed many of these distant worlds, and their spectra provided a ready-made library for comparison.

The resemblance was striking. The shape of the 3-micron water-ice band in 3I/ATLAS matches that seen in several groups of trans-Neptunian objects, especially those whose spectra also show clear signs of carbon dioxide ice and organic compounds. Like 3I/ATLAS, those objects lack strong 1.5- and 2.0-micron water-ice bands. The team is precise about what the resemblance does and does not mean. It lies in the water-ice band, not in the carbon dioxide: the comparison is about the form the water ice takes, not a claim that 3I/ATLAS shares the full chemistry of those worlds. But the shared band shape suggests a shared grain physics — tiny, dusty ice grains — in bodies that formed in very different places.

"At first glance, 3I/ATLAS looks different from the icy comets we know," Protopapa said. "But when we placed it alongside distant icy worlds in our own solar system, it no longer looked out of place. This suggests that the icy building blocks of planets, here and around other stars, may share ice in the form of tiny, micron-scale grains, ranging from relatively pure ice to ice mixed with dust, with 3I/ATLAS toward the dusty end."

That sentence contains the paper's big idea, and it is worth unpacking slowly. Planets form from planetesimals, small bodies that grow by sticking together in the disk of gas and dust around a young star. The properties of those planetesimals — how big their grains are, how much ice versus rock they contain, how they clump together — help determine what kind of planets eventually emerge: rocky worlds, gas giants, ice giants, or something in between. If the icy building blocks around other stars were wildly different from ours, then planet formation might proceed very differently elsewhere, and our solar system might be an oddity. If, instead, the building blocks share a common range of properties — the same tiny grain sizes, the same continuum from pure ice to dusty ice — then the process of building planets may be more universal than it first appears, even if the finished planets look different.

The authors frame that conclusion cautiously, as they should. "If confirmed, this shared range of grain properties could indicate that the physical form of water ice is established early, in planet-forming disks, and is at least partly preserved in the icy bodies that form there." The words "if confirmed" do real work in that sentence. One comet, however well observed, cannot prove a universal rule. It can suggest one, and it can set the terms for the test. Future Webb observations of comets in our solar system and of newly discovered interstellar visitors will help test these interpretations and determine whether 3I/ATLAS is typical or an outlier. That is how this kind of science advances: not with a single decisive measurement, but with a growing sample, each new object either strengthening the pattern or breaking it.

## Why dusty ice matters for planet formation

To see why grain composition matters so much, it helps to picture what a planet-forming disk actually is. Around a young star, gas and dust swirl in a flattened disk, and within that disk, microscopic grains collide and stick, growing first into pebbles, then into boulders, then into planetesimals kilometers across, and eventually into planets. The stickiness of those grains, the speed at which they drift inward toward the star, and the way they fragment when they collide all depend on what they are made of. Pure water ice behaves differently from ice mixed with rock. A grain's composition affects how it absorbs and radiates heat, how it responds to light pressure, and how efficiently it can grow without shattering.

A continuum of grain properties, rather than two distinct families of "clean" and "dirty" ice, would make sense in that picture. Disks are not uniform. Temperature, density, and chemistry vary with distance from the star and with time as the disk evolves. Grains forming in one part of a disk might end up icier than grains forming in another, and grains that are processed by heating, collisions, or radiation might have their ice-to-dust ratio altered over millions of years. Finding an interstellar comet at the dusty end of that continuum, while solar system comets cluster toward the purer end, is consistent with a universe in which the same processes produce a range of outcomes, and different systems sample that range differently.

There is also a deeper, more philosophical point hiding in the result. For decades, astronomers have wondered whether our solar system is typical. The discovery of thousands of exoplanets has shown that planetary systems come in bewildering variety — hot Jupiters hugging their stars, compact systems of rocky worlds, gas giants on wildly eccentric orbits — and it would be easy to conclude that the raw material must vary just as wildly. The 3I/ATLAS result points the other way, at least for one ingredient. The ice itself, at the micron scale, may be remarkably similar wherever it forms. The diversity of finished planets might then arise less from different starting ingredients and more from different histories: different disk masses, different migration paths, different collisions, different timing. That is a testable idea, and it gives future interstellar comet studies a clear job to do.

## A year of revelations, and what comes next

It is worth stepping back to appreciate how much 3I/ATLAS has already taught us in little more than a year. When it was discovered, it was a faint moving dot. Within months, it had become the most intensively studied interstellar object in history, observed by Webb, by Hubble, by Mars orbiters that caught it from a different vantage point, and by ground-based telescopes around the world. Studies have probed its gas chemistry, its dust production, its rotation, and now the microscopic composition of its ice grains. Each study has added a layer, and each layer has made the comet feel slightly less alien and slightly more comprehensible — which is, in a sense, the most alien thing about it. An object from another star system turns out to be built from the same kind of dusty ice as the frozen worlds at the edge of our own.

That comprehensibility should not be mistaken for completeness. Plenty about 3I/ATLAS remains unknown, including the star system it came from, which astronomers may never identify with certainty. Its exact nucleus size, its full chemical inventory, and the details of how its surface evolved during perihelion are all still being worked out. And the open question Kelley identified — whether the changing grain population reflects heating or newly exposed material — will not be answered by re-analyzing the same three spectra alone. It needs new data, from new objects, observed with the same care.

The good news is that the machinery for gathering those data is now in place. Webb's interstellar-object program was built to be ready, and it proved it could deliver. Surveys like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory are scanning the sky with unprecedented depth and cadence, increasing the odds that the next interstellar visitor will be found early, while it is still far away and faint, giving telescopes maximum time to prepare. And the experience of 3I/ATLAS — including serendipitous discoveries along the way, such as <a href="/blog/euclid-discovers-first-comet-c-2026-s1/">The Accidental Comet: How ESA's Dark-Energy Telescope Stumbled Upon Its First New Comet</a> — has shown that discovery can come from unexpected instruments as well as dedicated surveys. The next visitor may be found by a telescope built to study dark energy, or by an observatory mapping the sky for entirely different reasons, and the follow-up network that 3I/ATLAS trained will be ready for it.

## Reading a comet like a history book

There is a useful analogy for what Protopapa and her colleagues have done. A comet's coma is like the pages of a history book that is being written as it flies. The grains streaming off the nucleus are fragments of the original disk, preserved in deep freeze for billions of years, then released, briefly, into sunlight. Webb's spectrograph reads those pages not by looking at pictures, but by measuring light — by finding the dips where water ice absorbs infrared radiation and reasoning backward, through models of scattering and absorption, to the size and composition of the grains that made them. It is forensic work at a distance of hundreds of millions of kilometers, and the fact that it works at all is a testament to how far infrared astronomy has come.

The specific forensic result — grains about a micron or smaller, refractory-rich, with a strong 3-micron band and weak or missing shorter-wavelength bands — will now enter the literature as a benchmark. Future papers on interstellar comets will compare their spectra to 3I/ATLAS, just as this paper compared 3I/ATLAS to trans-Neptunian objects and to solar system comets. If the next interstellar comet shows pure, clean ice grains, that will be interesting precisely because it differs from 3I/ATLAS. If it shows dusty grains too, the continuum idea will gain strength. Either way, 3I/ATLAS has set the standard against which the next visitor will be judged, and that is a lasting contribution even though the comet itself is already gone, receding into the dark between the stars.

It is also a reminder of why Webb was built. The telescope's infrared vision, its spectrographs, and its ability to observe faint, moving targets were all designed for problems exactly like this one: reading the chemistry of objects too distant, too dim, or too fleeting for earlier telescopes to characterize. The broader story of what Webb has achieved in recent years — from baby galaxies to exoplanet atmospheres — is told in <a href="/blog/webb-telescope-2026-discoveries/">Webb's Breakthrough Year: Baby Galaxies, Impossible Planets, and the Clearest Alien World Ever Seen</a>. The 3I/ATLAS ice-grain study belongs in that story not as a headline-grabbing image, but as something quieter and, in the long run, perhaps more important: a precise measurement, carefully modeled, that changes how scientists think about a whole class of objects.

## The dusty end of a universal spectrum

If there is a single sentence to carry away from this study, it is Protopapa's summary of where 3I/ATLAS sits: toward the dusty end of a continuum that stretches from relatively pure ice to ice mixed with dust, shared across planet-forming disks. That framing does two things at once. It acknowledges that 3I/ATLAS is different — dustier than the comets we know best — and it insists that the difference is a matter of degree, not of kind. The comet is not built from exotic material unknown in our solar system. It is built from familiar material, in an unfamiliar proportion, and that proportion matches what we already see in the frozen worlds beyond Neptune.

That is, in the end, a profoundly reassuring result for anyone who wonders whether the universe is comprehensible. A visitor from another star, carrying ice formed in another disk, around another sun, turns out to be legible in the same language as our own comets and our own distant icy worlds. Its grains are tiny. Its ice is dusty. Its spectrum, once puzzling, now makes sense. And the sense it makes points to a shared recipe for planet building, written in micron-sized grains of ice and dust, repeated — with variations — around stars we will never visit, in disks we will never see directly, but whose products occasionally wander through our sky, trailing a coma of clues behind them.

3I/ATLAS will not come back. Its grains, though, have already done their work. They have shown that the building blocks of planets may be more alike than different, that dusty ice is not an anomaly but a point on a spectrum, and that a telescope designed to be ready for the unexpected can, when the unexpected arrives, read its chemistry grain by grain. The next interstellar visitor, whenever it appears, will be met by a science that is ready — and by a question that 3I/ATLAS has now sharpened to a point: not whether other stars build planets from the same stuff, but how far along the dusty-to-pure spectrum their building blocks lie, and what that tells us about the worlds they eventually become.
