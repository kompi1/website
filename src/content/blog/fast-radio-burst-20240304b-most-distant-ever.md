Somewhere in the direction of the distant cosmos, roughly 10 billion years before human beings existed, a star flared into an event so violent that a single millisecond of it outshone entire galaxies. The radio light it released set out across a universe only three billion years old — a universe still assembling its first great structures — and travelled through nearly all of cosmic history before washing, almost unimaginably faintly, over an array of dishes in the South African desert.

That flash, now designated FRB 20240304B, is the most distant fast radio burst ever detected. Announced this week in the journal *Science* by a team led by Dr Manisha Caleb and Dr Themiya Nanayakkara of the University of Sydney, it more than doubles the previous distance record and turns one of astronomy's most mysterious phenomena into a precision instrument for studying the young universe.

## The burst that broke the record

Fast radio bursts are among the most perplexing phenomena in modern astronomy. Each lasts only milliseconds, yet in that fleeting instant it can release as much energy as the Sun emits over days, all of it in the radio part of the spectrum. Since their accidental discovery in archival telescope data in 2007, when a team led by Duncan Lorimer reported the first burst in *Science*, catalogued bursts have grown from a handful to more than 10,000, according to recent tallies. Some repeat; others flash once and never again. Their physical origins remain contested.

What makes the new burst special is simply where — and when — it happened. FRB 20240304B was caught on March 4, 2024, by the MeerTRAP project, a real-time search for cosmic transients running on South Africa's MeerKAT radio telescope, a 64-dish array that ranks among the most sensitive radio observatories on Earth. MeerKAT found the flash, but it could not, on its own, say what had produced it. Pinpointing an FRB's origin requires finding the galaxy it came from, and that meant looking deeper than any ground-based telescope can manage.

The largest ground-based telescopes saw nothing at the burst's position. So the team turned to NASA's James Webb Space Telescope. Webb's near-infrared camera found a faint host galaxy where nothing had been visible before, and its spectrograph measured the redshift of the galaxy's light: 2.148. That number is the reason this discovery matters. It pins the burst to a time just three billion years after the Big Bang, when the universe was roughly a fifth of its present age.

## Doubling the record

The previous record-holder, FRB 20220610A, was found in October 2023 by Australia's ASKAP telescope at a redshift of about 1 — its light had been travelling for roughly eight billion years. The new burst more than doubles that distance. It arrived at Earth after crossing more than 10 billion light-years of expanding space, passing through roughly 80 percent of cosmic history.

"This is an extraordinary glimpse into the distant Universe," said Caleb, of the Sydney Institute for Astronomy in the university's School of Physics. "We have caught a fast radio burst from a time when the Universe was only about three billion years old, and we have used that brief flash of radio light to learn about the matter it has travelled through over billions of years."

The record matters not only because records matter. Distance is destiny in astronomy: the farther a burst originates, the earlier the cosmic epoch it illuminates, and the longer the column of intergalactic matter its signal must pass through on the way here. Each far-flung burst is a beacon shone down a corridor of space no other instrument can see into directly.

## A brief history of a mystery

To appreciate what the new burst achieves, it helps to know how far fast radio bursts have come. The first one was discovered almost by accident: in 2007, Duncan Lorimer's team dug a powerful radio pulse out of old telescope data and reported it in *Science*. It was clearly coming from outside the Milky Way, but for years nobody could say where. Its host galaxy was unknown, and its cause was a matter of speculation.

The field's big break came in April 2020, when astronomers caught a burst from SGR 1935+2154, a magnetar inside our own galaxy — the first time a known object had produced something recognisably close to a fast radio burst. A month later, Jean-Pierre Macquart's team used just six bursts with known host galaxies to locate the universe's "missing" ordinary matter, finding the intergalactic plasma that cosmology said had to be out there. The distance record then fell in October 2023, when Australia's ASKAP telescope traced FRB 20220610A to a host at redshift about 1, its light eight billion years old.

FRB 20240304B is the next step in that staircase — and a particularly long one. Doubling a distance record in a single jump is rare in astronomy. It also demonstrates something the field has hoped for but never proven: that bursts can be found and traced from the universe's adolescence, when the first great assemblies of galaxies were still forming.

## The galaxy that shouldn't have done it

The most interesting part of the story is not the record. It is the galaxy.

By combining sensitive radio observations with Webb's powerful infrared imaging and spectroscopy, the team could do more than detect the burst: they could study where it was born. What they found surprised them. "The galaxy hosting this burst is surprisingly small, metal-poor and undergoing a very active episode of star formation," said Dr Laura Driessen, a co-author at the University of Sydney. "That gives us an important clue about the environments in which FRBs are born and shows that these brief radio flashes can tell us not only about the distant Universe, but also about how galaxies and their stellar populations evolve."

Most galaxies known to host fast radio bursts are large, massive systems. This one was about a thousand times less massive than the team expected, according to reporting on the team's release — a young, metal-poor dwarf in the middle of a ferocious burst of star formation. Its stellar birth rate suggests that most of its stars formed within just 30 million years, a cosmic eyeblink. And it lived during "cosmic noon," the stretch of cosmic history when galaxies across the universe were forming stars faster than at any other time.

That environment is a clue with real teeth, because astronomy's two leading theories about what causes FRBs make sharply different predictions about where the bursts should turn up.

## Magnetars vs. mergers

One idea holds that fast radio bursts come from magnetars — young neutron stars with magnetic fields trillions of times stronger than Earth's, left behind when massive stars explode as supernovae. Magnetars can form within a few million years of the stars that make them, so if they are the source, bursts should appear in young, star-forming galaxies.

The rival idea holds that bursts come from two neutron stars spiraling together and merging, a process that typically takes billions of years. If mergers dominate, bursts should preferentially turn up in older galaxies full of aging stars.

The evidence has been accumulating on the magnetar side for years: in April 2020, astronomers caught a burst from SGR 1935+2154, a magnetar inside our own Milky Way, showing that such objects can produce at least burst-like events. The new result extends that evidence to a much earlier epoch. A merger origin, the team argues, is improbable for a burst born in a galaxy this young and busy — there simply had not been enough time since the galaxy's star formation began for pairs of neutron stars to age, inspiral and collide in large numbers.

Not every burst needs the same explanation. Some fast radio bursts repeat, firing off multiple flashes over days or years, while others are seen once and never again — which is why most researchers expect the observed population to contain more than one kind of engine. But the new host galaxy tilts the balance of evidence: in the young universe, at least, bursts were already being made by young things in young galaxies, long before the mergers of old stellar corpses could take over.

The result pushes the boundaries of how far astronomers can use fast radio bursts as probes of the universe. "In principle, sufficiently powerful bursts could be detectable from the very early Universe," said Kavya Shaji, a co-author and PhD student in the School of Physics. Dr Caleb added: "What is particularly exciting about our result is that we've now demonstrated that we can identify and study an FRB from when the Universe was young."

It is worth noting what Webb did not see. It did not see a magnetar. No telescope could: the burst itself lasted milliseconds, and its progenitor — whatever it was — is far too small and faint to resolve across 10 billion light-years. What Webb saw was the environment, and the environment tells the story: this is a galaxy where magnetars should be common, and it is hard to square with much else.

## A flashlight in the dark between galaxies

Beyond setting a distance record and testing theories of burst origins, FRB 20240304B acted as a cosmic beacon, illuminating the vast reservoirs of gas and matter between galaxies. This is arguably the discovery's deepest scientific value.

As radio waves travel through ionised gas, they are slowed by an almost imperceptible but measurable amount — longer wavelengths lag slightly behind shorter ones, stretching the burst out in frequency. The amount of stretching encodes the total amount of matter the signal encountered along its path. Astronomers call this the dispersion measure, and it turns every fast radio burst into a census-taker: each burst weighs the otherwise invisible matter it has passed through.

That matters because most of the universe's ordinary matter — the stuff of stars, planets and people — does not live in galaxies at all. It hangs in the near-invisible plasma of the intergalactic medium, strung along the cosmic web in filaments between galaxies. For decades this material was a bookkeeping problem: the universe's matter budget said it should be there, but telescopes could barely see it. Fast radio bursts are now the best tool for counting it, functioning as cosmic measuring instruments for the total atomic content of space.

A burst from redshift 2.148 is special in this role because its signal sampled the intergalactic medium across most of cosmic history, including the era of cosmic noon when galaxies were at their most active and the web between them was densest. "We have caught a fast radio burst from a time when the Universe was only about three billion years old, and we have used that brief flash of radio light to learn about the matter it has travelled through over billions of years," Caleb said.

## How the discovery was made

The result is a product of international astronomy operating at its most coordinated. The detection came from MeerTRAP, a project searching for transient phenomena in real time on MeerKAT, whose principal investigator is Professor Ben Stappers of the University of Manchester. Finding the host required Webb, whose unique sensitivity in the infrared made it possible to see a galaxy that was invisible to the largest ground-based telescopes.

"Our results further show the amazing capability of the Webb space telescope where we can push boundaries beyond what was previously possible," said Nanayakkara, who recently joined the University of Sydney.

The discovery also underscores the University of Sydney's growing leadership in radio astronomy: its researchers were involved in the 2023 discovery of what was then the most distant fast radio burst — the very record that FRB 20240304B has now broken. MeerKAT itself is one of the world's most powerful radio facilities and a precursor to the Square Kilometre Array Observatory, now under construction in Australia and South Africa, in whose scientific programs Sydney researchers are playing leading roles. The SKA is expected to transform our understanding of the universe through radio observations of unprecedented sensitivity.

"The next step is to push this frontier further and see how close we can get to the first generations of stars," Stappers said.

## Why this burst will echo for years

The announcement lands at a moment when fast radio bursts are moving from curiosity to tool. For their first decade, FRBs were mysterious flashes that astronomers struggled to explain. Now they are becoming what astronomers call probes — natural instruments scattered through the universe, each one measuring the matter and magnetism of space along its line of sight.

Each new record pushes that probe further back in time. The 2023 record reached eight billion years back. The new one reaches past ten billion, into an era when the galaxies that now fill the cosmos were still under construction. If sufficiently powerful bursts can be detected from the very early universe, as Shaji suggests, then the first generations of stars and the first galaxies may eventually be weighed by the flashes of things we cannot see.

The infrastructure to find them is already taking shape. MeerKAT is a precursor to the Square Kilometre Array Observatory, the giant radio telescope now under construction in Australia and South Africa. When the SKA comes online, its unprecedented sensitivity is expected to detect fainter and more distant transients than anything operating today — and University of Sydney researchers are playing leading roles in the science programs that will use it. MeerTRAP itself runs as a commensal instrument, scanning the sky for flashes in real time while MeerKAT pursues other observations, a strategy that has turned the telescope into one of the most productive FRB machines on Earth. Its principal investigator, Professor Ben Stappers of the University of Manchester, frames the ambition plainly: "The next step is to push this frontier further and see how close we can get to the first generations of stars."

There is a pleasing asymmetry to it. The burst itself — a violence beyond human scale, over in less time than a heartbeat — is gone. But its light, stretched and delayed by everything it met along the way, arrives carrying a record of the journey: a fossil of the cosmic web, written in radio, read out in the South African desert and decoded by a telescope floating a million miles from Earth.

Ten billion years ago, a magnetar — probably — flared in a small galaxy burning with newborn stars. Today we know, and the young universe is a little less dark.
