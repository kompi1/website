For fifteen years, a giant star in a distant spiral galaxy kept doing something that should have been impossible. Every few months it flared up so violently that it looked, from 30 million light-years away, like a supernova — the cataclysmic death of a massive star. And then, instead of dying, it faded, recovered, and did it again. And again. And again. Astronomers counted at least 27 of these outbursts between 2012 and 2026, arriving with the regularity of a clock, roughly every 113 days. The star refused to die, and nobody could explain why it kept exploding.

Now, thanks to NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory, the mystery has an answer. The star, known as AT 2016blu, is not exploding alone. It shares its patch of sky with a hidden companion — either a stellar-mass black hole or a neutron star, the crushed core left behind when a massive star dies — locked in an oval-shaped orbit that brings the two objects close together every 113 days. At each close approach, the companion tears material from the giant star, and that stolen gas, whirling around the compact object at speeds close to the speed of light, heats up until it blazes in X-rays. The X-rays then heat the dense gas surrounding the system, which glows in visible light — producing the brilliant flashes that telescopes on Earth have been recording for a decade and a half.

The result, published in The Astrophysical Journal on November 9, 2026, is the first X-ray detection of this class of erupting star, and the first time one of these objects has been found with a black hole or neutron star companion. It is also a clue to a much bigger puzzle: what really happens to the most massive stars in the final chapters of their lives, before they finally do explode for real.

## The Star That Kept Exploding — and Surviving

AT 2016blu sits in NGC 4559, a spiral galaxy about 30 million light-years from Earth. Its host star is a monster by any measure: astronomers estimate its mass at at least 33 times that of the Sun. It belongs to a rare and volatile family called luminous blue variables, or LBVs — hot, massive stars characterized by large changes in brightness. The most famous member of the family is Eta Carinae, a star in our own Milky Way that has fascinated and worried astronomers for nearly two centuries. AT 2016blu is a special type of luminous blue variable, one that undergoes rapid and repeated increases in brightness so extreme that they resemble supernova explosions.

In a genuine supernova, a massive star collapses onto itself and material hurtles outward into space, and the star is destroyed. AT 2016blu's outbursts look like that from a distance — sudden, brilliant spikes in brightness — but the star is not destroyed. It survives each one. That is why astronomers call it a "supernova impostor": a star that puts on the show of a supernova without paying the price.

"For 15 years, astronomers have been watching this star get really bright and then dim again on a regular basis," said Mojgan Aghakhanloo of the University of Virginia in Charlottesville, who led the study. "Until we were able to get Chandra data at the right time, we couldn't figure out what was making this happen."

The regularity was the strangest part. Between 2012 and 2026, astronomers detected at least 27 bursts of optical light from AT 2016blu, arriving roughly every 113 days — about three times a year. A star does not simply decide to erupt on a schedule. Something had to be driving it, something periodic, something that returned to the same place at the same time, again and again. An orbit was the obvious suspect. But an orbit of what, around what? For years, the evidence stopped there. The visible-light data could show the flashes, but it could not show the engine behind them.

"This star's behavior was confusing at first because it kept acting like a supernova, but clearly it's not because it keeps surviving these outbursts about three times a year," said co-author Núria Torres-Albà, also of the University of Virginia. "To understand the cause of these eruptions, we realized we needed X-ray data that was timed so that we'd observe the star when it was in an outburst."

That timing turned out to be everything. X-ray light is the fingerprint of the most violent processes in the universe — gas heated to millions of degrees as it falls onto a compact object, matter moving at a substantial fraction of the speed of light. If a black hole or a neutron star were lurking beside AT 2016blu, stealing its gas, the theft would leave exactly that kind of fingerprint. But the fingerprint would only be visible during an outburst, when the companion was close and feeding. Catch the star at the wrong moment, and there would be nothing to see.

## A 113-Day Clock

The 113-day rhythm is the heartbeat of the whole story, and it is worth pausing over what it means. AT 2016blu and its companion travel around each other in an oval-shaped orbit, not a circle. That shape matters enormously. In a circular orbit, two stars keep a constant distance, and any interaction between them stays roughly steady. In an oval orbit, the distance changes: the stars sweep far apart, then swing back in close. At the closest approach — the point astronomers call periastron — the companion's gravity reaches deepest into the giant star's outer layers, and material gets pulled away in a rush.

That is the mechanism the new study proposes, and it fits the clock. Every 113 days, the companion swings in close, strips material from the giant star, and the stolen gas spirals around the compact object at very high speeds, close to the speed of light. Friction and compression heat the gas until it shines brightly in X-rays. Meanwhile, the dense gas surrounding the whole system absorbs those X-rays, heats up, and glows in visible light — the optical flash that telescopes on Earth have been catching since 2012.

"We suspected that the bright repeating flashes at optical wavelengths might actually be powered by periodic blasts of high-energy X-rays," said co-author Nathan Smith of the University of Arizona in Tucson. "The dense gas surrounding the system is heated by the X-rays and then glows in the optical. So, we needed to plan the Chandra X-ray observations carefully to be simultaneous with an optical outburst."

It is an elegant explanation, and it makes a prediction that goes beyond this one star. If the optical flashes of erupting stars can be powered by X-rays from a hidden companion, then some of the most dramatic stellar fireworks in the sky may be binary performances rather than solo acts. The giant star supplies the gas; the compact companion supplies the violence. Neither could produce the show alone.

## Chandra Catches It in the Act

The team chose Chandra for a reason no other X-ray telescope could match. Chandra is the only X-ray telescope with vision sharp enough to distinguish AT 2016blu from another nearby bright X-ray source in the same galaxy. In the crowded field of NGC 4559, a blurrier instrument would have blended the two sources together and the signal would have been lost. Chandra's resolution made the measurement possible at all.

But resolution alone was not enough. The observations had to land at exactly the right moment. To collect the optical data that would tell the team when an outburst was underway, astronomers used the Katzman Automatic Imaging Telescope, the Las Cumbres Observatory Global Supernova Project, and observations from the American Association of Variable Star Observers — including contributions from more than 30 amateur astronomers who monitored the star's brightness night after night, year after year. When those observations showed that the star was in outburst in early 2026, Chandra observations were triggered and obtained in March 2026 as part of the observatory's Target of Opportunity program, which is designed to respond quickly to special events.

The payoff was dramatic. Chandra data from March 2026 revealed that AT 2016blu was at least 100 times brighter in X-rays than in Chandra data from 2001 to 2002, which predates the start of the outbursts. A hundredfold jump in X-ray brightness, arriving exactly when the star was flaring in visible light, is the signature the team had been hunting. The best explanation, the researchers concluded, is that material from the star is falling onto a neutron star or black hole during the closest approach in their oval-shaped orbit.

This is what makes the discovery a first on two counts. It is the first X-ray detection of one of this class of erupting stars, and it is the first of these objects found with a black hole or neutron star companion. Until now, the class was defined by what telescopes could see in visible light: repeated, supernova-like flares from a star that refused to die. Now the class has an X-ray identity and a physical cause, and that changes how astronomers will look for the next one.

The Chandra data also revealed something else. AT 2016blu is bright enough in X-rays to be classified as a candidate ultra-luminous X-ray source, or ULX. These are rare, bright X-ray sources found with telescopes like Chandra that astronomers have yet to fully understand. Current thinking holds that some of them contain neutron stars and others contain black holes. The new observations suggest there could be a link between ultra-luminous X-ray sources and the kind of violent stellar eruptions seen in AT 2016blu — especially because many ultra-luminous X-ray sources undergo large changes in X-ray brightness, just as AT 2016blu does.

That link matters because ULXs have been a puzzle in their own right. They shine brighter in X-rays than a single stellar-mass black hole or neutron star should be able to, at least under the simplest assumptions, and astronomers have debated for years what powers them. A system like AT 2016blu, where the X-ray brightness swings by a factor of a hundred as a companion feeds in bursts, offers a concrete example of how such a source can work — and a reminder that the brightest X-ray objects in a galaxy are not always the ones behaving steadily.

## The Hidden Companion: Black Hole or Neutron Star?

One question the new study cannot yet answer is which kind of compact object is doing the stealing. The companion is either a stellar-mass black hole or a neutron star — the dense collapsed core of a massive star. Both are endpoints of stellar evolution, both are extraordinarily dense, and both can pull gas from a neighbor and heat it to X-ray temperatures. Telling them apart from 30 million light-years away, in a system where the companion itself emits no light of its own that telescopes can separate, is a hard problem.

What astronomers can say is how the companion reveals itself: purely through gravity. The giant star's outbursts are timed to the companion's orbit, and the X-ray brightness surges when the two objects are closest. The companion's mass and nature shape the details — how much material is stripped at each pass, how hot the infalling gas gets, how the X-ray brightness rises and falls — and future observations, timed to the 113-day cycle, may be able to narrow it down.

The distinction is worth pursuing because black holes and neutron stars represent two different fates for massive stars, and the companion in this system is a fossil of an earlier chapter of the pair's shared history. Long ago, the companion was itself a massive star. It lived fast, burned through its fuel, and collapsed — leaving behind a black hole or a neutron star while its partner, the star we now call AT 2016blu, kept burning. The two have been dancing ever since, and now the survivor is feeding its dead partner's remains, one close pass at a time.

This kind of system is also a preview of what AT 2016blu itself may become. A star of at least 33 solar masses will not live forever. Its eruptions are already shedding material into space, and its companion is stripping more. Astronomers suspect that much of the brightness-changing behavior seen in stars before they undergo supernova explosions may be caused by systems containing a massive star orbiting a compact object or another massive star. AT 2016blu, caught in the act of erupting on schedule, is a laboratory for that idea — a star whose pre-supernova restlessness can be watched, timed, and measured in real time rather than reconstructed afterward from the debris.

The connection to other recent work is direct. Astronomers have used the James Webb Space Telescope and ALMA to study pairs of feeding black holes in the early universe, as in the <a href="/blog/lid-1166-dual-black-holes-z-4-5-november-2026/">LID-1166 Dual Black Holes at z 4.5: Webb and ALMA Catch Two Monsters Feeding Side by Side</a> system, and Webb's little red dots have reshaped ideas about the first supermassive black holes, as covered in <a href="/blog/jwst-little-red-dots-november-2026-direct-collapse-black-holes/">JWST Little Red Dots November 2026: The First Supermassive Black Holes Explained</a>. AT 2016blu works at the other end of the scale — a single star and a single compact object in a nearby galaxy — but the physics is the same: gravity, gas, and heat, turning a quiet pair into a lighthouse that flashes every 113 days.

## A Decade of Outbursts: The AT 2016blu Timeline

- **2001–2002:** Chandra observes the location of AT 2016blu and finds it faint in X-rays. This quiet baseline, recorded years before the outbursts begin, later becomes the comparison that reveals the hundredfold brightening.
- **2012:** Optical telescopes begin detecting the first of the repeating bursts of visible light from the star — the start of a run that will reach at least 27 outbursts over the next fourteen years.
- **2012–2026:** The outbursts keep coming, roughly every 113 days, about three times a year. Each flare resembles a supernova in brightness, but the star survives every one of them, earning its "supernova impostor" label.
- **Early 2026:** Optical monitoring by the Katzman Automatic Imaging Telescope, the Las Cumbres Observatory Global Supernova Project, and American Association of Variable Star Observers contributors shows the star is in outburst again — the trigger the Chandra team has been waiting for.
- **March 2026:** Chandra observes AT 2016blu under its Target of Opportunity program, catching the star mid-outburst. The data show the system at least 100 times brighter in X-rays than in 2001–2002.
- **November 9, 2026:** The paper led by Mojgan Aghakhanloo is published in The Astrophysical Journal, and NASA's Chandra X-ray Center announces the discovery: a hidden black hole or neutron star companion is driving the eruptions.

## Why the X-Rays Were the Missing Piece

For fifteen years, the visible-light record of AT 2016blu was both the clue and the trap. The flashes were bright, regular, and well documented, but visible light alone could not show what powered them. A star can brighten for many reasons: its outer layers can pulsate, its wind can thicken and thin, material it threw off years earlier can be struck by a new wave of ejected gas. The optical data described the symptom in detail and said nothing about the cause.

X-rays changed that because they are produced only by the most extreme conditions — gas heated to millions of degrees, matter moving at relativistic speeds, the deep gravitational well of a compact object. The March 2026 Chandra observation caught the system doing exactly that, at exactly the moment the optical flash was underway, and at a brightness a hundred times above its quiet state. That simultaneity is the heart of the discovery. It is one thing for a star to flare in visible light; it is another for its X-ray output to surge by two orders of magnitude at the same moment, on the same 113-day schedule. Together, the two signals point to a single engine: a compact companion feeding at closest approach.

The observation also explains why the answer took so long to find. Chandra had looked at this patch of sky before, in 2001 and 2002, and found nothing remarkable — because nothing remarkable was happening yet. The outbursts had not begun. And any X-ray observation taken between outbursts, when the companion was far from the giant star and not feeding, would have shown little. The discovery required patience on two fronts: years of optical monitoring to learn the schedule, and then an X-ray observation timed to the schedule. Without the first, the second would have been a shot in the dark.

There is a broader lesson here for how astronomy works in the 2020s. The biggest telescopes get the headlines, but a discovery like this one rests on a chain of instruments and people: a space telescope with uniquely sharp X-ray vision, robotic survey telescopes that watch the sky every clear night, and a network of variable-star observers — more than 30 of them amateurs — who kept watch over a single faint point of light in a distant galaxy for over a decade. No link in that chain is optional.

## Supernova Impostors and the Road to Real Supernovae

AT 2016blu belongs to a class of objects that has long blurred the line between a star in trouble and a star that is gone. Supernova impostors are massive stars that erupt so violently that, at first glance, they look like they have exploded. The difference only becomes clear with time: a supernova leaves a fading remnant and a missing star; an impostor leaves the star still shining, ready to erupt again. Eta Carinae, the most famous luminous blue variable, is the classic example of a star that can throw off enormous amounts of material in a giant eruption and survive.

What the AT 2016blu discovery adds is a mechanism. If some impostor eruptions are driven by a companion that strips material on a schedule, then the eruptions are not purely the giant star's own instability. They are the product of a binary system, and the timing of the eruptions is a direct readout of the orbit. That matters for a long-standing question in stellar astrophysics: how do massive stars lose mass in the years and decades before they explode? The answer shapes everything that follows — the brightness of the eventual supernova, the elements it scatters into space, and the kind of remnant it leaves behind.

The discovery of this system may also help studies of stars that change in brightness before they undergo supernova explosions. Astronomers suspect that much of this pre-supernova behavior may be caused by systems containing a massive star orbiting a compact object or another massive star. AT 2016blu is the clearest example yet of that suspicion playing out in real time, with a measured period, a measured X-ray surge, and a companion whose identity can be chased down with future observations.

The galaxy context is part of the story too. NGC 4559 is a spiral galaxy, and its star-forming regions are the natural home of massive, short-lived stars. Wide-field surveys of galaxies like it — the kind of work that missions such as Euclid are now doing at unprecedented scale, as in <a href="/blog/euclid-dr1-foundation-billion-galaxies-november-2026/">Euclid DR1 Foundation Billion Galaxies: ESA's Dark Universe Map Begins</a> — give astronomers the census of galaxies and environments in which systems like AT 2016blu live. The more galaxies are mapped, the more of these rare, eruptive binaries can be found, and the better the statistics become for understanding how common they really are.

## The Amateur Astronomers Who Kept Watch

One of the quieter details in the Chandra announcement deserves a louder mention: more than 30 amateur astronomers helped monitor AT 2016blu. The American Association of Variable Star Observers, a network of volunteers who measure the brightness of variable stars night after night, contributed observations alongside professional facilities. Their data, combined with the Katzman Automatic Imaging Telescope and the Las Cumbres Observatory Global Supernova Project, built the long, unbroken record that made the 113-day cycle visible and that told the Chandra team when to trigger their observations.

This is not a courtesy credit. A 113-day cycle can only be measured from a record that spans many cycles without gaps, and gaps are exactly what professional telescopes, with their competing schedules and limited time, tend to leave. A distributed network of observers, watching the same star from backyards and small observatories around the world, fills those gaps. When the star flared in early 2026, the network saw it, and Chandra was able to respond within weeks.

It is a reminder that some of the most consequential work in modern astronomy is still done by people who do it for love of the sky. The discovery of a black hole or neutron star companion, 30 million light-years away, depended in part on volunteers who pointed their telescopes at the same faint point of light, on clear night after clear night, for years. The professional paper has one lead author; the discovery has dozens of co-authors in spirit, most of whom will never see their names in a press release.

## What Comes Next

The immediate question is the identity of the companion. Is it a stellar-mass black hole, or a neutron star? The two possibilities predict subtly different behavior — different masses, different X-ray spectra, different details in how the brightness rises and falls through each 113-day cycle. Future Chandra observations, timed to the outburst schedule the team now understands, can test those predictions. So can other X-ray observatories, and so can continued optical monitoring, which will keep refining the timing of the cycle and catching any changes in it.

A second question is how common systems like this are. AT 2016blu is the first of its class to be detected in X-rays and the first found with a black hole or neutron star companion — but "first found" is not the same as "only one." Now that astronomers know what to look for — repeated, periodic, supernova-like optical flares paired with X-ray surges — they can search archival data for other stars that fit the pattern. Some of the unexplained ultra-luminous X-ray sources, the rare bright objects whose nature has been debated for years, may turn out to be systems of exactly this kind, caught at different points in their cycles.

A third question reaches further. If companion-driven eruptions are a common phase in the lives of massive stars, then the mass-loss history of those stars — and therefore the supernovae they eventually produce — is shaped by binary companions far more often than older models assumed. That has consequences for the chemical enrichment of galaxies, for the population of black holes and neutron stars, and for the gravitational-wave sources that form when two compact objects eventually merge. A single star in NGC 4559, flashing every 113 days, is a small window into all of that.

The watch continues. The next outburst is never far away — the cycle is only about three and a half months long — and every new one is a chance to look again, in X-rays and in visible light, at a star that keeps exploding and a companion that keeps pulling it apart. Fifteen years of flashes taught astronomers the schedule. The next fifteen may teach them what the companion really is, and how many more stars like this one are hiding in plain sight, flaring on a schedule, waiting for someone to notice the clock.

For a star that has exploded at least 27 times and survived them all, the real surprise was never the explosions. It was the company it kept while doing it — a dead star's heart, circling in the dark, pulling its living partner apart one close pass at a time, every 113 days, like clockwork. The supernova that never happened turned out to be a theft, repeated on schedule, and the thief has finally been caught in the act.
