Somewhere in the constellation Perseus, about 1,000 light-years from Earth, a cloud of gas and dust is busy making stars. It has been doing so for a few million years, and astronomers have studied its handiwork for decades. Yet when NASA's James Webb Space Telescope turned its golden mirrors toward this stellar nursery in a deep new survey, it found something nobody expected: objects so small they blur the very definition of what a star is allowed to be.

The objects are brown dwarfs — the so-called "failed stars" of the cosmos — and the smallest of them weigh only about twice as much as Jupiter. That is roughly 0.19 percent of the mass of the Sun, making them the least massive brown dwarfs ever confirmed anywhere in the universe. One of them even appears to be surrounded by a disk of gas and dust, the raw material from which planets are built, raising the startling possibility that planets could form around an object that is itself barely more massive than a planet.

The findings, published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, come from a team led by Kevin Luhman of Penn State University and Catarina Alves de Oliveira of the European Space Agency. They represent the deepest spectroscopic census ever taken of the star-forming region known as IC 348, and they are already forcing theorists to rethink how small the star-formation process can go.

## Failed stars, and why they matter

To appreciate what Webb found, it helps to understand what brown dwarfs are — and why astronomers care about them at all.

A star is born when a cloud of cold molecular gas collapses under its own gravity. As the cloud contracts, its core grows hotter and denser until the temperature climbs past roughly 10 million degrees Celsius. At that point, hydrogen nuclei begin fusing into helium, releasing the torrent of energy that makes a star shine. A star is, in essence, a controlled thermonuclear explosion that lasts for billions of years.

But not every collapsing cloud gathers enough mass to reach that threshold. The smallest true stars contain about 8 percent of the Sun's mass. Below that line, the core never gets hot enough to sustain ordinary hydrogen fusion. The result is a brown dwarf: an object that forms like a star, through the gravitational collapse of a cloud, but never ignites like one. Many brown dwarfs briefly fuse deuterium — a heavier isotope of hydrogen — in their youth, which gives them a short-lived glow, but they lack the mass to keep the fires burning. After that brief flare, they spend the rest of eternity cooling and fading, radiating away their leftover heat of formation.

Brown dwarfs occupy a strange middle ground. They are far more massive than planets — the textbook dividing line, based on the ability to fuse deuterium, sits near 13 times the mass of Jupiter — yet they never become stars. For decades, theorists argued about how small such an object could be. Calculations of the so-called opacity limit for fragmentation, the point at which a collapsing cloud becomes too small to keep fragmenting, suggested that star-like formation should bottom out somewhere around 7 to 10 times the mass of Jupiter. Below that, the thinking went, you might find planets — objects built up piece by piece around a young star, or ejected from their birth systems — but not free-floating bodies born through stellar collapse.

Webb has now smashed through that floor.

## A second, deeper look at IC 348

The new study did not come out of nowhere. It is the sequel to an earlier Webb survey of IC 348 that already turned heads.

In 2022, Luhman, Alves de Oliveira, and their colleagues used Webb to probe the cluster and found brown dwarfs with estimated masses of three to four times that of Jupiter — at the time, astonishingly small. But the team suspected the cluster might harbor even lighter objects, lurking just below the detection limits of that first survey. So they went back for a deeper look.

In 2024, they used Webb's Near-Infrared Camera, NIRCam, to photograph IC 348 in exquisite detail, picking out candidate brown dwarfs by their distinctive colors and faint brightness. The imaging campaign yielded 39 promising candidates — objects whose infrared hues matched what astronomers expect from extremely cool, low-mass bodies rather than distant background galaxies or ordinary stars.

Then came the hard part. A candidate is only a candidate until its spectrum confirms what it is. In 2025, the team turned Webb's second workhorse instrument, the Near-Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec), on 15 of the most promising targets, gathering detailed measurements of how each object's light is spread across wavelengths. A spectrum is like a fingerprint: the dips and peaks in an object's light reveal the molecules in its atmosphere, its temperature, and — through comparison with models — its mass.

Nine of the fifteen turned out to be genuine brown dwarfs rather than background interlopers that had merely looked the part. And their masses, the team found, plunged far below anything previously confirmed: down to about twice the mass of Jupiter.

## Nine new worlds that are not worlds

The nine confirmed brown dwarfs extend the spectroscopically classified population of IC 348 into genuinely uncharted territory. At roughly two Jupiter masses, the lightest of them sit well inside the mass range astronomers normally reserve for planets. By the deuterium-burning criterion — the famous 13-Jupiter-mass line — every one of these objects would be classified as planetary in mass. Yet everything about their context says they formed like stars.

Consider the evidence. These objects are members of IC 348, a young cluster still wrapped in the gas and dust of its birth. They are not orbiting any star; they drift freely through the cluster, just as the cluster's stars do. And at least one of them shows the infrared excess that betrays a surrounding disk of warm material — the signature of a body still wrapped in the leftovers of its own formation, exactly as young stars are. Spatial and spectroscopic evidence alike favors star-like formation over the alternative explanation, planetary ejection, in which a fully formed planet is gravitationally hurled out of its birth system.

That distinction matters enormously. If these objects were ejected planets, they would tell us about the violence of young planetary systems. But as products of star formation itself, they tell us something far more fundamental: that the process which builds stars can operate at masses far lower than theory predicted. The collapsing-cloud machinery, it seems, does not know where the textbooks told it to stop.

## A disk around a near-planet

Perhaps the most tantalizing detail in the new study concerns a single object among the nine, designated in cluster catalogs and discussed in follow-up coverage as one of the lightest newfound members. Its spectrum shows the telltale infrared glow of a circumstellar — or perhaps more accurately circum-substellar — disk: warm dust and gas orbiting the tiny body, left over from the collapse that formed it.

Disks like this are the birthplaces of planets. Around young Sun-like stars, such disks coalesce over millions of years into planets, moons, asteroids, and comets. Finding one around an object of only about twice Jupiter's mass raises a remarkable possibility: that small planets could be forming right now around a body that is itself barely more massive than a planet.

To be clear, Webb has not detected any planets there. What it has detected is the signpost — the disk — and the implication is profound enough on its own. It suggests that planet formation may not require a star at all, or at least not a star as we normally picture one. A scaled-down star, a failed star, a body with a planet's heft but a star's birth story, might host its own miniature planetary system. Astronomers have already found planets orbiting brown dwarfs; the new result hints that the process could extend to objects an order of magnitude less massive than anyone had dared to propose.

Theorists will now have to reckon with a new question: if a two-Jupiter-mass object can retain a disk, how far down does the planet-forming machinery go? And what would the resulting planets look like — tiny, cold, huddled close around a dim ember of a primary?

## A molecule nobody can name

The spectra held one more surprise, and it may be the strangest of all.

When the team examined the atmospheric fingerprints of IC 348's brown dwarfs, they found an absorption feature they could not identify — a dip in the spectrum consistent with a hydrocarbon, a molecule built solely from hydrogen and carbon atoms, but one that matches no known laboratory spectrum. Crucially, this mystery feature appears only in the atmospheres of the lowest-mass brown dwarfs in the sample. The warmer, slightly more massive objects do not show it.

That exclusivity is what makes the feature so intriguing. In astronomy, when a spectral signature appears only within a specific temperature or mass range, it often marks the boundary of a new spectral class — a new letter in the sequence astronomers use to categorize objects by their atmospheric chemistry. The researchers have suggested that these extreme objects might belong to a spectral class of their own, distinct from both stars and planets, with the proposed designation "H."

If confirmed by further observations and laboratory work, the H class would be the first new spectral class defined from the bottom up — discovered not by theory but by Webb stumbling across objects colder and stranger than the models anticipated. It would also give astronomers a practical tool: a way to recognize these borderline bodies in future surveys by the unique chemistry of their atmospheres.

The unidentified hydrocarbon itself is a puzzle worth savoring. Hydrocarbons are among the most interesting molecules in astronomy — they are the chemical precursors, in the right environments, to far more complex organic chemistry. Finding an unknown one in the atmosphere of a near-planetary-mass object is a reminder of how much basic chemistry of the cold universe remains unmapped.

## When models meet reality

Science advances when a clean prediction collides with a messy observation, and the IC 348 results are a textbook collision.

Star-formation theory, in its standard form, holds that a collapsing molecular cloud fragments into smaller and smaller pieces as it contracts, but that this fragmentation has a lower limit. As a fragment shrinks, it becomes opaque to its own cooling radiation; it can no longer shed heat efficiently, and further fragmentation stalls. Detailed calculations placed that opacity limit for fragmentation at roughly 7 to 10 Jupiter masses. Objects below it, the theory said, could still exist — but they would have to form the way planets form, by accumulating solid material around a young star, not by direct collapse.

Webb's two-Jupiter-mass brown dwarfs do not fit that picture. They appear to have formed by collapse — they are free-floating cluster members with star-like spectra and, in one case, a primordial disk — at masses a factor of several below the predicted floor. Something in the standard account is incomplete.

That does not mean star-formation theory is wrong in any wholesale sense. The theory correctly describes the formation of the vast majority of stars and brown dwarfs, and its predictions have been vindicated countless times. What the new results show is that the process has more range than the models captured — that under the right conditions, collapse can reach down to planetary masses. Theorists will now revisit the details: the role of turbulence in the parent cloud, the influence of magnetic fields, the possibility that some of these objects formed in the disks of larger bodies and were then liberated. Each hypothesis makes testable predictions, and Webb, with its ability to take spectra of the faintest objects in nearby star-forming regions, is the ideal tool to test them.

There is a pleasing symmetry here. The same telescope that has been rewriting the story of the early universe — finding unexpectedly massive galaxies in the cosmic dawn — is now rewriting the story at the opposite end of the mass scale, finding unexpectedly tiny products of star formation in our own galactic backyard.

## Redrawing the map between stars and planets

Beyond the technical challenge to models, the discovery exposes a deeper weakness in how astronomers label the inhabitants of the cosmos.

Ask a simple question — where does a planet end and a star begin? — and you will get several different answers depending on whom you ask. A physicist might point to the deuterium-burning limit near 13 Jupiter masses: above it, an object can briefly fuse heavy hydrogen; below it, it cannot. A dynamicist might point to orbital status: planets orbit stars, while brown dwarfs drift free. A formation theorist might point to birth history: planets are built up from solid cores in disks, while stars and brown dwarfs condense directly from collapsing gas.

The IC 348 objects confound all three schemes at once. By mass, they are planets. By orbital status, they are not — they orbit nothing. By formation, they are stars, or at least star-like. No single label fits, which suggests the labels themselves are what need fixing.

This is not merely a taxonomic quibble. How astronomers classify objects shapes how they count them, how they model their populations, and how they design the surveys that find them. If the star-formation process routinely produces planetary-mass bodies, then the census of "planets" in the galaxy — usually counted as objects orbiting stars — may be missing an entire shadow population of free-floating worlds born like stars. Some estimates already suggest such rogue objects could be numerous; the IC 348 results imply the smallest of them may be far smaller than assumed.

Future surveys will have to grapple with this ambiguity. Missions designed to find exoplanets by the thousands will need to distinguish true planets from tiny brown dwarfs — a task that requires spectra, not just masses. The proposed H spectral class, if it holds up, could become an essential diagnostic in that effort.

## A dynamic panorama

The brown dwarfs were the headline, but Webb's portrait of IC 348 contained more wonders. The same observations captured a brilliantly detailed collection of protostars — infant stars still gathering mass — in one corner of the image, several accompanied by Herbig-Haro objects, the luminous shock fronts that form when powerful jets from newborn stars slam into the surrounding gas and dust.

One such feature, the long horizontal streak known as HH 797, resolves on close inspection into two protostars driving nearly parallel outflows — a binary system caught in the act of sculpting its nursery. Nearby, the propeller-shaped HH 211 shows both narrow jets and broader outflows, a textbook example of how young stars expel excess angular momentum as they grow. These are the violent, messy realities of star birth, frozen in infrared light: fountains of gas moving at hundreds of kilometers per second, carving cavities through the cloud that made them.

The juxtaposition is poetic. In the same image, Webb shows us stars being born in dramatic, jet-driven fury — and, at the opposite extreme, the quietest, smallest products of the same process: embers barely heavier than Jupiter, cooling in the dark. It is a panorama of star formation in the fullest sense, from the most violent to the most diminutive.

## What comes next

The Astrophysical Journal Letters paper is a beginning, not an end. The nine new brown dwarfs are now prime targets for follow-up, and astronomers have no shortage of questions.

First among them is the disk. Confirming the disk's structure — its size, its mass, whether it shows gaps or rings that might betray forming planets — will require observations at longer wavelengths, where cold dust glows brightest. The Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, ALMA, in Chile is the natural tool for the job, and it has already revolutionized the study of disks around ordinary stars and brown dwarfs. Pointing it at a two-Jupiter-mass object would push the technique to its limits.

Second is the mystery hydrocarbon. Identifying it will take a combination of sharper spectra and laboratory astrophysics — measuring the absorption of candidate molecules under the frigid, high-pressure conditions of a brown dwarf atmosphere until something matches. If the feature truly defines a new spectral class, theorists will want to understand what chemistry switches on at these extreme low temperatures.

Third is the population question. IC 348 is one cluster; are planetary-mass brown dwarfs common in all star-forming regions, or is something special about this one? Webb surveys of other nearby nurseries — the Orion Nebula Cluster, Taurus, Chamaeleon — could answer that within a few years, rewriting the bottom end of the stellar mass function.

And then there is the biggest question of all, the one the discovery poses without answering: if star formation can make objects of two Jupiter masses, can it go lower still? Is there a true bottom, or does the process fade seamlessly into the realm of planets, with no sharp boundary at all? Somewhere in the dark between the stars, Webb may yet find objects that force astronomers to abandon the distinction entirely.

For now, the nine new members of IC 348 stand as a reminder of a truth astronomers relearn with every generation of telescopes: the universe is under no obligation to respect our categories. We draw lines between stars and planets, between success and failure, between the significant and the negligible. And every so often, the cosmos draws something right across them — in this case, a wisp of an object, twice the mass of Jupiter, wrapped in the makings of worlds, daring us to say what it is.

## A September to remember

The IC 348 results arrived as part of a remarkable month for the Webb telescope, whose observations continue to reshape astronomy across every scale it touches. But few of its recent findings are as philosophically unsettling as this one. Galaxies in the early universe challenge our models of cosmic history; tiny brown dwarfs in a nearby nursery challenge our models of how anything forms at all — and our very vocabulary for describing what forms.

The next time you look up at Perseus on a clear autumn night, consider that somewhere in that direction, a thousand light-years away, drift nine newly confirmed embers — the smallest failed stars ever seen. One of them may be building planets. None of them will ever shine like the Sun. And all of them are telling us, in the quiet language of infrared light, that the universe is stranger, subtler, and more creative than we supposed.
