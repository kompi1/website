On the evening of October 13, 2026, Matthew Holman was doing something that sounds routine and is, in practice, one of the hardest jobs in modern astronomy: asking a computer to find a moving speck in a sky full of stars. He was running data collected earlier in 2026 by the Vera C. Rubin Observatory through a search code he had developed with his colleague Kevin Napier, a program built to look at billions of detections of moving objects, compare them, and work out which detections belong to the same object. Most of what such a code finds is small, faint, and expected. This time, the brightness and the distance did not add up to anything small at all. The object was far away, beyond the orbit of Pluto, and it was far too bright to be just another icy pebble in the outer solar system.

"My first reaction was one of excited disbelief," Holman said. "Objects like this are very rare, so I immediately started checking whether I or the code had done something wrong."

He had not. Two weeks later, on October 27, 2026, the International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center announced the discovery, and Holman and Mario Juric, the Rubin Solar System lead scientist and a professor of astronomy at the University of Washington, presented the results at a press conference at the 58th annual meeting of the American Astronomical Society's Division for Planetary Sciences in Spokane, Washington. The object carries the provisional designation 2026 ED138, shortened to ED138. It is the Rubin Observatory's first dwarf planet candidate, and if follow-up observations confirm what its brightness suggests, it will join one of the smallest and most exclusive clubs in astronomy: the five worlds the International Astronomical Union currently recognizes as dwarf planets, Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake.

That conditional matters, and it is worth holding onto it through all the excitement that follows a discovery like this. ED138 is a candidate, not yet a confirmed dwarf planet. What astronomers know already is that it is large and bright and far away. What they do not yet know, precisely, is how large it is, and size is everything here. A dwarf planet must be massive enough for its own gravity to pull it into a round shape, a state astronomers call hydrostatic equilibrium. The team estimates ED138's diameter at between 600 and 1,500 kilometres, a range so wide that it spans the difference between a substantial world and a giant one, and that width is not a mistake or a hedge. It is the honest consequence of measuring a distant icy body by reflected sunlight alone: a smaller, shinier object can return exactly as much light as a larger, darker one. Until astronomers measure the heat the object itself gives off, at longer, infrared wavelengths, they cannot break that ambiguity. Holman has been frank about the timescale, saying that establishing the physical size will take a few months, probably. The candidate label is therefore not modesty. It is the scientific status of the object, and it will stay that way until the thermal measurements come in.

Even inside that wide range, though, the discovery is remarkable. Juric called it the largest trans-Neptunian object found in about 20 years, and added a detail that makes the find stranger still: Rubin turned it up while the telescope was still in commissioning, before its great survey had properly begun. "Finding an object this big, roughly 1,000 kilometres across, is something that happens maybe once in a generation," Juric said. A thousand kilometres is a useful mental anchor inside the 600 to 1,500 kilometre range, roughly the distance from Paris to Berlin, or about two-thirds the diameter of Pluto itself, which measures about 2,377 kilometres across. It puts ED138 in the size class of Makemake and Haumea, two of the five recognized dwarf planets, and it makes ED138, by the observatory's own assessment, among the largest and brightest bodies ever discovered in this region of the solar system.

## A world hiding in plain sight

The most arresting part of the ED138 story is not that the object is big. It is that it was hiding, and that it had been hiding for years, in data astronomers already had. ED138 sits in a region of sky close to the plane of the Milky Way, a crowded band filled with millions of stars. To most telescopes, it has simply been lost among the myriad background sources. A faint moving dot against a handful of stars is a tractable problem. A faint moving dot against millions of stars, night after night, is a needle in a haystack that is itself moving, and earlier surveys, for all their power, had not picked it out.

Rubin changed that equation in two ways, and both of them matter for understanding why this discovery arrived when it did. The first is the telescope itself: the 8.4-metre Simonyi Survey Telescope on Cerro Pachon in Chile, carrying the largest digital camera ever built, a 3,200-megapixel instrument that images huge swathes of sky in a single exposure. The second is the way Rubin works. It does not take a picture of a patch of sky once and move on. It returns to the same sky again and again, building a time-lapse record, and its software sifts through millions to billions of points of light to identify the ones that move. Movement is the giveaway. Stars stay put, at least on human timescales. Solar system objects creep, frame by frame, against the fixed background, and a survey that watches the whole southern sky repeatedly can catch that creep even in the most crowded fields.

Edward Ajhar, the National Science Foundation's program director for Rubin Observatory, described that capability in terms that now read like a prediction fulfilled. "Rubin Observatory's super power is the ability to clearly capture movement and change among a vast number of objects and phenomena that humans have never seen before," he said. "That power greatly extends our ability to reveal and understand new things about our Solar System, the Milky Way, and billions of other galaxies across the Universe."

The discovery sequence shows that power in action, step by step. After Holman's initial detection on the evening of October 13, Juric searched Rubin's catalogs and images and found 20 more observations, going back to March 4, 2026. In all, Rubin's Simonyi Survey Telescope observed ED138 30 times on 20 nights between March 4 and July 11, 2026. That alone would have been enough to establish an orbit, but the team went further, and this is where the story acquires its decade-long tail. Additional archival images from the Dark Energy Camera, an instrument fabricated by the Department of Energy and mounted on the Victor M. Blanco 4-metre Telescope at Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory, also in Chile, had captured ED138 without anyone knowing it. Those images, uncovered with the help of the Asteroid Institute's ADAM precovery service, stretch back to 2016, allowing the team to trace more than ten years of ED138's orbit.

Precovery, the recovery of an object in old images taken before its discovery, is one of the quiet revolutions of survey astronomy. Before Rubin, an astronomer who found a new object often had only a short arc of observations, a few weeks or months, from which to compute an orbit, and the orbit could be uncertain enough that the object might be lost again. With precovery, the arc stretches backwards, sometimes by years or decades, and the orbit tightens dramatically. Juric put the general point memorably: once Rubin identifies a new object, astronomers can search back months, and sometimes even decades, finding it in images where nobody knew to look. "This ability to both discover and 'precover' objects is transforming how quickly and accurately we can determine their orbits and understand the distant Solar System," he said. ED138 is the proof of that claim in a single object: discovered in October 2026, tracked back to March 2026 in Rubin data, and back to 2016 in Dark Energy Camera frames, a ten-year baseline assembled in days.

## Where it lives, and what its orbit says about its past

ED138 is currently about 46 astronomical units from the Sun, where one astronomical unit is the average distance between Earth and the Sun, about 150 million kilometres. For reference, Pluto orbits at an average distance of about 40 astronomical units, so ED138 is, right now, further from the Sun than Pluto, though that comparison needs a little care. Pluto's orbit is famously eccentric, swinging from about 30 astronomical units at its closest to about 49 at its farthest, so "beyond Pluto" is a statement about where ED138 is today and about its average distance, not a claim that it lives permanently outside Pluto's farthest reach. What is unambiguous is its classification: because it orbits the Sun at a distance greater than Neptune's orbit, ED138 is a trans-Neptunian object, a TNO, a member of the vast population of icy bodies that circles the Sun beyond the outermost giant planet.

TNOs are not all alike, and astronomers sort them by their orbits because an orbit is a fossil record. It preserves, in its shape and tilt, something about the gravitational encounters the object has suffered over billions of years. ED138's orbit is tilted almost 25 degrees off the plane of the solar system, a steep inclination that classifies it as a hot-classical TNO. The word "classical" here refers to the classical Kuiper Belt, the doughnut-shaped region of icy bodies beyond Neptune whose members orbit in a relatively orderly ring. The word "hot" does not mean temperature. It means dynamically excited: the orbit has been stirred up, tilted and stretched, relative to the flat, calm orbits of the cold classical population. The Rubin team says that tilt suggests ED138 was disturbed from its original orbit through gravitational interactions with Neptune, the giant planet whose outward migration in the solar system's youth is thought to have scattered and shepherded the Kuiper Belt into its present shape.

That reading is why astronomers study the size, composition, and orbital trajectories of TNOs at all. Each one is a clue to how the solar system formed, how the giant planets migrated over time, and how smaller objects were scattered throughout that evolution. A large TNO with a tilted orbit is a particularly valuable clue, because large objects are rare and because their orbits are harder to disturb without a substantial gravitational event. ED138, if its size estimate holds, is a survivor of that early reshuffling, a body big enough to have held itself together as a round world while Neptune's gravity tilted its path, and old enough that its surface, its composition, and its orbit together may preserve information about the conditions in the outer solar system when the planets were still settling into place.

There is one popular misunderstanding to clear up at this point, because every large object found beyond Neptune invites it. ED138 is not the long-hypothesised Planet Nine. That hypothetical world, proposed to explain the clustering of certain distant orbits, would be far more massive and far more distant, lurking hundreds of astronomical units from the Sun, if it exists at all. ED138, at 46 astronomical units and at most 1,500 kilometres across, is a Kuiper Belt world, a member of a known population, not a new giant planet hiding in the far dark. Its importance is precisely the opposite of the Planet Nine story: it shows that the known population still contains giants we had missed, not that an unknown population must be invoked to explain it. Readers who have followed the long-running hunt for new worlds in the outer solar system will recognise the distinction, and it is worth making explicitly, because the headlines that call ED138 a "new dwarf planet" without the word "candidate" risk blurring it.

## What counts as a dwarf planet, and why the bar is roundness

The definition that ED138 must satisfy was set by the International Astronomical Union in 2006, in the same decision that reclassified Pluto itself. A dwarf planet, under that definition, must orbit the Sun, must not be a satellite of another body, must not have cleared its neighbourhood of other objects, and, crucially, must have enough mass that its own gravity has pulled it into a nearly round shape. It is that last criterion, hydrostatic equilibrium, that separates dwarf planets from the thousands of smaller, irregularly shaped asteroids and Kuiper Belt objects that share their neighbourhoods. A small icy body can be any shape: a potato, a shard, a lumpy snowball. A large one cannot. Gravity wins, over geological time, and rounds it off.

That is why the size range matters so much, and why astronomers are careful about the lower end of it. Icy bodies as small as a few hundred kilometres across can be round, depending on their composition and history, and bodies at the lower end of ED138's range, around 600 kilometres, sit near the threshold where roundness becomes expected rather than merely possible. At the upper end, 1,500 kilometres, roundness is essentially certain for an icy body, which is why the team says that icy bodies this large are expected to be round. The gap between "expected" and "confirmed" is the gap that infrared observations must close. Measuring the thermal glow of ED138, the heat it radiates as opposed to the sunlight it reflects, will let astronomers separate size from reflectivity and pin down the diameter. If the diameter lands solidly in the upper part of the range, confirmation as a dwarf planet becomes a formality. If it lands near the bottom, the conversation becomes more interesting, and more dependent on shape measurements, perhaps from stellar occultations, where the object passes in front of a distant star and its shadow, timed from multiple sites on Earth, traces its outline.

None of this diminishes the discovery. The five recognized dwarf planets were not all confirmed overnight either, and the history of the category is a history of candidates becoming, after patient measurement, members. Ceres, the innermost dwarf planet, was discovered in 1801 and spent two centuries classified first as a planet, then as an asteroid, before the 2006 definition gave it its present status. Pluto was a planet for 76 years before it became the archetype of the new class. Eris, whose discovery in 2005 forced the 2006 decision, Haumea, and Makemake completed the current five. A sixth member, if ED138 earns it, would be the first addition to that list in nearly two decades, and it would arrive not from a targeted search for a single world, but from the first sweep of a survey designed to find everything that moves. That is a fitting origin for a new dwarf planet in the Rubin era: not a lone astronomer at an eyepiece, but an algorithm combing billions of detections, and a human being startled enough to check the code twice.

## Rubin, the survey, and the tip of the iceberg

To understand why ED138 appeared now, it helps to understand what Rubin is and what stage its great survey had reached. The observatory, jointly funded by the United States National Science Foundation and the Department of Energy's Office of Science, sits on Cerro Pachon in Chile and is named for Vera Rubin, the astronomer who provided the first convincing evidence for dark matter. Its primary mission is the Legacy Survey of Space and Time, a ten-year project to repeatedly scan the southern sky and create an ultra-wide, ultra-high-definition, time-lapse record of the universe. That survey formally began in June 2026. ED138 was found in data taken during the run-up to the survey, while the telescope was still being commissioned and its systems were still being tuned, which is why Juric's remark about commissioning carries real weight: the most productive survey telescope ever built found one of the largest objects in the outer solar system before its survey had officially started.

The scale of Rubin's early harvest gives the context. In April 2026, before the survey formally began, the Rubin Solar System team submitted observations of more than 11,000 new asteroids to the Minor Planet Center, including roughly 380 new trans-Neptunian objects. At the time, the team called that submission "the tip of the iceberg" and expressed excitement for the discoveries to come once the survey began. With ED138, the observatory says, those hopes are coming to fruition. The numbers are worth pausing over, because they reframe what a single discovery means. Eleven thousand new asteroids and nearly four hundred new TNOs, from early data alone, from a survey that will run for a decade and image the whole southern sky again and again. If a body as large as ED138 could hide until now in the Milky Way's crowded star fields, the natural question is how many more bodies, large and small, are hiding in the same fields, and in the fields earlier surveys skipped because they were too crowded, too far south, or too faint to be worth the effort. Holman's answer to that question is the mission statement of the whole survey: "Rubin will image the whole southern sky again and again for ten years, so any others hiding in the places earlier surveys skipped are now within reach."

That reach extends far beyond the solar system, of course, and readers of this site will have seen Rubin's fingerprints on other stories this year, from its sweeping sky surveys to the flood of alerts it generates each night. The observatory's broader story is told in <a href="/blog/vera-rubin-observatory-watching-sky/">The Telescope That Watches Everything: Vera Rubin's First Year Rewriting Astronomy</a>, which describes the camera, the cadence, and the alert stream that make discoveries like ED138 possible. What ED138 adds to that story is a concrete, named world, a demonstration that the survey's solar system science is not a promise for the future but a result in the present, delivered by a telescope that was, by the strictest reading, still warming up.

The solar system context matters here too, because ED138 does not arrive in isolation. It arrives in a year when spacecraft are rewriting the outer solar system from the inside out, and the contrast between the two kinds of exploration is instructive. The Rubin discovery is remote sensing at its grandest scale, a telescope on a Chilean mountaintop finding a world billions of kilometres away by its motion against the stars. The complementary approach, flying to the outer solar system and touching it, is embodied by missions like the one described in <a href="/blog/nasa-lucy-trojan-asteroid-region-2026/">NASA Lucy Trojan Asteroid Region Reached After Five Years and 2.2 Billion Miles</a>, which has just entered the realm of Jupiter's Trojan asteroids after a five-year cruise. One approach maps the population from afar; the other visits individual members up close. Together, they are how a solar system gets known: census first, then fieldwork, and ED138 is the census at its most dramatic, a new entry near the top of the size list.

## What happens next

Now that Rubin has identified ED138, scientists around the globe can organise follow-up observations to uncover more information about its nature. Images of the object are available to Rubin data rights holders on the Rubin Science Platform, an online environment where scientists can access, explore, and process the massive amounts of data being produced by the survey. The public, meanwhile, can interact with the discovery through Orbitviewer, an interactive web application that lets anyone explore the structure of the solar system in three dimensions and in real time, and watch ED138's tilted orbit traced against the orbits of the five recognized dwarf planets. That public dimension is not a footnote. It is part of how modern survey astronomy works: the data are vast, the discoveries are public, and the orbit of a new world can be explored by anyone with a browser, within days of its announcement.

The scientific follow-up will proceed in a rough order, dictated by what each technique can deliver. First, continued optical tracking, from Rubin itself and from other telescopes, will refine the orbit further, tightening the ten-year arc and confirming the inclination and the classification as a hot-classical object. Second, thermal infrared observations, from ground-based facilities and potentially from space, will measure the object's own heat and break the size-versus-reflectivity degeneracy, narrowing the 600 to 1,500 kilometre range to something far more precise. Third, and more slowly, astronomers will seek compositional information, from spectroscopy that splits ED138's faint reflected light into its component colours and looks for the fingerprints of water ice, methane, and other frozen volatiles that coat the surfaces of outer solar system worlds. A spectrum can reveal whether ED138 is a bright, fresh-surfaced world or a dark, ancient one, and that, in turn, feeds back into the size estimate and into theories about its history.

Stellar occultations, if the geometry cooperates, could provide the most direct measurement of all: a silhouette. When ED138 passes in front of a star, observers spread across the predicted shadow path on Earth can time the star's disappearance and reappearance, and the resulting chords trace the object's shape. It is a technique that has measured the sizes and shapes of other distant worlds with remarkable precision, and it is one that amateur astronomers, equipped with sensitive cameras and precise timing, have contributed to before. Whether ED138's path offers a favourable occultation in the near future is a question for the orbit computers, but the possibility illustrates how a discovery made by a billion-dollar survey can become, in part, a community endeavour.

There is also a longer, more speculative horizon to the story, and it is worth naming it without overstating it. If ED138 is confirmed, and if Rubin finds more large TNOs in the years ahead, astronomers will have a larger, better-measured sample with which to test theories of how the Kuiper Belt formed and how Neptune sculpted it. The hot-classical population, in particular, is a diagnostic of that migration: its members were stirred, but not ejected, and the distribution of their sizes, inclinations, and compositions encodes the violence and the timing of Neptune's outward journey. A single object cannot rewrite that theory. A growing census, anchored by large, well-measured worlds like ED138, can refine it, and can reveal whether the Kuiper Belt's size distribution has a cutoff, a pile-up, or a surprise at the large end that current models do not predict. The interstellar visitor that has dominated small-body headlines this year, described in <a href="/blog/3i-atlas-interstellar-comet-one-year-later/">3I/ATLAS Interstellar Comet, One Year Later</a>, reminds us that the solar system's small-body population is not a closed book, and that new kinds of objects keep arriving to challenge tidy categories. ED138 is a different kind of challenge: not a visitor from another star, but a native world we had simply overlooked, in our own backyard, behind a curtain of stars.

It is tempting, at the end of a discovery story, to reach for superlatives and stop there. The better ending is a timetable. ED138 was spotted on October 13, announced on October 27, and will spend the coming months under the infrared gaze of telescopes trying to measure its true size. If those measurements confirm a diameter in the upper part of the estimated range, the International Astronomical Union will have a decision to make, and the solar system will have, officially, six dwarf planets instead of five. If they land lower, ED138 will remain a fascinating, large, tilted Kuiper Belt world, and the search for the next candidate will continue, with a survey that images the sky again and again, night after night, for ten years. Either way, the lesson of ED138 is already secure, and it is a humbling one for a species that has been mapping the sky for centuries: a world a thousand kilometres across, orbiting our own Sun, hid for a decade in photographs we already owned, in a part of the sky we had looked at a thousand times, and it took a new telescope, a new algorithm, and a scientist willing to distrust his own code to notice that it was moving. The sky, it turns out, is not finished with surprises. It was only waiting for someone to watch it properly.
