"We got it done!" Those four words, radioed by NASA astronaut Anil Menon as he floated back toward the International Space Station's airlock, closed one of the longest-running construction projects ever attempted in orbit. After six hours and 59 minutes outside the station on Thursday, November 5, 2026, Menon and Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk had installed and deployed the eighth and final International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Array, known as an iROSA, on the station's 3B power channel. The five-year campaign to rebuild the orbiting laboratory's electrical system was complete, and the station's available power rose from about 160 kilowatts to approximately 215 kilowatts — a gain of roughly 30 percent that NASA says will carry the outpost through its final years of science and its planned controlled deorbit at the end of the decade.

The spacewalk, designated U.S. spacewalk 101, began at 8:36 a.m. EST and ended at 3:35 p.m. EST. It was the 286th spacewalk conducted in support of station assembly, maintenance, and upgrades, the ninth spacewalk at the station in 2026, and the sixth carried out by the resident Expedition 75 crew. For Menon it was his fifth spacewalk; for Kutryk, his second. The two men had performed the same job only a week earlier, when they installed the seventh iROSA on the 2A channel during U.S. spacewalk 100 on October 29. That outing lasted seven hours and 11 minutes. This time they returned to the far end of the station's truss to finish what they had started — and this time, the hardest part was not the installation itself but a stubborn electrical reading that forced them to stop, wait for darkness, and go back to check their own work.

## "We got it done"

The day began the way the previous Thursday had, with the two astronauts suiting up inside the Quest airlock and stepping out into the vacuum a little after sunrise over the Atlantic. Kutryk, designated EV1 for this outing, rode the Canadarm2 robotic arm wearing a suit marked with red stripes; Menon, as EV2, worked as the free-floating crewmember in an all-white suit. Inside the station, NASA astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney supported the pair, helping them into and out of their suits, watching their progress, and operating the robotic arm.

Their first task was to free the rolled-up array from its carrier on the station's truss and move it to the worksite at the far starboard end of the structure, on the S6 truss segment, where the 3B power channel's modification kit waited. The kit itself had been prepared months earlier, during a spacewalk in August, when Menon and NASA astronaut Jessica Meir installed the mounting bracket that the new wing would bolt onto. The array is a compact roll of solar cells, folded tight enough to be carried by hand, that unfurls once it is bolted down and released — a design that lets a wing far more powerful than its size suggests be delivered to orbit folded like a carpet and then unrolled in space. Once deployed, the wing stretches 63 feet, or 19 meters, long.

Getting the array to the worksite was the one part of the job that could not be rehearsed the same way twice. The mounting site sits at the far right end of the station's main truss, beyond the reach of Canadarm2, which had carried the earlier arrays into position. There was no mechanical shortcut for this one. Instead, the two astronauts passed the rolled-up array between foot restraints, handing it along the truss by hand in a maneuver the flight team had rehearsed in detail. Flight director Garrett Hehn had described the approach at a briefing at NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston on October 20, where the spacewalk team laid out the choreography for both outings: the arm does the heavy lifting where it can reach, and the astronauts do the rest themselves. On Thursday, the handoff went as planned. Kutryk, perched on the arm, passed the array to Menon, who carried it the final stretch to the bracket and bolted it down.

With the wing secured, the pair connected its electrical system to the 3B channel and prepared for deployment. That was when the day's complication appeared.

## A glitch at the halfway mark

As the array's circuits came alive, flight controllers on the ground noticed something wrong. The shunt current — the electrical flow that shows how much power the channel is actually carrying — was only about half of what it should have been. "We are seeing half of the expected shunt current that we want," radioed Nichole Stilwell, a flight controller in Houston, as the crew worked through the problem.

Half power on a brand-new array could have meant a faulty connection, a damaged cable, or a problem inside the wing itself, and none of those are things you can fix by wishing. The crew and the ground team made the careful choice: stop, wait, and inspect. The inspection had to wait for orbital night, when the station passes into Earth's shadow and the solar arrays stop generating power, making it safe to handle the connectors. During that dark pass, Menon and Kutryk went back over the electrical connections they had just made, inspecting them and reseating a power connector that had not seated fully.

The fix worked. "We are seeing good shunt currents on the ground, so we are go for deploy," Stilwell radioed, and the crew moved on to the final step. After releasing two bolts, Kutryk and Menon watched as the array started to unfurl. Driven entirely by stored elastic energy in its carbon-fiber composite booms — there are no motors involved — the wing unrolled itself to its full 63-foot length in about ten minutes. By the time the sun rose again on the station, the eighth iROSA was open, locked, and generating power.

The glitch was a reminder of how unforgiving this kind of work is. A connector seated a fraction of a millimeter short can halve the output of a wing that took years to build and a rocket launch to deliver. It was also a reminder of why NASA still sends people to do this work: a robot could not have diagnosed the problem by feel, reseated the connector, and watched the current come back. Menon and Kutryk could, and did, and the spacewalk finished on schedule — six hours and 59 minutes after it began, at 3:35 p.m. EST, or 3:35 a.m. on Friday in Jakarta.

## The handoff at the end of the truss

The final installation was in many ways a repeat of the one a week earlier, and the similarities were deliberate. Spacewalk 100 on October 29 had installed the seventh iROSA on the 2A channel, on the port side of the station, in seven hours and 11 minutes. Spacewalk 101 repeated the same sequence — unbolt the array from its carrier, move it to the worksite, bolt it to the modification kit, connect the electrics, deploy — but on the opposite end of the truss, on the starboard side, where the geometry is different and the arm cannot reach.

The differences mattered. On the port side a week earlier, Canadarm2 could deliver the array most of the way. On the starboard side, the final stretch belonged to the astronauts' hands alone. The manual handoff — one astronaut on the arm's foot restraint passing a 60-foot wing, rolled tight, to a free-floating colleague — was the signature maneuver of this spacewalk, and it is the kind of thing that looks simple in a briefing and feels very different in a pressurized suit, in the dark, moving at five miles a second. It went smoothly, and it is now part of the record of how the station was finished.

The two outings, a week apart, were also a study in how a crew builds rhythm. Menon and Kutryk had already done this job once, together, in the same suits, on the same truss. The second time, they knew the choreography, the handholds, the order of the bolts, and the way the array behaves when it comes off its carrier. That familiarity showed in the clock: spacewalk 101 came in twelve minutes shorter than spacewalk 100, even with the extra inspection pass for the electrical fault. Experience, in orbit as on Earth, is the difference between doing a job and doing it well.

## Five years, eight wings

The completion of the eighth iROSA closes a campaign that began in February 2021, when NASA approved the power augmentation program after flight controllers discovered that the station's eight original solar wings were losing output. The original arrays, launched aboard space shuttle flights between 2000 and 2009, were designed for a 15-year life. They had exceeded it, and their degraded output was beginning to constrain what the station could do. The iROSAs were the answer: smaller, far more efficient wings mounted in front of the originals, partially shading them while adding their own output on top.

Each iROSA measures about 60 feet long by 20 feet wide once deployed, roughly half the size of the original wings, which span 112 feet by 39 feet. But the new cells are far more efficient than the technology flown when the station was built, and each wing produces about eight kilowatts of power. Eight of them together add roughly 55 kilowatts to the station's supply, lifting it from about 160 kilowatts to approximately 215 kilowatts — a gain NASA describes as roughly 30 percent, and which Menon, standing at the base of the newly deployed wing, described more vividly at the close of the spacewalk. "Today marks the completion of not just one solar array but, over 15 years after its development, eight arrays," he said. "These solar arrays are incredible. Half the size of the old ones and bringing 40% more power."

The campaign took 18 spacewalks to prepare the power channel equipment and install the eight arrays, spread across more than five years. The first iROSA was installed in June 2021. The arrays and their mounting assemblies were manufactured by Redwire and delivered to the station folded in the unpressurized trunk of SpaceX Dragon cargo spacecraft, a delivery route that made the whole program possible: a wing that folds small enough to ride in a cargo trunk, then unfolds itself in space, needs no dedicated launch of its own.

The program's arithmetic is worth pausing on. A station that entered service in 1998, with solar wings designed when the Spice Girls were still on the charts, now carries a hybrid power system: eight aging original wings, partially shaded, plus eight modern roll-out wings layered in front of them. The combination gives the station more electrical capacity than it has ever had, at precisely the moment when it needs it most — in its final years, when the science manifest is at its fullest and the station must also prepare for its own end.

## What 215 kilowatts buys

Power is the station's most basic currency, and for years the station has been spending it carefully. Every experiment, every life-support system, every visiting vehicle, every communications pass draws from the same supply, and when the original arrays degraded, the station's managers had to choose. The iROSA upgrade ends those choices. With about 215 kilowatts available, the station can run its full science program, support visiting commercial vehicles, and keep its life-support systems running with margin to spare — all at the same time.

That margin matters for the science. The station's research program in its final years includes experiments that cannot be done anywhere else: protein crystal growth for drug development, materials science in microgravity, plant biology for future deep-space missions, and Earth observation. All of it runs on electricity, and all of it competes for the same supply. The extra 55 kilowatts does not just add capacity; it removes a constraint that has shaped scheduling decisions for years.

It also matters for the end. The station is currently scheduled for retirement and a controlled deorbit in late 2030, and the final years of that plan require power too — for the visiting vehicles that will gradually lower the station's orbit, for the systems that keep it stable while it descends, and for the controlled re-entry itself. A station that runs out of power before its deorbit is a station nobody can steer, and NASA has been explicit that the iROSA upgrade is part of making sure that never happens. The power the new wings provide will support station operations through retirement and the controlled deorbit that follows.

There is a longer lesson here as well. The iROSA design — a wing that folds small, launches in a cargo trunk, and deploys itself with no motors — is exactly the kind of technology that future stations and deep-space missions will need. The Lunar Gateway, the commercial stations now being designed to replace the ISS, and the solar-electric propulsion systems planned for Mars missions all depend on large, lightweight, deployable power systems. The eight wings now spread across the station's truss are not just the station's power supply; they are a flight-proven demonstration of the technology that will power whatever comes next. Every kilowatt they generate is also evidence that the design works.

## The crew and the count

For Menon, the spacewalk brought his career total to 33 hours and 30 minutes outside a spacecraft across five spacewalks. He is a relatively new astronaut — he flew to the station for the first time on this mission — and he has now spent more time outside the station than many astronauts accumulate in an entire career. For Kutryk, the outing brought his total to 14 hours and 10 minutes across two spacewalks, both of them conducted in the past eight days, both of them installing iROSAs. The two men have now done the two most recent spacewalks in the station's history, back to back, and they have finished the job between them.

The numbers around the spacewalk tell their own story. U.S. spacewalk 101 was the 101st American spacewalk conducted from the station without a space shuttle present, and the 286th spacewalk overall in support of station assembly, maintenance, and upgrades. It was the ninth spacewalk at the station in 2026 and the sixth conducted by Expedition 75, the station's 75th resident crew. The station has now hosted 286 assembly spacewalks across more than a quarter of a century, and the final one of that long sequence — the one that completed the power system — was performed by two men who had never done this particular job before October 29.

That is worth noting because it captures something essential about how the station is operated. The crew that finishes a five-year program is not the crew that started it. The engineers who designed the iROSAs began their work more than 15 years ago, as Menon observed. The astronauts who installed the first wing in 2021 have long since returned to Earth. The program survived changes of crew, changes of administration, and changes of plan, and it was finished by two astronauts who arrived at the station only recently and learned the job in a week. Continuity, in a program like this, is not a matter of the same people doing the same thing for years. It is a matter of the work being documented, rehearsed, and handed over so carefully that the next crew can pick it up and finish it.

## A station, finished

There is a temptation to treat a solar array installation as routine maintenance, the orbital equivalent of changing a light bulb. It is not. The station's power system is the difference between a laboratory and a derelict, and for five years its upgrade was the station's most important construction project. Every spacewalk in the campaign carried risk — the risk of a suit problem, a dropped tool, a connector that would not seat — and every one of them had to succeed, because there are only eight channels and each one needed its wing.

The last of them is now in place. The station's truss, which began as a bare spine of girders in 1998, now carries sixteen wings: eight originals, weathered by a quarter century of sunlight and radiation, and eight new ones, bright and efficient, layered in front of them. From the ground, the station is a bright star crossing the evening sky. From the inside, it is a laboratory with more power than it has ever had, and a future measured in experiments rather than constraints.

Menon's four words at the end of the spacewalk — "We got it done!" — were not just a celebration. They were a statement of fact about a job that took five years, 18 spacewalks, eight wings, and two astronauts working a week apart to finish. The station's power upgrade is complete. What the station does with that power, in the years it has left, is now up to the scientists, the engineers, and the crews who will follow Menon and Kutryk out of the airlock.
