The closest rocky planet we know around a red dwarf has no air. That is the blunt verdict from the James Webb Space Telescope, delivered in a paper posted on November 12, 2026, and it matters far beyond one small, scorched world. LTT 1445 A b sits nearer to the so-called Cosmic Shoreline than any other rocky planet Webb has ever measured in eclipse — the theoretical dividing line between planets that hold onto their atmospheres and planets that are stripped bare by their stars. Its dayside glows exactly as hot as a dark, airless rock should glow. There is no blanket of gas softening the heat, no wind carrying warmth to the night side, no sky worth the name. And because this planet sits so close to the line, its nakedness tells astronomers something they could not establish from any other world: the shoreline cannot sit much further toward the airless side than the models already predict. The boundary is real, it is roughly where theory put it, and the search for habitable worlds around red dwarfs just got a little harder — and a lot more precise.

The result comes from the Rocky Worlds programme, a Director's Discretionary Time campaign on Webb that has become the sharpest tool astronomers have for answering a single question: do rocky planets around M dwarfs keep their atmospheres? The answer, world after world, keeps coming back no. LTT 1445 A b is the latest and most telling case, because it is the closest such system to Earth and the most precisely measured. At just 6.9 parsecs — about 22.4 light-years — in the constellation Eridanus, it is practically next door by interstellar standards. Its host star is the primary of a triple system of red dwarfs, and the planet itself is a near-twin of Earth in size and composition: about 1.34 times Earth's radius and about 2.87 times its mass, circling its star every 5.36 days. If any rocky world around a red dwarf was going to keep an atmosphere, the betting before Webb was that a world like this one, around a quiet, slowly rotating star, had a fighting chance. Webb has now measured it twice over, in two different ways, and the answer is the same both times. The sky is gone.

## The Measurement: Watching a Planet Vanish Behind Its Star

The technique is called secondary eclipse, and it is beautifully direct. A planet on a tight orbit passes in front of its star — a transit — and then, half an orbit later, slips behind it. While the planet is hidden, the telescope sees starlight alone. When it re-emerges, the telescope sees starlight plus the faint thermal glow of the planet's dayside. The difference between the two, measured to a precision of a few parts per million, is the planet's own heat shining in the infrared. That heat is a thermometer with a story to tell. A planet with a thick atmosphere circulates warmth from its dayside to its nightside, so its dayside runs cooler than a bare rock would. A planet with no atmosphere at all has nowhere to send the heat, so its dayside bakes at the maximum temperature physics allows for a dark surface absorbing its star's light. Measure the dayside temperature, compare it with the bare-rock limit, and you know whether there is air.

For this new study, an international team led by Jegug Ih pointed Webb's Mid-Infrared Instrument, MIRI, at the LTT 1445 system through its F1500W filter, which stares at a wavelength of 15 microns — right in the band where carbon dioxide, if present, would absorb and re-emit the planet's heat. The team observed two eclipses of planet b and two of its sibling planet c, spread across three visits, and reduced the data three independent ways to make sure no quirk of the analysis was driving the answer. They then folded in every earlier Webb observation of the system and 220 radial-velocity measurements, improving the precision of the planets' radii, masses, and orbital parameters by factors of 1.6 to 2.3. The headline numbers are small and exact. Planet b's eclipse depth came out at 157 parts per million, with an uncertainty of roughly plus 18 and minus 17. Planet c's came out at 154 parts per million, with a much wider uncertainty of plus 54 and minus 37, reflecting the difficulty of its measurement. Translated into temperature, planet b's dayside shines at 528 Kelvin, give or take about 30 to 40 degrees. Planet c's dayside, if its measurement is taken at face value, glows at 625 Kelvin, with an uncertainty spanning more than a hundred degrees either way. Both are consistent with the dark, bare-rock limit — the temperature of a world with no air at all.

That consistency is the whole result. If LTT 1445 A b carried a substantial atmosphere, its dayside would be cooler, because winds would ferry heat around to the night side. It is not cooler. It is exactly as hot as bare rock. The team then ran the numbers through a Bayesian retrieval, combining the new 15-micron photometry with the archival MIRI low-resolution spectrum of the planet, and put hard numbers on what is not there. Carbon dioxide, the gas most likely to survive on a hot rocky world and the gas the 15-micron filter is designed to sniff out, is limited to a partial pressure of less than 0.6 millibars. The total surface pressure of any atmosphere at all is less than about 0.1 bar — a tenth of Earth's sea-level pressure, and far too thin to count as a substantial sky. Forward models also ruled out a subtle escape hatch: thick aerosol layers that could, in principle, mimic a bare surface in transmission observations. The models say no such haze can hide an atmosphere here. The conclusion, stated plainly in the paper, is that planet b has no substantial atmosphere.

## What 157 Parts Per Million Means

To appreciate the measurement, it helps to grasp its scale. An eclipse depth of 157 parts per million means that when LTT 1445 A b slides behind its star, the total infrared light reaching Webb drops by 0.0157 per cent. That is the entire thermal glow of a planet more than twenty light-years away, picked out of the glare of its star by a telescope that can hold its gaze steady to a few parts per million over hours. The Rocky Worlds programme was built for exactly this kind of work, and the LTT 1445 observations show why it is succeeding: the precision of the new eclipse depths is good enough to separate a bare rock from even a thin atmosphere, at least for the better-measured of the two planets.

| Quantity | LTT 1445 A b | LTT 1445 A c |
|---|---|---|
| Distance from Earth | 6.9 parsecs (22.4 light-years) | Same system |
| Orbital period | 5.36 days | 3.12 days |
| Radius | 1.34 Earth radii | ~1.07–1.15 Earth radii |
| Mass | 2.87 Earth masses | 1.54 Earth masses |
| Equilibrium temperature | ~431 K | Hotter (closer orbit) |
| Eclipse depth (15 microns) | 157 parts per million | 154 parts per million |
| Dayside brightness temperature | 528 K | 625 K |
| Carbon dioxide limit | Less than 0.6 millibars | Thick CO2 atmospheres ruled out only above ~1 bar |
| Total surface pressure limit | Less than ~0.1 bar | Poorly constrained |
| Eclipse geometry | Full eclipse | Grazing eclipse |
| Atmosphere verdict | No substantial atmosphere | Thick CO2 skies excluded; thin air still possible |

The table makes the asymmetry of the result clear. Planet b is nailed down. Planet c is not — and the reason is geometry. Planet c's orbit is tilted just enough that, from our point of view, it only grazes the edge of its star during eclipse instead of disappearing cleanly behind it. A grazing eclipse is shallower, shorter, and harder to measure, and in this system the two planets' transits and eclipses also overlap in time, tangling their signals together. The paper is candid about the consequence: for planet c, the data rule out only thick, carbon-dioxide-bearing atmospheres of about one bar or more with at least one per cent carbon dioxide. Thinner, more transparent gases, or a bare rock surface of various kinds, remain possible. The team also flags a practical lesson for future work: the observations revealed instrumental settling behaviour when the filter was changed and during breaks in exposure, small detector ramps that future eclipse programmes will need to plan around if they want the precision these measurements demand.

## The Cosmic Shoreline, Explained

The reason one airless planet can matter so much is the idea it tests. The Cosmic Shoreline is a theoretical dividing line in the population of rocky planets, first drawn in detail by planetary scientists studying which worlds keep their atmospheres and which do not. On one side of the line sit planets whose gravity is strong enough, and whose stellar irradiation is gentle enough, that gases stay bound for billions of years. On the other side sit planets that lose their skies to the violence of their environment — the ultraviolet and X-ray radiation of their stars, and the charged particles of the stellar wind, which heat the upper atmosphere until it boils away into space. A planet's fate depends on the balance between the energy pouring in from its star and the gravity holding its gases down, and the shoreline is where those two forces exactly balance. Earth sits comfortably on the safe side. Venus, despite its crushing carbon dioxide atmosphere, sits near the line and keeps its sky only because it started with so much gas and its gravity is strong. Mars sits on the wrong side, and its thin air is the scar of the loss.

For planets around red dwarfs, the shoreline is a harsher judge than it is around Sun-like stars. M dwarfs are small and cool, so their habitable zones — the orbits where liquid water could exist — lie very close in, where a planet is bathed in intense radiation and, in its youth, in the flares and X-ray tantrums that young red dwarfs are famous for. A rocky planet in the habitable zone of a red dwarf may spend its first billion years being sandblasted. Whether any of them keep an atmosphere is, as the papers in this field put it, a central goal of exoplanet astronomy — because red dwarfs are the most common stars in the galaxy, and their small rocky planets are the most common planets we know. If none of them can keep air, the census of potentially habitable worlds shrinks dramatically, and the search for life shifts decisively toward planets around stars more like the Sun.

That is why the exact position of the shoreline matters, and why LTT 1445 A b is such a valuable calibration point. The new paper states it directly: LTT 1445 A b is the planet closest to the canonical X-ray and ultraviolet-driven Cosmic Shoreline observed in eclipse so far. It sits very near the boundary, on the dry, airless side of it. And it is airless. If the true shoreline lay much further toward the airless side than the models predict — if planets like this one could keep their skies — then this planet should have kept its atmosphere. It did not. The shoreline, therefore, cannot lie much further toward the airless side than nominally predicted. The models had the line in roughly the right place. That is a negative result that sharpens a theory, and in the slow science of exoplanets, sharpening the theory is what lets the next observations aim at the right targets instead of the wrong ones.

It is worth noting what the result does not say. It does not say that no rocky planet around a red dwarf can have an atmosphere. The shoreline is a line, and planets on the far side of it — larger worlds, worlds further from their stars, worlds around quieter stars — may still hold their skies. It does not say that LTT 1445 A b never had an atmosphere, only that it does not have a substantial one now; it may have been born with a thick primordial envelope and lost it to billions of years of stellar wind, or it may have formed dry. And it does not close the book on the system itself, because the third candidate planet in the system, LTT 1445 A d, orbits on a 24.3-day period near the inner edge of the star's habitable zone, and it has not yet been probed this way at all. What the result does say is that the boundary is where theory put it, and that the easiest, nearest test case failed to keep its air.

## A Triple-Star Neighbourhood

Part of what makes this system such a compelling target is its strangeness. LTT 1445 is not a single star with a planet. It is three red dwarfs in a hierarchical dance: the primary, LTT 1445 A, with its planets, and a tight pair of smaller red dwarfs, B and C, orbiting each other every 36 years while the pair circles A at a distance of about 34 astronomical units, once every 250 years or so. All three stars are M dwarfs, with masses between roughly 0.16 and 0.26 times the mass of the Sun, and spectral types running from M3 to M4. From the surface of planet b, if it had a surface anyone could stand on, there would be three red suns in the sky — one bright and close, two dimmer companions hanging together in the dark. The system's discoverers gave their 2019 paper exactly that title: "Three Red Suns in the Sky."

The planets were found the way most small transiting planets are found: by watching starlight dip. NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite, TESS, caught planet b crossing the face of its star in 2019. It circles every 5.3588 days at a distance of just 0.038 astronomical units — about a tenth of Mercury's distance from the Sun — and its equilibrium temperature, the temperature it would have if it absorbed starlight evenly with no atmosphere at all, is about 431 to 433 Kelvin, roughly 160 degrees Celsius. A second planet, c, was found in 2021 on a still tighter 3.12-day orbit, close to a 12:7 resonance with b, meaning the two planets' orbits beat against each other in a near-rhythm of twelve laps of c to seven of b. A third candidate, d, turned up in radial-velocity data in 2022 on a 24.3-day orbit. Its mass is at least 2.72 Earth masses, but it has not been seen to transit, so its size is unknown.

As of late 2022, LTT 1445 was the second-closest transiting exoplanet system known, and the closest one whose host star is an M dwarf. That proximity is precisely what makes it observable at all. Secondary eclipses of small rocky planets are among the hardest measurements in astronomy, and they get dramatically harder with distance. A system 22 light-years away, with a bright host star, is a gift. It is also, by a quirk of the system's geometry, a system in which the planets' signals overlap and tangle, which is why this result took three independent reductions and a joint fit with 220 radial velocities to pin down. The precision gained — factors of 1.6 to 2.3 on the planets' radii, masses, and orbital parameters — is itself a product of the work, and it will make every future observation of this system sharper.

The star itself is unusually quiet for a red dwarf, which is part of why the system looked promising. LTT 1445 A rotates slowly and showed no significant flare activity in Chandra X-ray observations, a calmness that suggested its planets might have been spared the worst of the atmospheric stripping that young, active red dwarfs inflict. The new result shows that even a quiet star, over billions of years, is enough. The X-ray and ultraviolet output of a red dwarf, integrated across its lifetime, can evidently strip a planet that sits as close in as b does, even without dramatic flares. That is a sobering note for the many other quiet red dwarfs on the target lists, and it is also a clue: if the stripping is driven by the steady, cumulative X-ray and ultraviolet flux rather than by flares, then the shoreline's position is set by physics that can be modelled, measured, and predicted for other systems.

## The Sibling Problem: Planet c's Grazing Eclipse

Planet c deserves its own paragraph, because its story is a lesson in how astronomy actually works. On paper, c should be an easier measurement than b. It is smaller and closer to its star, so its dayside is hotter and its eclipse should be deeper relative to its size. In practice, its orbit is tilted just enough that it never fully disappears behind the star. Imagine watching a coin slide behind a dinner plate, but only ever catching its edge. The dip in light is smaller, the timing is harder to pin down, and the depth you measure depends on exactly how much of the planet was hidden. Add to that the fact that the two planets' transits and eclipses overlap in the light curve, and the measurement of c becomes a careful exercise in disentangling two signals at once.

The result for c is therefore weaker, and the paper does not pretend otherwise. Its eclipse depth of 154 parts per million comes with an uncertainty of plus 54 and minus 37, and its brightness temperature of 625 Kelvin carries an uncertainty of more than a hundred degrees. The data exclude thick, carbon-dioxide-dominated atmospheres like that of Venus — skies of a bar or more with at least one per cent carbon dioxide — but they leave open thinner, more transparent gases and a range of bare rock surfaces. That is still a useful constraint, and it points to the obvious next step: more eclipses of c, observed with the same care, until its geometry is no longer the limiting factor. The authors note that future observing strategies will need to account for the instrumental settling they saw in this campaign, a small operational detail that could make the difference between a marginal detection and a solid one.

The contrast with b is instructive. Both planets live in the same system, around the same star, under the same stellar wind. If c turns out to have retained a thin atmosphere while b has none, that would be a puzzle worth solving — perhaps a difference in composition, or in the history of how each planet formed. If c turns out to be bare as well, the system becomes a clean pair of data points on the airless side of the shoreline. Either way, the system has more to give, and the fact that this paper improved the planets' measured parameters by up to a factor of 2.3 means the next campaign starts from a firmer footing.

## Timeline: LTT 1445 A b's Road to the Verdict

The new result did not arrive out of nowhere. It is the latest step in a seven-year chain of observations, each one tightening the picture of this small system.

- **2019 — Discovery.** TESS detects LTT 1445 A b transiting its star every 5.36 days. Follow-up work establishes it as a terrestrial planet in a triple M-dwarf system just 6.9 parsecs away, the closest transiting system around a red dwarf.
- **2021 — A second planet.** Radial-velocity and transit work reveals LTT 1445 A c on a 3.12-day orbit, with a mass of about 1.54 Earth masses, and a probable grazing transit that will complicate later measurements.
- **2022 — Masses pinned down.** Precise radial velocities measure planet b at 2.87 Earth masses and confirm an Earth-like composition. A third candidate, d, is reported on a 24.3-day orbit near the inner edge of the habitable zone. Chandra X-ray observations show the host star is quiet, with no significant flare activity.
- **2023–2025 — The first Webb look.** Webb observes three secondary eclipses of planet b with MIRI's low-resolution spectrometer, covering 5 to 12 microns. The dayside temperature comes out at 513 to 525 Kelvin, consistent with a bare rock, and the eclipse arrives 2.4 minutes later than a circular orbit would predict.
- **August 2026 — The method paper.** Wogan and colleagues publish a climate-constrained Bayesian framework in the Astrophysical Journal Letters, using the existing LTT 1445 A b spectrum as its case study. Their two-sigma upper limits — carbon dioxide below about 0.1 bar, water below a thousandth of a bar — already point toward a bare rock, and they show that scheduled 15-micron observations could detect even a thin permitted atmosphere if the precision reaches 20 parts per million.
- **November 12, 2026 — The verdict.** Ih and colleagues post the new Rocky Worlds results to arXiv: two eclipses of each planet at 15 microns, a joint fit with all existing Webb data and 220 radial velocities, and the conclusion that planet b has no substantial atmosphere, with carbon dioxide below 0.6 millibars and total surface pressure below about 0.1 bar.

That sequence matters because it shows how exoplanet science converges. No single observation settled this question. The transits found the planets, the radial velocities weighed them, the first Webb spectrum hinted at the answer, a methods paper quantified what the hint meant, and the dedicated 15-micron campaign closed the loop. Each step made the next one possible, and the final precision — an eclipse depth measured to about 11 per cent — is the product of all of them.

## Why a Bare Rock Still Matters

There is a temptation to file a result like this under "nothing found" and move on. That would be a mistake, and the history of the field explains why. The search for atmospheres on rocky planets around red dwarfs has been, so far, a catalogue of empty skies. Webb's eclipse measurements of other small worlds have repeatedly returned dayside temperatures at the bare-rock limit, and each one has moved the shoreline debate from speculation toward measurement. LTT 1445 A b is the most precise point on that map yet, and the closest to the line. A null result at the shoreline is not the absence of science; it is the calibration of a theory, and theories that survive contact with their hardest test cases are the ones worth trusting.

The contrast with other recent Webb results sharpens the picture. Some lava worlds have surprised astronomers by keeping their skies against the odds. <a href="/blog/toi-561-b-lava-planet-atmosphere/">TOI-561 b, a molten world that refuses to lose its sky</a>, appears to hold onto a mineral-cloud atmosphere despite being scorched by its star, reflecting about three quarters of the light that hits it. <a href="/blog/toi-2431b-hubble-secondary-eclipse-lava-world-november-2026/">TOI-2431b, a lava world glowing at 2,524 K in Hubble's secondary eclipse</a>, shows how the same eclipse technique can measure a planet's dayside heat in exquisite detail. And on the imaging side, <a href="/blog/webb-flip-51-eridani-b-november-2026/">Webb's FLIP technique pulled 51 Eridani b out of two-year-old data</a>, making the telescope up to fifteen times more sensitive to faint planets without changing a single piece of hardware. LTT 1445 A b sits on the other side of that ledger: a world where the sky is gone and the measurement says so with unusual clarity. The two kinds of result need each other. The planets that keep their atmospheres tell us what retention looks like; the planets that lose them tell us where the line falls.

There is also a practical consequence for the search for life. The Rocky Worlds programme exists because the question of atmospheres around red dwarfs is the gatekeeper question for habitability. A planet without an atmosphere cannot have liquid water on its surface, cannot moderate its climate, and cannot shield its surface from its star's radiation. If the shoreline sits where the models say it does, then the habitable-zone planets around red dwarfs that sit on the airless side are not habitable in any Earth-like sense, however inviting their orbits look. The search must then focus on the worlds on the safe side of the line — larger planets, planets around quieter stars, planets a little further out — and on testing whether the line's position depends on things we have not yet measured, like a planet's initial water inventory or its magnetic field. None of that makes the search futile. It makes it targeted. The worst outcome in this field is not a bare rock; it is a decade spent observing the wrong planets.

For LTT 1445 itself, the work is not finished. Planet d, the unconfirmed candidate on a 24.3-day orbit near the inner edge of the habitable zone, is the system's most intriguing loose end. If it is real, and if it transits, it would be a test of the shoreline from the other side — a world far enough out that it might have kept what its siblings lost. Confirming it, weighing it, and eventually watching it slip behind its star is the kind of long, patient work this system rewards. Planet c, too, deserves a second campaign, with enough eclipses to beat down its grazing geometry. And the instrumental lessons of this run — the settling ramps, the filter changes, the overlapping events — will shape how the Rocky Worlds programme observes its next targets.

## What Comes Next

The paper is a preprint, posted to arXiv on November 12 and submitted to the AAS journals, so the formal peer review is still ahead. But its numbers are already in the open, and they will be tested quickly. Other teams will re-reduce the same data, the way the authors themselves reduced it three ways, and the eclipse depths will either hold or move. The history of this system suggests they will hold: the earlier MIRI spectrum pointed the same way, the Bayesian framework paper pointed the same way, and now the dedicated 15-micron photometry points the same way, with better precision than either. Convergence is what a real result looks like.

Beyond this system, the Rocky Worlds programme continues. Every rocky planet it measures in eclipse adds a point to the shoreline map, and the map is getting crowded on the airless side. The question now is where the first point on the other side will appear — the first rocky world around a red dwarf that demonstrably keeps a substantial atmosphere. When it comes, it will be news precisely because results like this one have made the expectation clear. Until then, the shoreline stands where the models drew it, and LTT 1445 A b stands as its nearest, sharpest marker: a small dark world, twenty-two light-years away, with three red suns in its sky and no air above its scorched ground — and a measurement that tells us exactly where the sky ends.
