Three years ago, a single faint smudge of X-rays rewrote the textbooks on how the first black holes were born. The galaxy UHZ1, glimpsed by the James Webb Space Telescope when the universe was barely 480 million years old, appeared to host a black hole weighing tens of millions of suns — as massive as all the stars in its host galaxy combined. It was hailed as the first "outsize black hole galaxy," a direct-collapse monster that seemed to prove black holes could be born big. Now a sweeping reanalysis has taken that crown away. The X-ray signal, the infrared glow, and the dust that should have hidden a buried giant have all failed to show up on a second look, and the team behind the new study says there is no compelling evidence that the famous black hole exists at all.

## The 2023 claim that rewrote the rulebook

UHZ1 first appeared in the deep infrared imaging of the James Webb Space Telescope, in the direction of the massive galaxy cluster Abell 2744. Webb's data placed the little galaxy some 13.2 billion light-years away, meaning its light set out when the universe was only about 3 percent of its current age. At a redshift of about 10, it was one of the most distant galaxies ever seen. Then NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory, staring at the same patch of sky for more than two weeks, picked up a small excess of high-energy X-rays from exactly that spot. The signal was extremely faint, and Chandra could detect it at all only because the gravity of the foreground cluster Abell 2744 acted as a lens, magnifying the light by a factor of four.

The astronomers who found it interpreted the X-rays as the signature of a black hole feeding on gas — an active galactic nucleus — buried in thick gas and dust. Working from the brightness and energy of the X-rays, they estimated the black hole weighed between 10 million and 100 million times the mass of the Sun, with a central estimate of about 40 million solar masses if it was feeding at the Eddington limit, the maximum rate at which a black hole can swallow matter before its own radiation pushes the food away. The bolometric luminosity implied by the X-rays was around 5 × 10^45 ergs per second, the output of a full-blown quasar shining less than half a billion years after the Big Bang.

What made the claim electrifying was the ratio. In galaxies in the nearby universe, the central black hole typically holds only about a tenth of a percent of the mass of all the stars in its host. In UHZ1, the black hole seemed to weigh about as much as the entire stellar population of the galaxy. That is not how black holes are supposed to grow. A black hole born from the collapse of a single massive star starts at tens of solar masses and needs billions of years of steady feeding, or a long chain of mergers, to reach tens of millions of suns. At 480 million years after the Big Bang, there simply should not have been enough time.

The discovery therefore looked like the first confirmed example of a completely different birth channel: the direct collapse of a vast cloud of pristine gas into a "heavy seed" black hole of around ten thousand solar masses, which then grew quickly into a giant. Theorists had predicted exactly such objects years earlier. In 2017, Priyamvada Natarajan of Yale University and colleagues described a transient class of objects they called outsize black hole galaxies, in which a heavy seed forms in a satellite halo, merges quickly with a young star-forming galaxy, and spends a brief cosmic moment with a black hole outweighing its stars. UHZ1 matched the template on nearly every count: the high redshift, the X-ray brightness relative to its infrared light, the shape of its light across wavelengths, and even a stretched, extended shape in Webb's images suggestive of a recent merger. In a paper published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters in January 2024, Natarajan and her co-authors declared UHZ1 the first detected outsize black hole galaxy candidate, and the press release from the Chandra X-ray Center in November 2023 called it the most distant black hole ever detected in X-rays.

It was a beautiful story, and it was built on a very small number of photons. That fragility was noted even at the time. Webb's own spectrum of UHZ1 showed no sign of a black hole at all, which the discoverers attributed to heavy dust blocking the emission. The key diagnostic features that would have confirmed an active nucleus lay outside the wavelength range of the instruments used. "Independent confirmation was therefore urgently needed," the authors of the new reanalysis write in their paper. Three years later, that confirmation has arrived — and it points the other way.

## A closer look at the X-rays

The new study is led by Fan Zou of the University of Michigan, and it was published in The Astrophysical Journal on October 14, 2026. Rather than taking the original detection at face value, Zou's team went back to the raw data and started over. They reprocessed every second of the old Chandra observations, and then they added 0.95 million seconds — roughly eleven days — of brand-new Chandra observing time on UHZ1. They also analyzed new mid-infrared data from Webb's MIRI instrument, which extends the wavelength coverage into the range where a dust-buried black hole should betray itself. In effect, they gave the claim the independent test it had always needed, with more data and better tools than the original team had.

The X-rays did not survive the scrutiny. The reanalysis found a weaker detection than the original claim, and — more damningly — the result changed depending on exactly how the X-ray image was aligned with Webb's infrared image. Aligning images from two different telescopes to sub-arcsecond precision is delicate work, and the team simulated many plausible alignments to see how much the answer depended on that choice. Only 0.1 percent of those simulated alignments produced a signal as strong as the one originally reported. In other words, the original detection sat at the extreme lucky end of the range of reasonable alignments, not in the middle of it.

The new Chandra time delivered the second blow. A real, stable X-ray source should get clearer the longer you look at it: more observing time collects more photons, and the signal should rise above the noise. Instead, adding nearly a million seconds of new Chandra data added almost nothing to the signal. A genuine quasar shining steadily at the claimed brightness would have grown brighter in the combined dataset. It did not. Whatever produced the original excess, it does not behave like a persistent, luminous X-ray source sitting at the center of UHZ1's host galaxy.

The team is careful about what this does and does not prove. A weaker X-ray signal is not, by itself, proof that no black hole is there. It is proof that the evidence for one is far weaker than advertised — and that the original claim rested on an alignment choice and a photon count that do not hold up under reprocessing. The rest of the new paper is devoted to asking whether any black hole as powerful as the one originally proposed could still be hiding in UHZ1, unseen by every instrument pointed at it.

## UHZ1: from record-breaker to question mark

The rise and fall of the UHZ1 black hole is a short story in cosmic terms, but it spans three years of observation, argument, and re-observation. Its milestones show how a headline discovery becomes a settled fact — or doesn't.

## Timeline

- **August 2023** — The preprint by Natarajan and colleagues first presents UHZ1 as a candidate overmassive black hole galaxy, based on Chandra X-rays and Webb infrared imaging.
- **November 6, 2023** — The Chandra X-ray Center announces the discovery as the most distant black hole ever detected in X-rays, in the galaxy UHZ1 behind the cluster Abell 2744.
- **January 2024** — The interpretation is published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, naming UHZ1 the first detected "outsize black hole galaxy" and evidence for heavy black hole seeds formed by direct collapse.
- **2024–2025** — UHZ1 becomes a touchstone in debates about how the first supermassive black holes formed, cited as the clearest case for the heavy-seed channel.
- **October 14, 2026** — Zou and colleagues publish their reanalysis in The Astrophysical Journal, reporting no compelling evidence for the claimed active nucleus.
- **November 15, 2026** — The reanalysis is reported publicly, three years to the month after the original announcement.

## Why the infrared silence matters

If the X-ray reanalysis cast doubt on the original claim, the infrared data may be what buries it. The whole point of the 2023 interpretation was that the black hole was buried — a so-called Compton-thick quasar, wrapped in gas so dense that even X-rays struggle to escape and visible light stands no chance. But buried black holes cannot hide completely. The dust that swallows a quasar's light heats up and re-radiates that energy as infrared glow. A quasar as luminous as the one proposed for UHZ1, wrapped in that much dust, should shine brightly in the mid-infrared, like a furnace seen through a blanket: you cannot see the flames, but the blanket itself glows.

That is what Webb's Mid-Infrared Instrument, MIRI, was brought in to check. The new study analyzed fresh MIRI data covering the wavelengths where that heated-dust glow should appear. It found nothing of the kind. A non-detection is not proof that nothing is there — every instrument has a limit, and a sufficiently faint source can hide below it. So the team did the honest calculation: they took two different templates for what a hidden black hole's light should look like, scaled each one up until it just reached MIRI's detection limit, and asked how bright a buried quasar could possibly be without MIRI seeing it. The answer was devastating for the original claim. The maximum brightness allowed by the MIRI non-detection turned out to be 10 to 1,000 times lower than the brightness the original X-ray claim required. A black hole as powerful as originally proposed is ruled out — not by a missing signal, but by a ceiling the signal cannot exceed.

It is worth pausing on the logic, because it is the kind of reasoning that separates a tentative detection from a settled one. The original claim asked the reader to accept two things at once: that a tremendously luminous black hole was present, and that it was hidden well enough to leave no trace in Webb's spectrum. The reanalysis shows those two requirements contradict each other. Hidden well enough means wrapped in dust; wrapped in dust means glowing in the mid-infrared; and the mid-infrared is empty. You can have a hidden black hole, or you can have a luminous one. The data say you cannot have both in UHZ1.

## The two escape routes that closed

The team did not stop at the MIRI limit. Good skepticism means hunting for the loopholes in your own argument before someone else finds them, and the paper walks through the two ways a luminous black hole might still escape detection. Neither survives contact with the data.

The first escape route is a black hole with no dust at all. If the gas around the black hole contained no dust, there would be no heated blanket and therefore no mid-infrared glow — the non-detection would be expected. But dust-free gas is transparent, which means the black hole's own visible and ultraviolet light would shine straight through and into Webb's instruments. That light was not seen either. A dust-free quasar cannot hide in the infrared and stay invisible in the optical at the same time.

The second escape route is dust that is far away and cold. Dust at a great distance from the black hole would be heated only weakly, so it would glow in the far-infrared rather than the mid-infrared, beyond MIRI's reach. But that scenario requires a large reservoir of cold dust, and the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, ALMA, has looked for exactly that. ALMA found very little dust in UHZ1, and the galaxy's own starlight is barely dimmed, which is the opposite of what a dust-shrouded system should look like. There is simply not enough dust in the galaxy, hot or cold, to hide a quasar of the claimed power.

With both exits closed, the authors state their conclusion plainly: "Our principal conclusion is that no compelling evidence for such an AGN exists." The emission lines Webb does see from UHZ1 are consistent with something far more ordinary — a young, low-mass, metal-poor galaxy busily forming stars, with a stellar mass of around 100 million suns. No black hole is required to explain anything in the data. The galaxy that was supposed to be the poster child for heavy black hole seeds may be nothing more exotic than a small galaxy doing what small galaxies in the early universe did: making stars.

## What UHZ1 is instead

That alternative is not a consolation prize; it is interesting in its own right, and it fits a pattern astronomers have been seeing throughout Webb's survey of the early universe. The first galaxies were small, poor in heavy elements, and ferociously star-forming, and many of the objects that were first announced as monster black holes have turned out, on closer inspection, to be vigorous starbursts whose light was misread. The emission lines that once looked like the fingerprint of a feeding black hole can also be produced by hot young stars, by shocks in galactic gas, and by the crowded, violent conditions of galaxies assembling themselves for the first time. Telling those possibilities apart requires exactly the kind of multi-wavelength follow-up that Zou's team has now done for UHZ1: X-rays to catch the feeding, mid-infrared to catch the hidden heat, and millimeter waves to weigh the dust.

The stakes of getting this right go beyond one galaxy. The question UHZ1 was supposed to answer — how did black holes weighing a billion suns exist less than a billion years after the Big Bang? — is still open, and the heavy-seed channel is still a live and serious idea. The <a href="/blog/jwst-little-red-dots-november-2026-direct-collapse-black-holes/">little red dots direct-collapse black holes</a> that Webb keeps finding in the early universe may yet turn out to be galaxies growing their first supermassive black holes from collapsed gas clouds. What the UHZ1 reanalysis changes is the strength of the evidence, not the theory. The best candidate for a confirmed direct-collapse black hole has been withdrawn from the evidence column, and the theory must now stand on its other supports until a stronger case is built.

That is also why the comparison with the newest Webb black hole claims matters. The <a href="/blog/marta-3887-black-hole-grew-faster-than-galaxy-november-2026/">MARTA-3887 black hole</a>, reported just this month, is a very different kind of case: a black hole of about 3.3 million solar masses in a galaxy of roughly 145 million solar masses in stars, seen about 12 billion years ago, where the evidence rests on a broad hydrogen line showing gas moving at about 1,270 kilometres per second — a signal present in hydrogen but absent in oxygen, which points to a black hole rather than an ordinary outflow. MARTA-3887's claim is modest in scale and specific in its evidence, and its authors present it as a candidate, with a supernova or a thick outflow still listed as possible mimics. UHZ1's claim was maximal in scale and thin in corroboration, and it is now a case study in what happens when a maximal claim meets maximal scrutiny.

The <a href="/blog/ic-342-intermediate-mass-black-hole-november-2026/">IC 342 intermediate-mass black hole</a> offers the opposite lesson in how such claims are earned. There, astronomers used the Keck telescope to measure the motions of stars around the galaxy's nucleus, finding a central velocity dispersion rising to about 49 kilometres per second and weighing the dark mass at about 195,000 suns. It is a small black hole by cosmic standards, but the measurement is dynamical — built from the gravity of stars, not from a handful of X-ray photons — and that makes it hard to argue with. The contrast is the point: in black hole astronomy, the size of the claim matters less than the solidity of the evidence, and solidity comes from independent lines of measurement that agree.

## What this means for heavy seeds

None of this kills the direct-collapse idea. Heavy seeds remain the most elegant solution to the timing problem of the first quasars, and theorists have not abandoned them — they have simply lost their favorite example. The simulations that produce heavy seeds, in which a pristine gas cloud collapses without fragmenting into stars, predict black holes starting at around ten thousand solar masses. Those seeds then need to grow fast, feeding at a healthy fraction of the Eddington limit for hundreds of millions of years, to reach the billion-solar-mass quasars seen at redshift 7 and beyond. That growth is demanding but not impossible, and other lines of evidence — the abundance of little red dots, the masses of the earliest quasars, the chemistry of the oldest stars — still point toward some black holes starting big.

What the UHZ1 episode does change is the standard of proof. The original claim was published in one of the most prestigious journals in the field, announced by NASA, and repeated in textbooks and talks for three years. Its undoing did not come from a rival theory or a press release, but from the slow, unglamorous work of reprocessing old data, adding new observations, and checking how sensitive the result was to choices that had seemed like technicalities. The alignment of two images. The behavior of a signal as observing time accumulates. The brightness ceiling set by a non-detection. These are not the parts of astronomy that make headlines, but they are the parts that decide whether a headline survives.

There is also a quieter lesson here about the pace of Webb-era astronomy. The telescope is discovering distant galaxies faster than any team can follow them up, and the first interpretation of a strange object is often the most exciting one. The incentive to announce is enormous; the follow-up observations that confirm or refute a claim take years to schedule, execute, and analyze. In that gap, a claim can harden into received wisdom. UHZ1 shows that the correction mechanism works — the data eventually got its hearing — but it also shows how long the gap can be. For three years, the "most distant black hole ever detected in X-rays" was a fact in every news story about early black holes. Now it is a cautionary tale, and the citation that future papers will reach for is the reanalysis.

## The debate is not over

It would be a mistake to read the new paper as the last word. Science does not end with a single reanalysis, however thorough, and the original team may yet respond with its own reprocessing or with new observations that revive the claim. The X-ray signal did not vanish entirely — it weakened, and its significance depends on alignment choices that reasonable astronomers could still argue about. A fainter, less obscured black hole could still be hiding in UHZ1 below the limits the new study sets; what is ruled out is specifically a black hole as luminous as the one originally proposed. And there is always the possibility that a future instrument — a more sensitive X-ray telescope, a deeper infrared spectrum — will find something the current data cannot.

That open-endedness is a feature, not a flaw. The new paper does not claim that UHZ1 has no black hole; it claims that the evidence for one is not compelling, and that the galaxy is fully consistent with having none at all. That is a narrower statement than the headlines suggest, and it is the statement the data support. The burden now sits with anyone who wants to revive the monster: bring a detection that does not depend on one alignment, that grows with observing time, and that leaves a trace in the infrared. Until then, the record for the most distant X-ray black hole is, once again, vacant.

For the wider field, the practical consequence is a shift in attention. The search for heavy seeds will move on to the next candidates — the little red dots, the new Webb galaxies with broad emission lines, the objects whose evidence rests on more than a few photons. The instruments are already in place: Chandra is still watching, Webb's MIRI is still listening in the mid-infrared, and ALMA is still weighing the dust. The UHZ1 story will be remembered not because a black hole vanished, but because astronomy's self-correction worked in public, on a famous object, in full view. A galaxy that was supposed to prove how the first giants were born has instead proved something just as valuable: that in science, even the most beautiful claim has to survive being checked.
