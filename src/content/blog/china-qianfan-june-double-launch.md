Forty-eight hours. Two launch pads, 2,000 kilometers apart. Thirty-six satellites now circling the Earth in polar orbit.

On the evening of Thursday, June 4, 2026, a Long March 6A rocket rose from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center in north China's Shanxi Province at 7:39 p.m. Beijing Time, carrying 18 satellites bound for the Qianfan — "Thousand Sails" — broadband constellation. Barely 19 hours later, on Friday afternoon, a Long March 8 lifted off from the Hainan commercial spacecraft launch site on China's southern island province at 2:34 p.m. Beijing Time with 18 more of the same satellites. Both payloads entered their preset orbits successfully.

The back-to-back missions were the 11th and 12th deployment groups of the first-generation Qianfan constellation, and they pushed the network's total launched satellite count to exactly 200 — a milestone that had been years in the making and that signals the project's recovery from a difficult 2025.

## Thursday night in Taiyuan: the 11th group

The first of the two missions began in the hills of Shanxi. According to the official account carried by Xinhua, the modified Long March-6 carrier rocket lifted off from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center at 7:39 p.m. local time on June 4. The 18 satellites it carried constitute the eleventh batch of the first generation of the Spacesail Constellation, the English-language trade name for Qianfan, developed by Shanghai SpaceSail Technologies Co. Ltd. to provide what the company describes as low-latency, high-speed and ultra-reliable satellite broadband internet services to global users.

The launch marked the 648th flight of the Long March carrier rocket series, according to the launch center — a running tally that underscores just how routine orbital launch has become for China's state space program, which is now adding to that count roughly once every two or three days.

The Long March 6A is a workhorse of China's constellation-building effort. A new-generation medium-lift rocket with a modular design, it has become the default carrier for the standard 18-satellite Qianfan batches, launching them from Taiyuan's inland pads into the polar sun-synchronous orbits where the first-generation network is being assembled. The 18-satellite batch size has become something of a Qianfan signature: a full fairing of flat-packed spacecraft, standardized for rapid deployment.

## Friday afternoon in Hainan: the 12th group

Less than a day later, the scene shifted to China's tropical south. At 2:34 p.m. Beijing Time on June 5 — 06:34 UTC — the Long March 8 Y9 vehicle lifted off from Commercial Launch Pad 1 at the Hainan commercial spacecraft launch site in the Wenchang area, carrying another 18 satellites toward polar orbit.

The spacecraft on this flight were produced by the Innovation Academy for Microsatellites of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, the Shanghai-based research institute that has become one of the principal manufacturers for the Qianfan program. Official sources confirmed that the 18 satellites constitute the twelfth batch of the first generation of the Spacesail Constellation — and with their arrival in orbit, the total number of Qianfan satellites launched reached 200.

The mission was the third flight of the Long March 8 rocket in 2026, coming just 18 days after its previous mission on May 17 — a turnaround pace that reflects the rocket's growing role as a dedicated constellation-deployment vehicle. The Long March 8 is a new-generation medium-lift liquid-fueled rocket designed specifically with commercial satellite constellation missions in mind, and its manifest is increasingly dominated by Qianfan flights. Together with its larger sibling the Long March 8A — rated for 5 tonnes and 7 tonnes to sun-synchronous orbit respectively — the Long March 8 family gives China a modern, purpose-built fleet for filling out low-Earth-orbit networks.

## What Qianfan actually is

For all the launch-day excitement, the two missions are chapters in a much larger story. Qianfan, sometimes rendered in English as "Thousand Sails" and marketed internationally as SpaceSail, is operated by Shanghai Spacesail Technologies Co. Ltd. with backing from the Shanghai local government. It is one of three great broadband constellations now being assembled in low Earth orbit — alongside SpaceX's Starlink, the undisputed leader, and China's state-backed Guowang (SatNet) network — and it is the most commercially oriented of the Chinese entries.

The satellites themselves are compact, standardized spacecraft. Each weighs roughly 300 kilograms and uses a flat-pack design with a single solar array, a configuration chosen to squeeze as many spacecraft as possible into a rocket fairing in two parallel stacks. For maneuvering in orbit, they carry electric Hall-effect thrusters burning krypton, generating about 20 millinewtons of thrust. Their communications payloads operate in Ku, Q and V bands — the high-frequency spectrum suited to broadband data services.

The deployment plan, as laid out in Chinese regulatory filings and industry tracking, is ambitious. As of late 2025, the program's stated aims called for 324 satellites to be launched in 2026, another 324 in 2027, some 4,000 across 2028 and 2029, and 5,000 in 2030, toward roughly 15,000 total satellites approved to operate. The initial operational constellation is targeted at 1,296 satellites by 2027, with long-term expansion plans reaching 12,000 and beyond. Those numbers put Qianfan in the same order of magnitude as its rivals: a genuine attempt at global coverage rather than a regional demonstration.

The business case is equally international. Shanghai Spacesail Technologies has said it aims to provide space-based internet connectivity in China and abroad — naming Brazil, Malaysia, Kazakhstan and Türkiye among its early target markets, as well as aviation connectivity — with commercial service possible as soon as the end of 2026. The company has also said the network's growing density has completed the necessary networking to begin supporting automatic identification systems for connecting maritime vessels at sea, an early practical application of the constellation's global coverage.

## A comeback after a bruising 2025

The June double header carried extra significance because of what happened the year before. Industry trackers note that the June 5 launch was the fifth Qianfan deployment in the last month, part of a return to routine operations after a period in which several Qianfan satellites failed in orbit during 2025. The constellation only restarted proper orbital deployments about two months before June — making the 200-satellite milestone a marker of recovery as much as of growth.

That history matters because megaconstellations live or die on reliability. A broadband network is only as good as its weakest spacecraft, and investors, regulators and early customers all watch failure rates closely. The steady drumbeat of successful 18-satellite batches in the spring of 2026 — including the new Long March 12B's maiden flight on June 1 carrying a small Qianfan group from the Dongfeng commercial zone — suggests the program has moved past its teething troubles and into the industrial phase of constellation building: standardized satellites, standardized batches, standardized rockets.

The cadence is the story. Five Qianfan launches in roughly a month, two of them 19 hours apart, from pads at opposite ends of the country, demonstrates a launch infrastructure that can sustain parallel campaigns. Taiyuan handles the polar-orbit batches on the Long March 6A; Wenchang's commercial pads handle the Long March 8 family; and newer commercial vehicles like the Long March 12B are beginning to join the manifest. For a program that needs to average hundreds of satellites per year to hit its targets, that kind of throughput is not optional — it is the entire business plan.

## The rockets behind the numbers

Both vehicles used in the double header deserve attention in their own right, because they represent two different philosophies of Chinese launch.

The Long March 6A is the proven workhorse. Developed as a modular, solid-and-liquid hybrid launcher, it has settled into a rhythm of hauling full 18-satellite Qianfan complements to polar orbit from Taiyuan. Its reliability and standardized mission profile make it the backbone of the deployment campaign — the rocket you choose when the goal is to move metal on schedule.

The Long March 8 is the newer, more specialized machine. A new-generation medium-lift liquid-fueled rocket that first flew in 2020, it was designed from the outset with commercial constellation deployment in mind. Its 2026 flight rate tells the story: three missions by early June, with the June 5 flight coming only 18 days after the previous one. That 18-day turnaround — close to the vehicle's fastest ever — hints at the operational tempo China is trying to normalize for its commercial launch sector. The Long March 8's larger sibling, the 8A, adds roughly two tonnes of additional sun-synchronous capacity, and the family as a whole is meant to give China a purpose-built tool for the medium- and low-orbit deployment missions that constellations demand.

Behind both stands an ecosystem that is maturing fast. The satellites for the June 5 mission came from the Innovation Academy for Microsatellites, one of several Chinese manufacturers now producing Qianfan spacecraft on something approaching an assembly line. The coordination between the China Academy of Launch Vehicle Technology, which builds the rockets, and Shanghai Spacesail Technologies, which operates the constellation, is handled by dedicated commercial liaison organizations — a sign of how far China's commercial space sector has come from its state-monopoly roots.

## The race for the orbital internet

Qianfan does not exist in a vacuum. Its great rival, SpaceX's Starlink, had by early June 2026 launched more than 12,100 satellites in total, with over 10,500 still in orbit and working — an installed base roughly fifty times Qianfan's. Starlink's head start, built on the back of the reusable Falcon 9's relentless cadence, remains the defining fact of the broadband-from-space market.

But Qianfan's backers are not trying to beat Starlink satellite-for-satellite in 2026. They are trying to build a credible alternative — particularly for markets where a Chinese-operated network has political or commercial advantages. The company's named target markets in Brazil, Malaysia, Kazakhstan and Türkiye, plus aviation connectivity, sketch a strategy focused on the Global South and on mobility customers, rather than a head-on assault on Starlink's strongholds in North America and Europe. Domestically, Qianfan is positioned as China's commercial answer to the satellite internet question, complementing the state-run Guowang constellation, which is itself deploying thousands of satellites toward a planned 13,000-spacecraft network.

The economics are shifting, too. Qianfan satellites are reported to cost under 10 million yuan each — roughly $1.4 million — a figure that, if accurate, makes them among the cheapest broadband satellites ever built at scale. That cost discipline, combined with standardized buses and high launch cadence, is the formula every megaconstellation operator is chasing: cheap satellites, cheap launches, relentless tempo.

## What comes next

With 200 satellites launched, Qianfan is roughly one-sixth of the way to its initial 1,296-satellite operational constellation targeted for 2027 — and the program's 2026 goal of 324 launches means the second half of the year will need to be even busier than the first. The June double header showed the machinery can deliver; the question now is whether it can sustain that pace for months on end, and whether the satellites already in orbit can be brought into reliable commercial service.

The June 5 launch also marked a quieter milestone: with the constellation's density now sufficient for basic networking functions like maritime vessel tracking, Qianfan is crossing the threshold from "satellites in orbit" to "network in service." That transition — from deployment to operations — is where megaconstellations prove their worth, and where the real competition with Starlink will be fought.

Two days, two rockets, thirty-six satellites. In the history of the orbital internet, June 4–5, 2026 may be remembered as the weekend China's challenger stopped being a promise and started being a network.

## Three launches in five days

The June 4–5 double header was actually the tail end of an even busier stretch. Just three days earlier, on June 1, China had successfully flown the maiden mission of the Long March 12B — a new-generation reusable rocket developed in only 21 months — from the Dongfeng commercial space innovation pilot zone in the northwest, carrying a small group of Qianfan networking satellites (the constellation's 10th group) into orbit. The rocket was designed from the outset with reusability in mind, carrying landing hardware and a validated re-entry aerodynamic configuration even though no recovery was attempted on the debut flight; a first-stage recovery test is scheduled for a later date.

Three Qianfan launches in five days, from three different spaceports, on three different rocket types. That is the kind of operational breadth that turns a constellation program from a series of missions into a production line — and it is precisely the capability China needs if it intends to average more than 300 Qianfan satellites per year.

## Why polar orbit

All of the June satellites were bound for polar orbit — a choice that reveals the architecture of the first-generation network. Polar sun-synchronous orbits, which pass over or near both poles on every revolution, allow a constellation to provide truly global coverage, including the high-latitude regions that geostationary satellites cannot serve well. For a broadband network that aspires to serve maritime vessels, aircraft and remote communities worldwide, polar coverage is not a luxury; it is the product.

The trade-off is launch geography. Reaching polar orbit from China means flying south from Taiyuan over the country's interior or southeast from Wenchang over the sea — trajectories that demand careful range safety management. That China can now run parallel polar campaigns from both sites, sometimes on consecutive days, speaks to a range infrastructure that has been quietly modernized for exactly this purpose. Wenchang's commercial pads, in particular, were built to support the high flight rates that megaconstellations require, with streamlined processing flows borrowed from commercial launch practice worldwide.

## The money and the politics

Qianfan's ambitions are underwritten by serious capital. Shanghai Spacesail Technologies is backed by the Shanghai municipal government, and the constellation sits at the intersection of two powerful Chinese policy currents: the push to build a world-class commercial space sector, and the strategic imperative not to cede the orbital internet to an American company. The 15,000-satellite approval — granted through China's regulatory system — is both a business plan and a statement of intent.

The international dimension is impossible to ignore. Every Qianfan launch is also a frequency-coordination event: megaconstellations must secure spectrum rights through the International Telecommunication Union, and the order in which constellations deploy affects who gets priority in crowded orbital shells. Launching 36 satellites in two days is, among other things, a way of planting a flag — demonstrating to regulators and rivals alike that the constellation is not a paper design but an operating network growing by the week.

There are skeptics, of course. Astronomers have raised concerns about every large constellation, and Qianfan is no exception: hundreds of sunlit satellites streaking through twilight skies complicate ground-based observations. Space sustainability advocates note that the more satellites go up, the more careful operators must be about collision avoidance and end-of-life disposal — particularly in the increasingly crowded 500-to-1,200-kilometer altitude bands where broadband networks cluster. How Shanghai Spacesail Technologies handles deorbiting and debris mitigation at scale will be watched closely as the constellation grows from hundreds toward thousands.
