China's answer to Starlink just had its busiest weekend ever. On Saturday, July 4, a Long March 6A rose from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center carrying 18 internet satellites. Barely a day later, on the evening of Sunday, July 5, a Long March 8A lifted off from the commercial spaceport in Wenchang, Hainan, carrying 20 more — the largest single group of Qianfan satellites ever launched at once. In 48 hours, the constellation grew by 38 spacecraft.

The Ministry of Industry and Information Technology confirmed the tally the following day: the Qianfan constellation — also known as Thousand Sails, or its original name, G60 Starlink — now has 238 satellites in orbit. The announcement also confirmed domestic frequency coordination and the issuance of satellite radio station and frequency permissions, the bureaucratic machinery that turns launched hardware into a usable network.

Two launches in two days is not, by itself, a record in the age of SpaceX's weekly cadence. But for China it marks something real: the country's second megaconstellation is moving from demonstration to deployment, and the industrial machine behind it is finally hitting its stride.

## Saturday: eighteen from Taiyuan

The weekend began at 17:30 Beijing time on Saturday, July 4 (09:30 UTC), when a Long March 6A blasted off from Launch Complex 9A at Taiyuan in Shanxi Province, heading for a near-polar orbit. The payload was the thirteenth overall group of the Qianfan constellation: eighteen satellites, designated Qianfan Jigui 13A-R, with Ku, Q, and V band communications payloads.

These spacecraft were manufactured by Genesat, a commercial satellite company, under its fifth production contract with the constellation program — a detail that matters because it shows Qianfan has moved beyond one-off prototypes into serial manufacturing by competing suppliers. The satellites rode the rocket's second stage to a deployment altitude of roughly 800 kilometers, from where they will raise themselves into their operational slots.

For the Long March family, the flight was the 655th — one mission after the Haiyang-2E launch two days earlier, a neat illustration of how densely China's manifest is packed this year.

## Sunday: twenty from Hainan, a new record

The second act came from the Wenchang Commercial Space Launch Site on the southern island of Hainan, where a Long March 8A carried the fourteenth Qianfan group: twenty satellites, the biggest batch the program has ever flown on a single rocket. The "one rocket, twenty satellites" approach pushed the in-orbit total from 218 to 238, a new milestone for the constellation.

The Long March 8A is the upgraded variant of the Long March 8, a newer rocket in China's fleet designed with constellation deployment in mind. Its appearance on the Qianfan manifest is significant in its own right: China is widening the stable of rockets feeding its megaconstellations, spreading launches across Taiyuan, the commercial pads at Wenchang, and other vehicles, rather than betting everything on a single launcher. Redundancy in launch is how you keep a 15,000-satellite plan credible.

## Anatomy of a Qianfan satellite

What exactly went up this weekend? The current generation of Qianfan spacecraft is a study in constellation economics. Each satellite weighs roughly 300 kilograms and uses a flat-pack design with a single solar array, so that as many units as possible can be stacked in two parallel columns inside the rocket fairing. Packing density is destiny in the megaconstellation business: every extra satellite per launch divides the launch cost per unit.

For maneuvering, each satellite carries an electric Hall-effect thruster burning krypton, producing about 20 millinewtons of thrust with a specific impulse of 1,385 seconds. Krypton is cheaper than the xenon traditionally used in electric propulsion, and at constellation scale the propellant choice is a cost decision as much as an engineering one. The thrusters handle orbit raising, station-keeping, and eventually the disposal burns that will keep dead satellites from becoming debris.

On the communications side, the satellites carry Ku, Q, and V band payloads, with reported capabilities of around 100 gigabits per second of intersatellite throughput — enough, by the program's own figures, to deliver roughly 20 megabits per second down and 5 megabits per second up to a cell phone-class terminal. Later generations will be larger: second-generation spacecraft are expected to weigh 400 to 500 kilograms and add maritime services, while third-generation units may reach 1,500 kilograms with substantially more capacity.

## The road to 238

To understand where Qianfan stands, rewind two years. The first batch of 18 satellites launched in August 2024 aboard a Long March 6A. The fleet reached 108 satellites by February 2025. Then came a pause: deployment stopped for several months after some satellites suffered thruster or gyroscope failures in orbit. SpaceSail never publicly disclosed the precise technical cause, but corrective measures were applied, and launches resumed in April 2026.

June brought acceleration: a two-satellite test batch on a Long March 12B from Jiuquan on June 1, eighteen more on a Long March 6A on June 4, and another eighteen on a Long March 8 on June 5 — pushing the official total past 200. The July 4-5 doubleheader added 38 more for 238. In other words, Qianfan has more than doubled its fleet in about three months.

The recovery behind those numbers deserves emphasis. Through 2025, the constellation's deployment had stalled for several months after some satellites suffered thruster or gyroscope failures in orbit — the kind of teething problem that can kill a young constellation program if the root cause isn't found. SpaceSail never publicly disclosed the precise technical cause, but engineers applied corrective measures, and launches resumed in April 2026 with a noticeably healthier rhythm. Three launches in June, two more in the first week of July: whatever was wrong appears to have been fixed, and the manufacturing line is now running.

The operator behind all this is Shanghai Spacesail Technologies Co., backed in part by the Shanghai municipal government, which handles development, manufacturing, and commercial operations. The program relies on a domestic industrial chain — satellite platforms, communications payloads, rockets, and ground tracking all sourced within China. That self-reliance is partly strategic: a constellation meant to provide sovereign broadband cannot depend on foreign supply chains.

## The rockets feeding the machine

A megaconstellation is only as fast as its launch supply, and Qianfan's July weekend showcased how China is diversifying that supply. The Long March 6A — a medium-lift vehicle pairing a liquid-fueled core with solid rocket boosters, capable of lifting about 6.5 tonnes to a 500-kilometer sun-synchronous orbit — has been Qianfan's workhorse since the very first batch in August 2024. The Long March 8A, its upgraded sibling, is now joining the campaign from the commercial pads at Wenchang.

But the manifest is widening beyond the Long March family. In June, a Long March 12B debuted from Jiuquan carrying two Qianfan test satellites — a sign that China's newest heavy-medium rockets are being qualified for constellation duty. And the program has started tapping commercial providers: September 2026 saw Qianfan satellites fly on LandSpace's Zhuque-2E and Orienspace's Gravity-1, the latter launching from a converted barge off Haiyang port. That sea launch is a statement of intent — when land pads are full, China will launch its internet from the ocean.

The direct-to-cell angle is developing too. In June 2026, a Zhuque-2E carried the Qianfan direct-to-cell demonstration satellite, testing the ability to connect unmodified mobile phones — the same capability AST SpaceMobile is chasing with its BlueBird satellites. If Thousand Sails can offer phone connectivity without special terminals, its addressable market expands enormously across the developing world, where the company is already courting partners.

## Guowang: the other constellation

Qianfan is not alone in its ambitions. It is one of two Chinese megaconstellations racing each other as well as SpaceX's Starlink — and the sibling rivalry is worth understanding, because the two programs have very different characters.

Guowang — literally "national network," also known as SatNet — is the state-backed heavyweight, run through central government channels with a planned total of around 13,000 satellites. Its deployment has been steady but slower: groups flying from Wenchang on Long March 8A and Long March 12 rockets, including two groups in August 2026. Qianfan overtook Guowang in deployed numbers for the first time in June 2026 and has been trading the lead since — a remarkable outcome for the commercially oriented upstart against the state program.

Both are chasing Starlink, which had more than 10,400 active satellites by mid-2026 and adds dozens every week. But raw counts flatter everyone: what matters is working satellites in their intended slots delivering paying capacity, not just objects with Qianfan or Starlink in their names. Analysts caution that launched totals, operational totals, and revenue-generating capacity are three different numbers, and only the last one decides the business.

The competition has a legal dimension that rarely makes headlines but drives the schedule: international spectrum rules. The International Telecommunication Union requires constellations to hit deployment milestones or risk losing their orbital slots and frequency rights. Qianfan missed a self-imposed target of 324 satellites by July 2026. Guowang faces a steeper test — China would need roughly ten percent of its planned total, about 1,299 satellites, in orbit by September 2029 to hold its spectrum claims, and it is well behind that pace. Every launch weekend like this one is, in part, a down payment on those deadlines.

## Money, markets, and momentum

The deployment surge is happening against a backdrop of serious capital. In August 2026, SpaceSail raised about $1 billion in a funding round that notably locked foreign capital out — the company is keeping its ownership and technology firmly domestic. The raise valued the venture's ambitions plainly: build the world's second-largest satellite internet constellation.

What does the company actually sell today? As of the raise, SpaceSail's only confirmed commercial service in China was maritime connectivity tests conducted in Hong Kong waters with China Mobile Hong Kong as a partner. That is a long way from a consumer broadband product. But the stated roadmap is aggressive: the company aims to offer services in China and abroad — with Brazil, Malaysia, Kazakhstan, and Türkiye named as early markets, plus aviation connectivity — around the fourth quarter of 2026, backed by 324 satellites.

That 324 figure keeps recurring. As of late 2025, the deployment plan called for 324 satellites launched in 2026, another 324 in 2027, then a dramatic ramp: 4,000 per year in 2028 and 2029, and 5,000 in 2030, for roughly 15,000 total. The July milestone of 238 puts the 2026 target within reach if the current pace holds — but the 2028 ramp requires an order-of-magnitude increase in launch capacity that China does not yet have.

The astronomy community is watching with familiar concern. Qianfan satellites are bright — early measurements put them between magnitude 4 and 8, visible to the naked eye near zenith — and at 15,000 strong they would streak through research images that software cannot fully clean. It is the same debate Starlink provoked, now with a second giant constellation joining it. Studies of the first Qianfan batches found that their brightness poses a genuine threat to photographic surveys, and some satellites have even been observed tumbling, which makes their brightness vary unpredictably. Astronomers have asked operators to mitigate reflectivity — darkening surfaces, adjusting orientations — but so far the constellation's growth has outpaced the mitigations.

There is a debris dimension too. At 15,000 satellites with five-to-seven-year lifespans, Qianfan would need to deorbit thousands of dead spacecraft per year at steady state, every year, forever. The krypton thrusters can do it in principle — but only if the satellites stay controllable at end of life, and the 2025 thruster failures are a reminder that not every spacecraft cooperates. The orbital shells around 800-900 kilometers, where Qianfan flies, are already among the most crowded in low Earth orbit.

## The hard math

Here is the part of the story that the launch photos don't show. China currently has the capacity to launch perhaps 500 satellites a year across all its vehicles. Qianfan alone wants 4,000 a year by 2028. Guowang wants similar numbers. Satellites in these orbits last roughly five to seven years, so even a completed constellation needs constant replenishment launches just to stand still. Building one megaconstellation of this size — let alone two — demands far more rockets, launch pads, and satellite factories than exist today.

That is why the details of this July weekend matter more than the headline count. A second launch vehicle (the 8A) joining the Qianfan campaign. A commercial manufacturer (Genesat) delivering its fifth contract batch. A commercial spaceport (Wenchang) handling back-to-back constellation traffic. Twenty satellites on one rocket instead of eighteen. Each is a small step in building the industrial capacity the 2028 ramp will require.

## What comes next

If the plan holds, Qianfan should cross 300 satellites before the end of 2026 and begin limited commercial service in its first markets — Brazil, Malaysia, Kazakhstan, and Türkiye are named as early targets, with aviation connectivity as a flagship use case. Airlines are a natural first customer: a plane full of passengers will pay for bandwidth that a single rural household might not, and the polar orbits Qianfan favors cover the high-latitude routes where geostationary satellites struggle.

The real test comes in 2027 and 2028, when the deployment curve must bend sharply upward — 324 more satellites in 2027, then thousands per year. Hitting those numbers will require the satellite factories to run like assembly lines and the launch pads to turn around like airports. Genesat and other suppliers have been told to accelerate production; the commercial spaceports at Wenchang are adding capacity; new rockets are being qualified. Whether the whole system can scale tenfold in two years is the central question of China's space program for the rest of the decade.

And the competition will not be standing still. Starlink will be larger, Guowang will be pressing its own cadence, and regulators, astronomers, and debris trackers will all be asking harder questions about who gets to fill low Earth orbit and on what terms. The direct-to-cell race adds another front: if Qianfan's demonstration satellite proves out, the constellation could compete for the phone in your pocket, not just the dish on your roof.

For now, though, the scoreboard reads 238, and the weekend of July 4-5 showed that China's constellation machine can run two rockets in two days from two coasts. In the race for the sky, operational rhythm is everything — and Thousand Sails is finally finding its wind.
