Some planets keep their skies the way a desert keeps its water: briefly, and only by accident. TOI-561 b was supposed to be one of those worlds. It orbits so close to its star that a year passes in less than half an Earth day, its day side is hot enough to melt rock, and the radiation beating down on it should have stripped away any atmosphere billions of years ago. Instead, NASA's James Webb Space Telescope has found evidence that this scorched, lava-covered world still has a thick atmosphere, shielded by clouds made not of water but of vaporised rock. It is a result that does not merely add one more exotic planet to the catalogue. It challenges the simple story astronomers have told about which rocky worlds can hold on to air, and which cannot.

The new study, published in The Astronomical Journal and reported on November 1, 2026, comes from an international team led by Samuel Boucher of the Université de Montréal, with Lisa Dang of the University of Waterloo in Canada as project lead. Anjali Piette, an assistant professor of astronomy at the University of Birmingham in the United Kingdom, is also part of the team. Piette put the surprise plainly: "Our discovery challenges current theories. We would expect a heavily irradiated rocky planet like TOI-561 b to have lost its atmosphere billions of years ago." That sentence is worth sitting with, because it captures why a planet nobody will ever stand on matters so much. If the models are wrong here, in the most extreme case imaginable, they may be wrong in subtler ways for the temperate rocky worlds astronomers most want to understand.

TOI-561 b sits about 280 light-years from Earth. It is a little more than twice Earth's mass and about 1.4 times as wide, which makes it a super-Earth in size but nothing like Earth in character. It circles its star in about 10.7 hours, so a full year there passes in less time than a single working shift on Earth. At that distance, the planet is tidally locked: one side always faces the star and the other always faces away, just as the Moon always shows the same face to Earth. The day side is likely covered by an ocean of molten rock, which is why astronomers call TOI-561 b one of the lava planets. The night side never sees its star at all. Between them lies a terminator, a permanent twilight boundary where day turns to night, and, as the new work suggests, where the planet's strange weather may be born.

## A year shorter than a work shift

To understand why TOI-561 b's atmosphere is surprising, it helps to picture the forces working against it. A planet this close to its star lives inside a wind of charged particles and a flood of ultraviolet and X-ray radiation. Over billions of years, that bombardment heats the top of an atmosphere and gives its lightest gases enough energy to escape into space, a process astronomers call atmospheric escape. Small rocky planets are especially vulnerable, because their gravity is weaker than that of a gas giant and they have less atmosphere to lose in the first place. Add a molten surface, which sounds like it should boil away anything above it, and the expectation was clear: TOI-561 b should be bare rock, its sky long since blown away.

The planet's star makes the puzzle sharper, not softer. It is at least 10 billion years old, more than twice the age of the Sun, and poorer in heavy elements. That age matters because it sets a clock. Whatever atmosphere TOI-561 b has today cannot be a leftover wisp that simply has not had time to escape yet. It has survived, or been replenished, for longer than Earth has existed as a planet with continents and oceans. TOI-561 b is also lighter for its size than most planets in such tight orbits, which tend to match Earth-like rock. That lower density is itself a clue: a planet with a substantial atmosphere, or a different interior composition, would weigh less for its volume than a solid ball of Earth-like rock. On its own, density cannot prove an atmosphere exists. Combined with Webb's temperature measurements, however, it points in the same direction.

There is a useful comparison close to home, and another one among the lava planets themselves. Venus, the brightest planet in our night sky, reflects most of the sunlight that hits it because of its thick cloud deck, and it keeps a crushing carbon dioxide atmosphere despite sitting closer to the Sun than Earth does. Io, one of Jupiter's moons, is the most volcanically active body in the solar system, constantly resupplying a thin atmosphere of sulfur dioxide from its eruptions. TOI-561 b, as the new study frames it, may sit somewhere between those two analogies: a world with a thick, reflective sky, fed from below by a planet-wide reservoir of molten rock. Another lava planet, <a href="/blog/webb-hd-80606b-roasted-exoplanet/">55 Cancri e</a>, is also thought to have a substantial atmosphere, probably carrying carbon dioxide or carbon monoxide, and earlier work by Dang, Piette and their colleagues pointed to gases from TOI-561 b's interior refilling its air. The family of lava worlds is small, but it is starting to tell a consistent story: molten rock and thin air are not the contradiction they once seemed.

## The 37-hour stare

The first hint that TOI-561 b had air at all came not from this study but from an earlier one. A 2025 study led by Johanna Teske measured heat from the planet's day side each time it passed behind its star, an event astronomers call a secondary eclipse. When the planet slips behind its star, its own light briefly disappears, and the size of that dip tells astronomers how hot the day side is. Teske's team found the day side too cool for bare rock, and too cool even for an atmosphere made only of rock vapour. Something was carrying heat away, or reflecting sunlight before it could be absorbed, or both. It was a hint, not a proof, and it left the obvious question open: what exactly was doing the cooling?

The new study answers that question with patience. The team used Webb to watch TOI-561 b for a single 37-hour stretch in one observing program. During that time, the planet passed behind its star four times, completing nearly four full orbits while Webb kept watching. That long, unbroken watch mattered for two reasons. First, it let the team analyse the whole orbit, night side included, rather than just the day side glimpsed during eclipses. Second, it let them separate the planet's signal from slow flickering on the star's surface, where hot gas rises and sinks. Stars are not steady light bulbs; their surfaces churn, and that churning can mimic or mask a planet's faint signal. Watching for nearly four orbits gave the team enough repeated patterns to tell the planet apart from its star, a distinction that a single snapshot could never have made with confidence.

That method is one of Webb's quiet strengths, and it is worth understanding because it explains why this result arrived now rather than years ago. Webb does not take a photograph of TOI-561 b. At 280 light-years, the planet is an unresolved point of light drowned in its star's glare. What Webb measures, with exquisite precision, is how the combined light of star and planet changes over time: dimming slightly when the planet passes in front of its star, dipping again when it passes behind, and rising and falling in between as different faces of the planet rotate into view. From that phase curve, the rise and fall of brightness across a full orbit, astronomers can reconstruct temperatures on the day side and the night side, and infer how much heat is being moved around the planet and how much sunlight is being reflected away. It is forensic astronomy, reconstructing a world from the flicker of a distant light, and the 37-hour stare gave the team a flicker long enough to read.

## The day side ran cool

The numbers from that stare are what turn a hint into a case. The researchers calculated how hot the day side would get if the planet were dark, bare rock with no air to carry heat around. The answer was about 5,300 degrees Fahrenheit (3,200 kelvin). Webb measured about 3,200 degrees Fahrenheit (2,050 kelvin) instead. More than 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit of expected heat was missing. That is not a rounding error or a marginal detection. It is a gap large enough to demand a physical explanation, and there are only two places the missing heat could have gone: it could have been carried around to the night side by moving air, or it could have been reflected away before the planet ever absorbed it.

The night side measurement narrows the answer. It came in at about 1,500 degrees Fahrenheit (1,100 kelvin). That is warm for a side that never sees its star, so some heat is definitely reaching it, carried around the planet by something that moves. But it is not warm enough to explain the whole gap. Heat transport alone cannot account for a day side more than 2,000 degrees cooler than bare rock would be. Reflection has to cover the rest. The team estimates the planet reflects about 74 percent of the starlight that hits it, close to the share reflected by Venus. For comparison, the Moon reflects only about 12 percent of sunlight, and Earth's average reflectivity is roughly 30 percent. A world reflecting nearly three-quarters of its star's light is, by planetary standards, dazzlingly bright.

Laboratory tests rule out the simplest explanation for that brightness. Molten-rock surfaces reflect very little light, so a bare lava ocean cannot be doing the reflecting. Something above the surface has to be responsible. That logic is the hinge of the whole study: the day side is too cool, the night side is too warm to explain it by transport alone, and the surface itself is too dark to explain it by reflection. Only an atmosphere, carrying heat and holding something reflective aloft, fits all three measurements at once. It is the kind of argument that does not rest on a single spectacular image but on the way several independent numbers refuse to fit together any other way, and that makes it harder to dismiss than a single anomalous data point would be.

The result also reframes what "too cool" means for a lava planet. A day side at 3,200 degrees Fahrenheit is still far beyond anything habitable, still hot enough to keep rock molten, still a world where no ocean of water could survive and no familiar weather could exist. The point is not that TOI-561 b is temperate. The point is that it is cooler than physics said it should be, by a margin too large to ignore, and that margin is the signature of an atmosphere doing real work: moving heat, reflecting light, and changing the planet's energy balance in ways a dead rock cannot.

## Sunscreen made of rock

If an atmosphere is present, the next question is what is in it, and here the team turned to computer simulations of the planet's climate. They compared Webb's readings with models of different atmospheres, different compositions, and different cloud decks, and asked which combinations could reproduce the measured day and night temperatures and the high reflectivity. Heat moving around the planet could not explain the readings alone. The best matches came from a thick atmosphere, with surface air pressure between one and ten times Earth's, holding something that reflected a lot of light. That pressure range matters: it is not a wisp of vapour clinging to the surface, but an atmosphere comparable in heft to Earth's, or several times thicker, on a world where nobody expected any atmosphere at all.

The leading candidates for the reflective material are mineral clouds, made from rock that evaporates off the molten surface. In the simulations, clouds of silicon dioxide, the mineral in quartz and most sand, and magnesium silicate could form on the day side. They would gather near the edge where day turns to night, where the air cools enough for those minerals to condense out of vapour and into particles. It is a weather cycle familiar in outline and alien in substance: on Earth, water evaporates from warm oceans and condenses into clouds where the air cools; on TOI-561 b, rock evaporates from a molten ocean and condenses into clouds where the air cools. The clouds would work as a sunscreen, sending starlight back out before it can heat the planet, which is exactly the reflective shield the temperature measurements demand.

This is where the terminator, that permanent twilight boundary, becomes more than a curiosity. It is the planet's cold trap, the place where vapour becomes cloud, and the simulations suggest the clouds gather there preferentially. A planet tidally locked to its star has no seasons and no day-night cycle in the Earthly sense, but it has a permanent geography of heat: a scorching day side, a cooler night side, and a ring between them where the physics of condensation can do its work. If the mineral-cloud picture is right, TOI-561 b's sky is not uniform. It is structured by that geography, thickest and most reflective where the temperature crosses the threshold for rock to condense, thinner elsewhere. Future observations that can map brightness across the planet's face in more detail may be able to test that structure directly, turning a global average reflectivity into a weather map of a lava world.

It is also worth noting what the mineral-cloud idea does not claim. It does not claim that astronomers have directly photographed clouds of quartz and silicate above a lava ocean. No telescope can do that at 280 light-years. It claims that, among the atmospheres the team simulated, thick atmospheres with reflective mineral clouds are the ones that reproduce Webb's measurements, while bare rock and thin rock-vapour skies do not. That is a strong claim, but it is a model-dependent one, and the team is careful about its limits, as the caveats later in this article show. The distinction matters for how readers should hold this result: as the best current explanation for a robust set of measurements, not as a snapshot of an alien sky.

## Venus or a hotter Io?

The gas that makes up most of the atmosphere is still unknown, and the team is candid about that gap. It could be a thick layer of carbon dioxide, like a much hotter version of Venus. Or it could be rich in sulfur dioxide, a gas released by the volcanoes on Io, one of Jupiter's moons. Those two options are not just chemical trivia; they imply different histories and different futures for the planet. A carbon dioxide atmosphere points toward a Venus-like story of a thick, stable sky dominated by a gas that is hard to destroy. A sulfur dioxide atmosphere points toward a volcanic story, in which the sky is constantly fed by eruptions and outgassing from the interior, and would fade if that supply ever stopped. Webb's current measurements, taken in a narrow band of infrared light, cannot yet tell the two apart, because the gases absorb light differently at wavelengths the team has not yet observed in full-orbit detail.

That uncertainty is connected to the deeper puzzle Piette identified: how the atmosphere is still there at all. "This raises major questions such as whether the planet started with an unusually large supply of gases or if the ocean of molten rock covering its surface acts as a planet-wide volcano that is constantly feeding its atmosphere," she said. Those are the two live hypotheses, and they lead to different predictions. If TOI-561 b started with an unusually large reservoir of volatile elements, its atmosphere is a survivor, slowly leaking away over billions of years but starting from so much gas that some remains even now. If the lava ocean is constantly outgassing, the atmosphere is not a survivor at all but a renewable resource, replenished as fast as it escapes, and it could persist indefinitely as long as the interior stays hot. Earlier work by Dang, Piette and their colleagues already pointed toward gases from the planet's interior refilling its air, which leans toward the second picture, but the new study does not settle the question. It sharpens it, and gives future observations a specific test: an atmosphere fed from below should show chemical signatures of fresh interior gas, while a primordial survivor should look more thoroughly processed by billions of years of radiation.

The comparison with other extreme worlds helps place TOI-561 b in context. <a href="/blog/webb-wasp-17b-true-shape-egg-planet/">WASP-17b</a>, a puffed-up gas giant whose shape Webb helped reveal, shows how the telescope can measure the structure of an atmosphere far beyond the solar system. <a href="/blog/beta-pictoris-b-first-exoplanet-magnetic-field-detection/">Beta Pictoris b</a>, the first exoplanet with a detected magnetic field, shows how a planet's interior and its environment interact in ways that shape what survives above the surface. TOI-561 b belongs in that growing gallery of worlds where the atmosphere is not a passive blanket but an active system, coupled to the interior below and the star above. What makes it special is the extremity: it is a rocky world, not a gas giant, holding air where the textbooks said air should be impossible, and doing so for more than 10 billion years of stellar punishment.

There is also a broader lesson about how astronomers read absence. For years, the working assumption for ultra-short-period rocky planets was that observations would find bare rock, and a bare-rock result would confirm the models. TOI-561 b breaks that expectation in the most useful way a single planet can: it shows that the absence of an atmosphere is a hypothesis to be tested, not a default to be assumed. That shift matters far beyond lava worlds, because the same atmospheric-escape physics governs the temperate rocky planets around small red dwarf stars, where astronomers hope to search for habitability. If a lava planet can keep a thick sky, then the survival of atmospheres on less extreme rocky worlds may be more likely than the pessimistic models suggested, and the search for air on those worlds becomes more promising, not less.

## What Webb will look at next

The team is equally clear about what this study does not yet prove, and those caveats deserve as much attention as the headline. The temperatures are best estimates. Webb watched the planet in a narrow band of infrared light, and that band misses the wavelengths where the planet gives off the most heat. That means the day and night temperatures are inferred from a slice of the spectrum, not measured across the full range where the planet radiates, and a different slice could shift the numbers. The two detectors inside Webb's instrument also did not agree perfectly once their data were compared with the climate simulations. That difference could come from the instrument, from the star, or from clouds or gases that look different at different wavelengths, and the team cannot yet say which. These are not footnotes; they are the reasons the authors present a thick, cloudy atmosphere as the best match rather than a certainty.

The path forward is already mapped, and it runs through longer wavelengths. Webb also recorded the planet passing in front of its star during the same observation, and those transit data will get a paper of their own, which should help show how thick the atmosphere is and whether clouds are really there. When a planet transits, starlight filters through the thin upper edge of its atmosphere, and the gases there leave fingerprints in the spectrum. A transit spectrum is therefore a more direct probe of composition than a phase curve alone, and it may be able to distinguish a carbon dioxide sky from a sulfur dioxide one. Dang framed the next question directly: "The next big question we will seek to answer is whether TOI-561 b's atmosphere resembles Venus or an extreme version of Io. Observing the planet at longer wavelengths with JWST could give us insights and help explain how a planet this hot has held on to an atmosphere for billions of years."

Carbon dioxide absorbs light at one of those longer wavelengths, so a full orbit watched there with Webb's mid-infrared instrument could show whether the sky over TOI-561 b looks more like Venus. That observation is not a formality. If the mid-infrared phase curve shows the deep carbon dioxide feature the Venus hypothesis predicts, the case for a thick carbon dioxide atmosphere strengthens considerably. If it does not, the sulfur dioxide picture, or something more exotic, gains ground. Either way, the next observation has the power to discriminate between the hypotheses this study leaves open, which is exactly what a good result should do: answer one question and pose a sharper one.

## Why a lava world matters for everyone else

It is tempting to file TOI-561 b under curiosities: a hell world, a record-breaker, a planet too hot to matter. That would miss why astronomers care. Rocky planets are the hardest atmospheres to study and the most important to understand, because every question about habitability eventually comes down to air. Does the planet have an atmosphere? How thick is it? What is it made of? How long can it last? For gas giants, those questions are easier, because their atmospheres are huge and their signals are loud. For rocky planets, the signals are faint, the atmospheres (if they exist) are thin by comparison, and the history of escape, outgassing, and stellar violence is written in subtleties of temperature and reflectivity. TOI-561 b is valuable precisely because its signal is not subtle. A 2,000-degree gap between expectation and measurement is loud enough to hear clearly, and the physics that explains it, heat transport plus reflection by mineral clouds, is physics that applies to rocky planets everywhere.

There is a second reason this world matters, and it is about time. TOI-561 b's star is ancient, and the planet has endured that star's radiation for billions of years longer than Earth has existed. If atmospheres can survive, or renew themselves, on that timescale under those conditions, then the window for rocky planets to keep their skies is wider than the early models allowed. That does not make any particular temperate planet habitable; habitability needs liquid water, a stable climate, and a host of other conditions that TOI-561 b spectacularly fails. But it does mean that when astronomers point Webb at a temperate rocky world and find no atmosphere, they will need to work harder to prove the negative, and when they find one, they will have a better framework for understanding how it got there and how long it might last. TOI-561 b is an extreme laboratory, and extreme laboratories are where theories get tested hardest.

The study is also a reminder of how exoplanet science actually progresses. It did not arrive as a single eureka image but as a chain: an earlier hint from Teske's team that the day side was too cool, a long and patient Webb stare that covered nearly four orbits, a careful separation of planet from star, a set of temperature measurements that refused to fit bare rock, and a suite of climate simulations that found thick, cloudy atmospheres to be the best match. Each link in that chain depended on the one before it, and the result is stronger for it. Science at this distance is rarely about seeing a world clearly. It is about listening carefully enough, for long enough, that a flicker of light becomes a temperature, a temperature becomes an atmosphere, and an atmosphere becomes a story about how planets live and die under their stars.

## A sky that should not be there

TOI-561 b will never be a destination, never be a home, never be anything but a point of light that flickers as it races around its star every 10.7 hours. Its day side will stay molten, its night side will stay dark, and its clouds, if they are what the simulations suggest, will keep condensing out of vaporised rock at the boundary between fire and night. And yet this uninhabitable world has done something remarkable: it has survived. Against radiation that should have stripped it bare, against billions of years that should have finished the job, against models that predicted a dead rock, it still has a sky, thick enough to move heat, bright enough to reflect three-quarters of its star's light, and strange enough to be made of the very rock it covers.

That is the kicker the study leaves behind, and it is larger than one planet. The universe, as Piette's team found, does not always follow the neat models drawn for it. Sometimes a lava world keeps its atmosphere because its ocean boils rock into clouds that shield it. Sometimes the most hostile place imaginable turns out to be the place where a theory breaks and a better one begins. Webb will look again at longer wavelengths, and the transit data will have their say, and the Venus-or-Io question will, with luck, be answered. Whatever the answer turns out to be, TOI-561 b has already changed the question astronomers ask about rocky worlds. It is no longer whether a scorched planet can have a sky. It is how, against all expectations, this one kept its own.
