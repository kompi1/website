Somewhere on the outskirts of the Andromeda galaxy, a star slightly less massive than the Sun spiraled into a bloated supergiant and never came back out. Two stars went in. One came out. And for the first time in the history of astronomy, scientists watched the whole thing happen in real time — from the first suspicious brightening, through a violent eruption, to the infrared glow of the wreckage — and proved it with the most powerful telescope ever flown.

The merged star has a nickname: Bao. Its formal designation is AT 2025abao, and it sits about 2.5 million light-years from Earth in the nearest large spiral galaxy to our own Milky Way. On Wednesday, November 18, 2026, a team led by Viraj Karambelkar, a NASA Hubble Fellow at Columbia University, published the full reconstruction of the merger in the journal *Nature*, and NASA announced that observations from the James Webb Space Telescope had supplied the final evidence that tied the story together. It is the first merger of its kind ever identified: a sun-like star consumed by a supergiant, caught on camera, in effect, from beginning to end.

Stellar mergers are among the most consequential events in the universe, and also among the hardest to witness. They forge the tight binary systems that later become neutron stars, black holes, supernovae, millisecond pulsars, and even sources of gravitational waves. Yet they are, in Karambelkar's words, "very, very difficult to observe in real time because they are hard to predict." A merger announces itself only when it is already underway, and by the time telescopes slew to look, the crucial evidence is usually gone. Bao is the exception that proves how much has been missing. Astronomers noticed something odd in 2023, kept watching for two years, and when the system erupted in 2025, Webb was on it 47 days after the eruption peaked — the earliest follow-up ever made of an event like this.

## A star ate its neighbor

Before the merger, Bao was a binary pair that had spent millennia placidly orbiting one another. The primary star was, by any measure, an absolute beast. According to the team's modeling, the star nicknamed Big Bao was about seven times as massive as the Sun, had swollen to roughly 350 times the Sun's radius, and shone about 18,000 times as brightly. It was a puffy supergiant — an aging star that had expanded enormously as it exhausted the fuel in its core, its outer layers diffuse and cool, its grip on its own material loosening.

Its companion was a different creature entirely. Little Bao, as the team calls it, was likely about 0.7 times the mass of the Sun, with a slightly smaller radius than our star and far fainter. In the glare of Big Bao, it was effectively invisible. As the MIT Kavli Institute's account of the discovery put it, Big Bao was 18,000 times brighter than its companion, which made it impossible to spot Little Bao in any other way. The smaller star's existence could only be inferred from what it did to its giant partner — and, in the end, from what its giant partner did to it.

The trouble began when Big Bao's expansion finally carried its outer layers out to meet its companion's orbit. The smaller star brushed against the giant's surface, and the encounter began to strip gas from the supergiant. That gas flowed away into surrounding space, and the companion's orbit began to decay. This is the opening act of a process astronomers call common envelope evolution, one of the most important and least understood mechanisms in stellar astrophysics. The gas between the stars becomes a shared outer layer — a common envelope — and the companion plunges inward, spiraling through the envelope as drag robs it of orbital energy.

What stunned the team was the speed. Once the smaller star came into contact with the surface of the giant, their "unification" happened after only seven orbits — about seven years. Other known binary star mergers, featuring two smaller stars, occurred after thousands of orbits. Bao's final plunge was, by stellar standards, a sprint. "We thought an outburst from such an extended star would take years," said co-author Nadia Blagorodnova, an associate professor at the University of Barcelona. "One day, its brightness started rising and within a week, it peaked."

The violence of the finale is hard to overstate. The team's simulations showed that Little Bao was dragged into Big Bao's core, and the encounter was, in the words of co-author Kishalay De, an assistant professor at Columbia University, "almost like a bomb exploding inside a living star." The merged star that remains — now simply called Bao — may even have been "rejuvenated" by the collision, its outer layers churned and its future evolution reset by the infusion of fresh material and angular momentum. Only future observations will reveal how its new heft settles.

## The seven-year stakeout

The reason Bao could be reconstructed at all is that astronomers were already watching. The system first caught the team's attention in February 2023, when they saw an unusual variable star in data from NASA's retired NEOWISE mission, the Near-Earth Object Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer. Before 2018, the object had shown a consistent luminosity. Then it began to brighten, slowly and steadily, in a way that did not match any familiar pattern.

Karambelkar, then at Caltech, and De, then at MIT, noticed the brightening independently — Karambelkar in data from WINTER, the Wide-field Infrared Transient Explorer at Caltech's Palomar Observatory, and De in the NEOWISE data. They kept watching. Observations from WINTER in September 2024 showed that Bao was not only beginning to brighten, but also spewing dust. In May 2025, the team published their first paper on the system, proposing that the star was a red giant — a star that had puffed up, often to hundreds of times its original size, and become extremely diffuse and cool. The paper appeared in *The Astrophysical Journal* just before the eruption, a piece of timing that now looks almost prophetic.

Then, in July 2025, NASA's SPHEREx telescope — the Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer, which had launched earlier that year — serendipitously caught Bao fading rapidly in visible light and brightening in infrared light during a routine survey. It was another indicator of unusual activity: dust was thickening around the star, blocking visible light and re-radiating the energy in the infrared.

The eruption itself arrived in October 2025. On October 17, the Zwicky Transient Facility detected the stars rapidly brightening and radically heating up. An amateur astronomer, Koichi Itagaki, spotted the bright flash in the Andromeda galaxy the same month while observing the galaxy. The system had become a luminous red nova — the class of transient that marks a stellar merger — formally catalogued as AT 2025abao. Within a week of the brightness starting to rise, the eruption peaked.

## Webb's 47-day sprint

What happened next is what makes Bao a first. In November 2025, the team received fast-turn observations from NASA's now-retired Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory and from NOIRLab's Gemini North telescope in Hawaii. Then, on December 24, 2025 — Christmas Eve — Webb observed Bao under a just-in-time Director's Discretionary Time program. That was just 47 days after the eruption peaked, the earliest-ever follow-up of an event like this.

Webb's mid-infrared observations were the decisive piece. They detailed the dust shed by the more massive primary star over centuries, and when the team matched that dust signature against their ongoing simulations, they found the model that fit. The simulations, led by Karel Temmink, a Ph.D. student at Radboud University in Nijmegen in the Netherlands, had been updated continuously as new data arrived. Webb's data let the team measure how much mass was ejected during the process and how efficient common envelope evolution actually is — a number astronomers had previously only been able to estimate from systems that merged long ago.

"Webb observed Bao only 47 days after the stars' eruption peaked, the earliest-ever follow-up of an event like this," Karambelkar said. "The telescope's data helped us characterize what the system looked like before, during and immediately after the merger, which is absolutely mind-blowing."

The reconstruction also answered a question that had lingered in the modeling: whether Little Bao might escape. The model showed there might be leftover energy for the smaller star to "flee" the scene. Further analysis determined otherwise. Dragged into the core of its giant companion, Little Bao was demolished, and the two stars became one. The team's analysis, as NASA's announcement put it, offers a new approach to reconstructing such events — conducting the analysis and making the observations in real time, rather than reverse-engineering a merger that may have happened hundreds or even millions of years ago.

The contrast between the two stars is the heart of the story. Big Bao outshone its companion by a factor of roughly 18,000, which is why Little Bao could never have been found by direct observation. Its presence was written only in the brightening, the dust, and the eruption — and in the simulations that Webb's dust measurements finally anchored. The seven-orbit plunge is equally striking: other known binary mergers, involving two smaller stars, took thousands of orbits to complete. Bao's giant primary, with its enormous, diffuse envelope, swallowed its companion with a haste that no one had predicted.

## Why common envelope evolution matters

Bao is not just a curiosity. Common envelope evolution is one of the main ways the universe builds the exotic objects that dominate modern astrophysics — and it is also one of the least understood. When two stars share an envelope, two outcomes are possible. In Bao's case, the companion was consumed and the stars merged. But in other systems, both stars survive the process, shedding a lot of mass along the way but remaining independent in tight orbits. Over time, such surviving pairs can transform into neutron stars and black holes, explode as supernovae, produce ripples in space-time known as gravitational waves, and create millisecond pulsars.

The problem has always been the efficiency of the process: how much of the orbital energy goes into ejecting the shared envelope, and how much mass is actually thrown off? Astronomers describe this with a parameter they call the common envelope efficiency, and for decades its value has been one of the biggest uncertainties in binary star evolution. Estimates came from working backward — looking at the tight binaries left behind and guessing what must have happened to produce them. Bao flips that approach around. Because the team watched the merger as it happened and measured the ejected dust directly with Webb, they could measure the efficiency rather than infer it. That measurement will feed into models of how binary black holes form, how often they merge, and how many gravitational-wave events observatories like LIGO should expect to hear.

The connection to gravitational waves is not abstract. The black hole binaries whose mergers LIGO and Virgo detect almost certainly passed through a common envelope phase at some point in their history. If the envelope is ejected efficiently, the two stars end up close together and can merge within the age of the universe; if it is not, they drift apart and never collide. A single well-measured event like Bao cannot settle the efficiency question on its own, but it is the first direct calibration point — a number measured from a live event rather than reconstructed from a fossil.

## Not the first merger — but the first proof

Astronomers have seen stellar mergers before, and Bao joins a short but distinguished list. The most famous is V838 Monocerotis, which erupted in 2002 and produced one of the most photographed light echoes in astronomy as its flash illuminated the dust around it. V1309 Scorpii, whose merger was established in a 2011 paper, was a contact binary — two stars already touching — that spiraled together and erupted. And Andromeda itself has hosted one before: M31-LRN-2015, a luminous red nova in the same galaxy, was the subject of detailed study, and the *Nature* paper's references note that AT 2025abao is the fourth luminous red nova identified in M31.

What sets Bao apart is not the merger itself but the proof. Earlier mergers were identified after the fact, reconstructed from the wreckage. Bao was watched from the first suspicious brightening through the eruption and into the aftermath, with Webb's infrared eyes arriving while the dust was still fresh. It is the first time astronomers have been able to characterize a system like this before, during, and immediately after the merger — and the first time the common envelope efficiency has been measured from a live event rather than reverse-engineered.

There is also a difference of scale. V1309 Scorpii involved two relatively small stars. Bao involved a genuine supergiant, a star 350 times the Sun's radius, swallowing a sun-like companion. The physics of a common envelope around a supergiant — with its vast, tenuous outer layers — is different from the physics around a smaller giant, and the fact that the plunge took only seven orbits rather than thousands suggests that the most bloated stars may consume their companions far faster than models had assumed. "We thought an outburst from such an extended star would take years," Blagorodnova said. It took a week to peak.

The comparison also sharpens what remains unknown. The merged star, Bao, is still settling. Its outer layers are still expanding, its dust still cooling, and its internal structure is still reorganizing after the injection of a whole star's worth of mass and angular momentum. Future observations will be needed to determine how the new star looks today and how its "heft" settles — and, as some accounts of the discovery note, to confirm conclusively the long-term picture of the merged object. For now, the reconstruction stands on Webb's dust measurements, the multi-wavelength light curve, and simulations that matched them. It is the most complete picture of a stellar merger ever assembled, and it is also, honestly, a beginning.

## What happens to Bao now?

The merged star is, in the team's word, potentially "rejuvenated." A merger can mix fresh hydrogen into a star's core, spin it up, and reset its evolutionary clock — the merged star can look younger than its true age, burning hotter and brighter than a single star of the same mass would. Whether Bao follows that path depends on how thoroughly Little Bao's material was mixed into Big Bao's interior, and on how much mass the eruption threw away. The answers will come from watching Bao over the coming years as its dust shell expands and thins, and as the star itself settles into whatever its new normal will be.

There is a broader question, too: how common is this? Bao was found because astronomers happened to be looking at the right patch of Andromeda with the right instruments at the right time — and because they had the patience to watch a slowly brightening star for two years before it did anything dramatic. Most mergers will not be so obliging. They will erupt without warning, in galaxies too distant for detailed follow-up, and fade before telescopes can respond. That is why Karambelkar's team is already looking ahead to the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, which NASA launched recently and which is about to begin vast, publicly available surveys of the sky. Its Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey will yield a huge number of new transient events that could benefit from similar analyses. "There's going to be a large influx of similar discoveries just ahead," Karambelkar said.

Roman's wide field of view is the key. Where Webb is a scalpel — exquisitely sensitive, but pointed at one target at a time — Roman is a net, sweeping up the whole sky and flagging anything that changes. The combination is what Bao's story really demonstrates: a survey telescope notices something odd, ground-based telescopes and smaller space missions track it, and then Webb is called in for the decisive observation at exactly the right moment. That pipeline, from discovery to characterization, is now a proven method rather than a hope. Readers can follow Roman's progress in our earlier report on the <a href="/blog/roman-telescope-optics-alignment-november-2026/">Roman Space Telescope optics alignment</a>, and see what Webb is capable of when it turns its instruments on the distant universe in our coverage of the <a href="/blog/jwst-3d-dark-matter-map-cosmos-web-november-2026/">JWST 3D dark matter map from the COSMOS-Web survey</a>.

The same logic applies closer to home. The story of a giant star swallowing a companion is, in miniature, the story of what will happen to stars across the universe — including, in a distant echo, the fate of binary systems whose remnants will one day merge as black holes and shake space-time itself. To understand those cataclysms, astronomers first have to understand the quiet, patient, and then suddenly violent process by which two stars become one. Bao is the first time they have seen it clearly.

## The long view

There is something quietly remarkable about the way Bao was found. No one set out to watch a merger. The team noticed a star getting brighter in survey data, and they kept watching — for months, then years — on the suspicion that something unusual was happening. They published a paper proposing it was a red giant, and then the star erupted, almost as if on cue. The eruption was caught by a professional survey telescope and by an amateur astronomer on the other side of the world on the same night. A retired infrared survey mission, a brand-new space telescope, a doomed Swift observatory in its final months, and the most powerful telescope ever built all contributed pieces of the same puzzle, and the pieces fit.

That is how modern astronomy increasingly works: not as a single heroic observation, but as a relay. Survey telescopes notice. Small telescopes track. Great observatories confirm. And when the relay works — when a star brightens in 2023, erupts in 2025, and is observed by Webb 47 days later — the result is not just a discovery but a reconstruction, a story with a beginning, a middle, and an end.

Bao's end is still being written. The merged star is out there now, 2.5 million light-years away, settling into its new life as a single star with the mass of two. Its dust shell is expanding into the space between the stars. Its light is still traveling toward us, carrying news of an event that happened when our ancestors were still learning to farm — and of a merger that, for the first time, humanity watched happen. The next Bao may already be brightening somewhere in a survey image, unnoticed, waiting for someone patient enough to keep watching.
