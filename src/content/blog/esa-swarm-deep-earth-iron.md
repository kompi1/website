You cannot go there. No drill will ever reach it, no camera will ever see it, no hand will ever touch it. Nearly two thousand kilometers beneath your feet, rock flows like cold honey under pressures that would crush a submarine into foil and temperatures hot enough to melt steel twice over. Yet this week, scientists published the first map of the iron hidden inside that unreachable world — and they drew it using satellites flying hundreds of kilometers above the planet's surface.

The research, published in the journal Science Advances on July 16, 2026, was led by Kui Han at the University of Bayreuth in Germany, with geomagnetic modeling contributions from Alexander Grayver of the University of Cologne. It brings together two scientific fields that have been developing in parallel for decades: mineral physics, the laboratory science of how rocks behave under extreme conditions, and geomagnetism, the study of Earth's magnetic field from space and the ground.

The result is a picture of iron distribution in the lower mantle at depths of around 825 and 1,225 kilometers — a region no human instrument has ever sampled directly, now rendered in maps built from orbiting magnetometers and laboratory furnaces. It resolves an ambiguity that has frustrated geophysicists for decades, and it sheds new light on the deep processes that have shaped our planet over billions of years.

## The Problem With Looking Down

Earth's interior is layered like a vast, violent onion. Beneath the thin crust we live on lies the mantle, a rocky layer extending from about 30 kilometers down to roughly 2,900 kilometers. The lower mantle — the region from around 660 to 2,900 kilometers — is dominated by a single mineral: bridgmanite, a dense magnesium iron silicate that makes up roughly 80 percent of the lower mantle by volume.

The concentration of iron locked inside bridgmanite's crystal structure varies from place to place, and that variation matters enormously. Iron influences the density of minerals, and density differences drive mantle convection — the slow, inexorable churning of rock that moves tectonic plates, builds mountains, and recycles the planet's crust. Iron has also played a role in the long-term habitability of the planet, shaping how Earth differentiated in its youth and how heat has flowed out of the interior ever since.

The trouble is that nobody can sample the lower mantle directly. The deepest hole ever drilled penetrated barely 12 kilometers — a scratch on the crust. So geophysicists have traditionally relied on seismic tomography: mapping how earthquake waves speed up or slow down as they travel through the planet's interior, and inferring composition from those velocity patterns.

It works, up to a point. The problem is a fundamental ambiguity. Both iron content and temperature alter seismic wave speeds, and they do so in ways that overlap. A region that slows seismic waves could be hotter, or richer in iron, or both — and from seismic data alone, there is no clean way to tell the difference. That ambiguity has limited researchers for decades, leaving the iron architecture of the lower mantle essentially invisible.

## The Laboratory Key

Kui Han's team at the University of Bayreuth attacked the problem from the laboratory side. They synthesized samples of bridgmanite — the aluminum-bearing variety found in the real mantle — with varying iron contents, then subjected them to the crushing pressures and searing temperatures of the lower mantle: pressures up to 27 gigapascals, roughly 270,000 times atmospheric pressure, and temperatures up to 2,000 kelvin.

Then they measured something seismologists rarely think about: electrical conductivity.

The result was striking. Bridgmanite's electrical conductivity proved to be extraordinarily sensitive to iron content, following a steep power-law relationship — add a little iron, and the conductivity jumps dramatically. But it showed comparatively little sensitivity to temperature. Where temperature and iron produce nearly indistinguishable seismic signals, they produce very different electrical signals. Conductivity, in other words, cuts cleanly through the ambiguity that has haunted seismic tomography for decades.

As Alexander Grayver put it, the conductivity's sensitivity to iron offers an almost perfect proxy for studying iron content in the mantle — and iron content plays a big role in Earth's long-term evolution. (That is the paraphrase of his central point: conductivity is so sensitive to iron that it becomes a nearly ideal stand-in for the iron that no drill can reach.)

It was, by the researchers' own account, the missing piece of a puzzle. The geophysical models mapping electrical conductivity inside the planet had been growing steadily more sophisticated. What they lacked was a way to translate conductivity into chemistry. The Bayreuth laboratory results provided exactly that translation: a calibration curve linking what satellites can measure to what geologists want to know.

## Satellites That See Inside the Planet

The satellite half of the story belongs to ESA's Swarm mission — a constellation of three identical satellites launched in 2013 to map Earth's magnetic field with unprecedented precision. Swarm's magnetometers are sensitive enough to distinguish the different sources of magnetism that overlap in every measurement: the field generated by the churning liquid-iron outer core, the permanent magnetism of crustal rocks, and the fields induced in the mantle itself.

That last source is the key. Variations in the external magnetic field — driven by solar activity and other space-weather sources — induce secondary magnetic signals inside the planet. Those induced signals carry information about the electrical conductivity of the rocks they pass through, because more conductive rock supports stronger induced currents. Swarm's sensitive magnetometers, flying 450 to 530 kilometers above the surface, can detect those faint induced signals from orbit.

Grayver's team combined the Swarm data with measurements from INTERMAGNET, the global network of ground-based geomagnetic observatories. The ground stations were essential, he emphasized — they calibrate and validate the satellite measurements, and in his words, the science would not be possible without them. Together, the space and ground data underpin a global electrical-conductivity model of the mantle: a three-dimensional picture of how conductive Earth's deep interior is, voxel by voxel.

Applying the laboratory conductivity–iron relationship to that global model, the team produced the first maps of iron distribution in the lower mantle — slices at 825 and 1,225 kilometers depth where iron's signature stands out clearly.

## What the Maps Show

Two features dominate the new iron maps.

The first is a set of iron-enriched regions beneath the western Pacific and South America, lying close to major subduction zones — the places where tectonic plates plunge beneath one another and descend into the mantle. The team interprets these as vast reservoirs of ancient oceanic crust: iron-rich basaltic rock that once formed the floor of prehistoric oceans, was carried down into the mantle by subducting plates over hundreds of millions of years, and has remained there ever since. These iron-rich remnants of drowned seafloor have now been traced to depths exceeding 1,000 kilometers — far deeper than subducted slabs had been chemically fingerprinted before.

It is a striking image: the floors of oceans that vanished before complex life existed, still lying in the deep Earth, their iron content betraying their origin. Plate tectonics has been recycling the planet's surface for billions of years, and the new maps suggest that the deep mantle keeps a far more detailed archive of that recycling than anyone could previously read.

The second notable feature concerns one of the great mysteries of deep-Earth science: the large low-shear-velocity provinces, or LLSVPs. These are two enormous anomalous structures at the base of the mantle — one beneath Africa, one beneath the Pacific — each spanning thousands of kilometers. Discovered by seismic tomography decades ago, they have puzzled scientists ever since. They slow seismic waves dramatically, but seismic data alone cannot say whether that is because they are hotter than the surrounding mantle, compositionally different, or both.

The new study finds evidence of iron enrichment coinciding with the African LLSVP — a finding that supports the hypothesis that these structures are thermochemical in nature, meaning they are compositionally distinct from the surrounding mantle rather than merely hotter. Whether they represent ancient subducted material, primordial rock left over from Earth's formation that never fully mixed, or something else entirely remains an open question. But the iron signature tilts the debate toward chemistry: these are not just hot blobs, they are different stuff.

As Grayver noted, if the LLSVPs are indeed enriched with iron, the implications reach into the very long-term evolution of the planet — into questions of how Earth's interior differentiated, how heat has been transported over billions of years, and how the deep mantle couples to the surface world.

## Why Iron Runs the Planet

It is worth pausing on why iron, of all elements, deserves a satellite mission and a laboratory campaign. The answer is that iron is, in a real sense, the element that makes Earth Earth.

Iron's story begins at the planet's birth. When the young Earth was a molten ball, dense iron sank to the center to form the core — a process called differentiation that released enormous energy and set the thermal structure of the planet. The core that remains, mostly iron and nickel, generates the magnetic field that shields the atmosphere from the solar wind. In the mantle, iron substitutes into the crystal structures of minerals like bridgmanite, changing their density, their melting behavior, and their electrical and thermal conductivity.

Density is destiny in the mantle. Rock that is slightly denser sinks; rock that is slightly lighter rises. Over geological time, those tiny density differences organize the entire convective system — the currents that drag continents across the globe, feed volcanoes at mid-ocean ridges and hotspots, and recycle oceanic crust back into the deep. Iron content is one of the main levers controlling those density differences. Map the iron, and you begin to map the engine.

Iron also ties the mantle to habitability. The long-term carbon cycle, the outgassing of volatiles at volcanoes, the thermal evolution that keeps plate tectonics running — all of these depend on how the mantle convects and differentiates, and all of them feel iron's influence. Understanding where iron is concentrated, and why, is understanding one of the boundary conditions of Earth's 4.5-billion-year habitability.

## Bridgmanite: The Most Important Mineral You've Never Heard Of

Bridgmanite deserves a moment in the spotlight. Named after Percy Bridgman, the Nobel laureate who pioneered high-pressure physics, it is almost certainly the most abundant mineral on Earth — and almost no one outside geoscience has heard of it. It cannot exist at the surface; its crystal structure is only stable at the crushing pressures of the lower mantle, deeper than about 660 kilometers. Every sample ever studied has been synthesized in a laboratory, squeezed between diamond anvils and heated with lasers.

Yet this invisible mineral makes up roughly four-fifths of the lower mantle by volume, which makes it the dominant solid material of the planet's largest layer. Its properties — how it deforms, how it conducts heat and electricity, how much iron and water it can hold — set the behavior of most of the solid Earth. The Bayreuth experiments that underpin the new study are part of a decades-long effort to characterize this phantom mineral, one diamond-anvil experiment at a time.

The new work adds a crucial property to bridgmanite's dossier: its electrical conductivity as a function of iron content, measured at true lower-mantle conditions. That single calibration curve is what turns Swarm's conductivity maps into iron maps. It is a beautiful example of how laboratory mineral physics — often seen as the most esoteric corner of geoscience — can unlock observations made from orbit.

## The Paper and the People

The study appears as Han, K., Özaydın, S., Fei, H., Man, L., Wang, F., Chanyshev, A., Withers, A.C., Grayver, A. and Katsura, T. (2026), "Lower-mantle iron heterogeneity constrained by the electrical conductivity of Al-bearing bridgmanite," in Science Advances, volume 12, article eaec7875. The author list itself tells the story of the collaboration: experimental mineral physicists at Bayreuth who synthesized and measured the samples, and geomagnetic modelers at Cologne who turned satellite measurements into a global conductivity model.

It is worth noting the pedigree behind the laboratory work. The Bayreuth group, including senior mineral physicist Tomoo Katsura, has spent decades pushing the frontiers of high-pressure experimentation, developing the multi-anvil and diamond-anvil techniques that make it possible to recreate the conditions of the deep mantle on a laboratory bench. Those techniques — squeezing samples between tungsten-carbide or diamond anvils while heating them with furnaces or lasers — are what allowed the team to hold bridgmanite at 27 gigapascals and 2,000 kelvin long enough to measure its electrical response. Without that experimental infrastructure, built up over a generation, the satellites would have had nothing to calibrate against.

The publication also reflects a broader trend in Earth science: the growing power of combining independent observational lenses. Seismology, mineral physics, geomagnetism, and geodesy each see the planet differently, and each has blind spots the others can fill. The iron maps of 2026 are an early dividend of that convergence — and a preview of what multi-physics deep-Earth imaging may achieve in the decade ahead.

## A New Era of Geomagnetic Exploration

For the researchers involved, the study is a beginning, not an end. Swarm was designed in part to probe the electrical properties of the mantle, which remain far less well understood than its seismic properties. The new results show what becomes possible when satellite observations and laboratory science work in concert — and the team expects to go further.

Grayver is among those working on a new generation of conductivity models that combine data from multiple satellite missions with ground observatories, promising greater resolution and deeper insight. The current maps resolve iron distribution at 825 and 1,225 kilometers; future models may sharpen those images, extend them deeper, and reveal additional iron-rich regions that the present data cannot quite separate from the background.

The approach itself may be the most important result. Seismology will always be the backbone of deep-Earth imaging — nothing else matches its resolution. But the study demonstrates that electromagnetism, measured from space, can supply an independent lens, one that sees chemistry where seismology sees ambiguity. The two methods together can do what neither can do alone: separate the effects of temperature and composition, and read the deep Earth in something closer to full color.

There is a poetic symmetry in it. To understand the iron heart of our planet, scientists had to look at it from both extremes at once: recreating the deep mantle in a laboratory the size of a thimble, and reading its magnetic whispers from satellites hundreds of kilometers overhead. The maps that resulted — iron-rich ancient seafloor pooled beneath the Pacific and South America, a chemically distinct province looming beneath Africa — are the first of their kind. They will not be the last.

Somewhere beneath our feet, the floors of oceans that disappeared before the first fish swam are still sinking, still iron-rich, still shaping the convection that moves the ground under our cities. We cannot go there. But for the first time, we can see it — in iron, mapped from space.
