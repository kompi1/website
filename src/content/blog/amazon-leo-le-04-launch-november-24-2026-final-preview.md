Amazon's battered satellite-internet programme gets its second chance in two days on Tuesday, when an Ariane 6 rocket is due to lift off from French Guiana carrying 36 Amazon Leo satellites into low Earth orbit. The flight, designated VA271 by Arianespace and LE-04 by Amazon, was supposed to fly on Monday. Instead, a technical fault in the ground equipment that prepares the launcher stopped the countdown hours before liftoff, and the rocket never left the pad.

Arianespace moved fast. Within hours of Monday's scrub, the company announced that its teams had completed the analyses and checks needed to restart launch operations, and it set a new window for Tuesday, November 24, 2026: 10:45 to 11:14 UTC, which is 17:45 to 18:14 in Jakarta. The rocket and its 36 passengers, Arianespace said, "remain in stable and safe conditions."

The stakes are plain. This is Amazon's first launch attempt since November 15, when a United Launch Alliance Vulcan rocket failed minutes into its first flight for Amazon Leo and all 40 satellites aboard were lost. A clean flight on Tuesday would put 36 more satellites into a constellation that Amazon says already numbers 392 working spacecraft, and it would take the total launched by Arianespace for Amazon to 136. A second consecutive failure would deepen a difficult month for a programme that is racing to build the third-largest satellite constellation in orbit.

## The new window: Tuesday evening, Jakarta time

The revised launch window is a 29-minute slot, and it opens earlier than Monday's did. On Monday, the window was set for 11:09 to 11:38 UTC; on Tuesday, it runs from 10:45 to 11:14 UTC. Converted to the time zones that matter to the people watching, that is 05:45 to 06:14 in Washington, D.C., 07:45 to 08:14 in Kourou, 11:45 to 12:14 in Paris and Berlin — and 17:45 to 18:14 WIB in Jakarta.

The shift matters for anyone planning to watch live. Arianespace streams its launches on its YouTube channel, and the evening slot in Southeast Asia makes this one of the more watchable launches of the year for the region: liftoff falls in the early evening, and the flight itself is a long one. The final satellite separation is expected about two hours and four minutes after liftoff, which puts the last deployment at roughly 19:49 WIB if the rocket leaves at the opening of the window, or about 20:18 WIB if it goes at the close.

That long coast is a feature of the mission design, not a delay. The Ariane 6 upper stage, powered by its restartable Vinci engine, fires twice and releases the 36 satellites one after another between one hour 39 minutes and two hours four minutes after liftoff. A third firing then brings the upper stage down out of orbit, so it does not linger as debris. The satellites separate at an altitude of about 465 kilometres, and from there Amazon's mission operations centre in Redmond, Washington, takes control of them, checks their health, and raises each one to its operational height of about 630 kilometres.

## What stopped Monday's countdown

Monday's scrub was a ground problem, not a rocket problem — at least as far as Arianespace has described it. The company's first update said the launch chronology "was interrupted following a technical event affecting the launcher's ground support systems," and that technical teams were assessing the event and preparing for launch operations to resume. A later update confirmed the rescheduled date and said the necessary analyses and checks had been carried out.

The stop came early in the sequence. The chronology was halted before the mobile gantry — the tall service tower that encloses the rocket on the pad — was rolled back, and before the launcher's own systems had begun operating. That sequence points to a pad-side issue rather than a fault in the vehicle itself, although Arianespace has not said which piece of ground equipment failed or what the fix involved. Launch trackers logged the hold at around 06:43 UTC on Monday, or 13:43 WIB, with the window still hours away.

The caution is understandable. Monday's attempt was already carrying the weight of the Vulcan failure nine days earlier, and Arianespace had no reason to push a rocket off the pad with an unresolved ground fault. Scrubbing before the gantry moved also kept the operation simple: the vehicle stayed cocooned in its tower, the satellites stayed sealed inside the fairing, and the turnaround to a second attempt took a single day. That is about as clean a scrub as a launch campaign gets.

## The rocket: Ariane 64 and the P160C boosters

Flight VA271 is the tenth Ariane 6 launch since the rocket's debut, and the fourth in its most powerful configuration, the Ariane 64, which flies with four solid rocket boosters strapped to its core. It is also only the second flight to use the upgraded P160C boosters, each carrying 156 tonnes of solid propellant — 14 tonnes more than the boosters they replace. The extra propellant lifts what the rocket can carry to low Earth orbit to about 22 tonnes.

The first P160C flight, in June, carried Amazon's previous batch of 36 satellites, so Tuesday's mission is in a sense a repeat of a proven combination: the same rocket, the same boosters, the same payload count, the same customer. That continuity is part of why the one-day turnaround was possible. Nothing about the vehicle had to change between Monday and Tuesday; only the ground fault had to be understood and cleared.

The P160C upgrade is also a marker of where European launch is heading. Ariane 6 flew four times in 2025, and up to eight flights were planned for 2026, with an eventual ramp to eleven launches a year. The P160C boosters are shared with the future Vega C evolution, so every flight that proves them in service de-risks two European rockets at once. For Amazon, which has bought 24 Ariane 6 launches — six of them added to the contract only in September, taking the total from 18 to 24, with the new flights starting in 2029 and running through 2031 — the boosters are the workhorse that will carry a large share of its constellation.

Amazon's reliance on Ariane 6 has only grown since the Vulcan failure. The <a href="/blog/vulcan-lv-01-launch-failure-november-15-2026/">Vulcan LV-01 launch failure</a> on November 15 destroyed 40 Amazon Leo satellites and grounded the only other rocket that had been flying Amazon's constellation. ULA has not flown since, and has not said when it will. Its preliminary indications, the company has said, point to an inadequate fuel supply to the first stage's two engines — not a problem with the BE-4 engines themselves, but with getting enough propellant to them. While that investigation runs, the Ariane 6 manifest is Amazon's clearest path to orbit.

## Why this flight matters for Amazon

Amazon Leo is the company's bid to join the satellite-internet business, and it is now a serious constellation by any measure. Amazon has 392 Leo satellites in orbit, all of them working, according to the company's own count — and it calls the network the third-largest constellation in orbit. The 36 satellites on Tuesday's flight would take that count to 428. Amazon plans more than 3,000 satellites in its first constellation design, so LE-04 is an early step rather than a late one, but the pace matters: every launch now is a launch toward a network that has to reach critical mass before it can sell a service at scale.

The arithmetic of the constellation is unforgiving. Amazon lost 40 satellites in a single morning on November 15, more than an entire Ariane 6 launch carries. The 36 satellites on LE-04 almost exactly replace that loss, which is why the flight has taken on a significance beyond its manifest. A success does not just add satellites; it restores the number Amazon had before the Vulcan failure, and it proves that the programme can absorb a catastrophic loss and keep launching within nine days.

It also matters because the competition is not standing still. The count of tracked objects in orbit stood at 32,979 on Monday, according to public catalogues, and the 36 new satellites would push it past 33,000 once they are catalogued — a small milestone, but a reminder of how crowded low Earth orbit has become and how quickly the constellations are growing. Every slot in the sky is contested, and every launch window that slips is a window a rival can use.

There is a third reason Tuesday matters, and it has nothing to do with Amazon. Arianespace is carrying the European end of the heavy-lift market almost single-handedly while its competitors work through their own problems. A clean VA271 would be the tenth consecutive success of the Ariane 6 programme and the second straight success of the P160C booster — the kind of record that turns a new rocket into a trusted one. A failure, especially one so soon after a scrub, would invite the opposite question. The rocket, the boosters, and the ground systems all have something to prove on Tuesday evening, and they have to prove it in front of a customer that has just watched another rocket fail with its hardware aboard.

## LE-01 to LE-04: how the count has grown

Tuesday's flight is the fourth Amazon Leo mission Arianespace has flown, and the pattern of the campaign is visible in the numbers. The first two flights, in February and April, each carried 32 satellites. The third, in June, carried 36 — the first flight of the upgraded P160C boosters. LE-04 carries 36 again, and if it succeeds, Arianespace will have launched 136 Amazon Leo satellites in total.

| Flight | Launch | Vehicle | Satellites | Cumulative total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LE-01 | February 2026 | Ariane 64 | 32 | 32 |
| LE-02 | April 2026 | Ariane 64 | 32 | 64 |
| LE-03 | June 2026 | Ariane 64 (first P160C flight) | 36 | 100 |
| LE-04 | November 24, 2026 (planned) | Ariane 64 (second P160C flight) | 36 | 136 |

The gap between LE-03 and LE-04 is the story of the year. Five months passed between the June flight and the November attempt, and in that time Amazon's launch plans were reshaped by events outside Kourou. The company ordered six more Ariane 64 launches in September, extending its contract with Arianespace from 18 flights to 24, with the new missions starting in 2029 and running through 2031. Then the Vulcan failure in November removed 40 satellites from the constellation in a single stroke and grounded one of the programme's two launch vehicles. The long gap, the expanded contract, and the sudden loss together explain why a routine 36-satellite flight is being watched as closely as a debut.

The comparison with the earlier flights also shows what has not changed. The payload count has stabilised at 36, the vehicle has stabilised on the Ariane 64 with P160C boosters, and the deployment profile — release at about 465 kilometres, raise to about 630 kilometres under Amazon's control from Redmond — is now a routine the teams have run three times. The only thing that has not been routine is the launch campaign itself, and that is what Tuesday is really testing: whether Arianespace can take a scrubbed countdown on Monday and turn it into a clean liftoff on Tuesday.

## What to watch on Tuesday

The first thing to watch is the simplest: whether the countdown runs clean this time. A scrub on Monday was a ground fault; a second hold on Tuesday, for any reason, would raise harder questions about the campaign. The window is short — 29 minutes — so there is little room for a hold and a recycle. If the rocket is not off the pad by 11:14 UTC, the day is done and the wait begins again.

The second thing to watch is the long coast. Most launch coverage ends at liftoff; this flight's real work happens two hours later, when the Vinci engine restarts and the satellites begin to separate one by one. Arianespace's own timeline puts the first deployment at about one hour 39 minutes after liftoff and the last at about two hours four minutes. Confirmation of those deployments, and of the satellites' health once Amazon's Redmond team takes control, is the difference between a launch and a mission.

The third thing to watch is what happens next. The <a href="/blog/ariane-64-p160c-amazon-leo/">Ariane 64 P160C boosters</a> flew their debut in June and are flying for only the second time on Tuesday; a clean flight cements them as the standard for Amazon's remaining 20 Ariane 6 launches. The programme has already ordered six more flights beyond the original 18, and the cadence of those flights will depend on how quickly Arianespace can turn rockets around. A one-day scrub turnaround, followed by a clean launch, is exactly the kind of operational rhythm the contract assumes.

There is also the question of the other rocket. Vulcan has not flown since November 15, and ULA has not said when it will return. Until it does, Ariane 6 is Amazon's only ride to orbit, and every Amazon launch is an Ariane 6 launch by default. That concentration is a risk the programme accepted when it split its constellation across providers, and Tuesday is the first test of whether the European leg of that plan can carry the load alone. The <a href="/blog/ariane-6-mtg-i2-launch/">Ariane 6 MTG-I2 launch</a> earlier showed the rocket can handle demanding missions beyond low orbit; LE-04 will show whether it can handle a demanding schedule.

## A second shot, on a short fuse

Monday's scrub was the best kind of failure a launch can have: early, on the ground, with nothing damaged and no hardware lost. The rocket stayed in its tower, the satellites stayed in their fairing, and the teams had a day to find the fault and clear it. That is the version of events Arianespace is selling, and the one-day turnaround is the evidence offered for it.

But the context has changed since the June flight. Amazon has lost 40 satellites to a rocket failure, its other launch vehicle is grounded indefinitely, and its constellation is the third-largest in orbit only because the two ahead of it are larger still. A 36-satellite launch no longer just adds capacity; it replaces a loss, defends a schedule, and keeps a programme that has ordered 24 flights moving while its backup stands down.

Tuesday evening will tell whether the ground fault was the small, contained problem Arianespace describes, or the first sign of a campaign under strain. If the Ariane 6 lifts off inside its 29-minute window, deploys its satellites across the two-hour coast, and hands 36 healthy spacecraft to the team in Redmond, the story of LE-04 will be a footnote about a one-day delay. If it does not, the story becomes something else entirely — and the countdown to a third attempt will start the moment the window closes.
