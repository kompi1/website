A small black rocket on a remote New Zealand peninsula is about to do something no commercial small-lift rocket has ever done. On Saturday, November 14, Rocket Lab plans to fly its Electron rocket for the 100th time, carrying a Japanese radar satellite into orbit in a mission named "The Mountain God Rises." If the flight goes as planned, Electron will become the first commercial small-lift orbital rocket in history to reach triple digits — a milestone that took the company nearly a decade to build, and one that says as much about the small-satellite revolution as it does about the rocket itself.

The launch is scheduled for no earlier than 6:30 p.m. New Zealand Daylight Time on Saturday, November 14, which is 05:30 UTC, 2:30 p.m. Japan time, and 12:30 p.m. WIB on Saturday in Indonesia. Rocket Lab will fly from Launch Complex 1 on the Mahia Peninsula, the same pad from which nearly all of Electron's flights have departed. The payload is a single satellite, QPS-SAR-19, nicknamed YAMATSUMI-II, built by the Japanese company iQPS for its growing constellation of synthetic aperture radar Earth-observation satellites. The rocket will place it into a 575-kilometre low Earth orbit inclined at 42 degrees.

Rocket Lab itself has framed the flight as a defining moment for the company and for the class of rocket Electron represents. "Electron reaching 100 launches is an unprecedented milestone for a commercial small-lift orbital rocket and a threshold rarely crossed in modern spaceflight," the company said in its mission description. "Over its 99 missions to date, Electron has rewritten the playbook when it comes to launch for small satellites, and delivering an Earth-observation satellite for iQPS to space on our 100th launch reflects the very mission that started it all: opening up access to space to improve life on Earth."

## The mission: The Mountain God Rises

The Mountain God Rises is, in many ways, a familiar Electron mission. It is the 11th dedicated Electron flight for iQPS, the Japanese Institute for Q-shu Pioneers of Space, which is building a constellation of small radar satellites to image the Earth in near real time. Rocket Lab is the primary launcher for that constellation, and the relationship has become one of the steadiest in the small-launch business: eight more dedicated flights are already booked to complete the constellation by 2030.

The satellite aboard is QPS-SAR-19, known as YAMATSUMI-II, named after a Japanese mountain deity. It will join a sister satellite, QPS-SAR-11 YAMATSUMI-I, which flew on Electron in June 2025 under the mission name "The Mountain God Guards." The naming is deliberate: iQPS names its satellites after Shinto deities of sea and mountain, and Rocket Lab has leaned into the theme, giving the paired missions matching names. The <a href="/blog/rocket-lab-99th-mission-sea-god-surveys-iqps/">99th Electron flight</a>, flown just eleven days before the 100th, was called "The Sea God Surveys" and carried QPS-SAR-20, WADATSUMI-II, a sea-god namesake.

There is a quirk in the numbering that has confused some observers: QPS-SAR-20 launched before QPS-SAR-19. The reason is mundane. iQPS assigns satellite numbers in the order launches are contracted, not the order they fly, so the reversal reflects manufacturing and integration schedules rather than any change to the constellation plan. The two flights are also a technical first for the programme: the November 2 launch placed WADATSUMI-II into an orbital plane already occupied by another iQPS satellite, and The Mountain God Rises will repeat the manoeuvre in an adjacent plane, doubling up slots in the constellation's architecture.

The launch window opens at 6:30 p.m. NZDT on Saturday, November 14 — 05:30 UTC, 12:30 p.m. WIB, 2:30 p.m. JST, 12:30 a.m. Eastern, and 9:30 p.m. Pacific on Friday, November 13. As with every "no earlier than" date, the flight can slip for weather, technical checks, or range requirements, and Rocket Lab has not published a backup date. What cannot slip is the count behind it: 99 Electron rockets have already flown, and the hundredth is next, whichever day it goes.

## Electron at a glance

The rocket flying the mission is, by orbital-rocket standards, tiny. Electron is a two-stage vehicle built to carry small satellites to orbit on dedicated flights, rather than as secondary passengers on big rockets. Its defining technology is the Rutherford engine, the first electric-pump-fed engine ever to power an orbital-class rocket. Instead of the complex turbomachinery that drives propellant into most rocket engines, Rutherford uses battery-powered electric motors, an approach that made the engine simpler to build and, crucially, largely 3D-printed.

The numbers tell a story of acceleration. It took Electron seven years to reach 50 flights. It took just two more years to reach 100. Twenty of those flights have come in 2026 alone, a record year for the rocket and for Rocket Lab. The company, founded in New Zealand and now publicly traded in the United States, has made Electron the second most frequently launched American rocket, behind only SpaceX's Falcon 9.

## A decade of firsts: the road to 100

Electron's story begins in failure, or something close to it. The rocket completed flight qualification in December 2016, and its first launch, on May 25, 2017, carried the mission name "It's a Test." The rocket crossed the Karman line into space but failed to reach orbit because of a glitch in ground communications equipment. It was a test flight, and it tested exactly what it needed to: the vehicle worked; the ground systems did not.

The second flight, "Still Testing," on January 21, 2018, reached orbit and deployed three CubeSats. The third flight, "It's Business Time," on November 11, 2018 — exactly eight years before the 100th flight's launch window — was Electron's first commercial mission. Since then, the rocket has launched successfully 95 times, with four failures across its 99 flights, a record that includes the loss of customer payloads in July 2020, the first failure after the maiden test flight.

What made Electron different was never raw power. It was the business model. Before Electron, a small satellite typically waited months or years for a spare slot on a large rocket, sharing the ride and accepting whatever orbit the primary payload dictated. Electron offered the opposite: a dedicated rocket, a dedicated orbit, and a schedule measured in weeks. For the constellation builders, Earth-observation companies, and research institutions that became Rocket Lab's customers, that control was worth the premium over ridesharing.

The company has kept experimenting with the rocket even as the flight count climbed. In August 2019, Rocket Lab announced plans to recover and eventually reuse Electron's first stage, a remarkable ambition for a rocket that had been designed as expendable. The recovery programme progressed through guided re-entry tests, parachute trials, and, on Flight 26, the first attempt to catch a returning booster mid-air with a helicopter. The helicopter catch was later abandoned in favour of ocean recovery, but the programme produced tangible results: Rocket Lab has recovered the first stage twice, one recovered Rutherford engine passed five full-duration hot-fire tests and was declared ready to fly again, and the company's 40th Electron mission flew a refurbished Rutherford engine recovered from an earlier flight.

Electron has also grown beyond its original role. A suborbital variant called HASTE flies defence and hypersonics test missions, and the rocket's Photon spacecraft bus has carried payloads to the Moon and deep space. Rocket Lab built NASA's twin <a href="/blog/escapade-powered-earth-flyby-mars-november-2026/">ESCAPADE spacecraft</a>, which are now on their way to Mars after powered Earth flybys this month, and the company is developing the larger <a href="/blog/rocket-lab-neutron-delay-2027/">Neutron rocket</a>, whose debut has been delayed to 2027 as chief executive Peter Beck prioritises reliability over a revised launch target.

## The customer: a radar constellation that sees through cloud

The satellite riding Electron's 100th flight is small — about 100 kilograms — and its job is to see the Earth in a way optical cameras cannot. QPS-SAR-19 carries a synthetic aperture radar, an instrument that illuminates the ground with its own microwave pulses and records the returning echoes, building up an image from the motion of the satellite itself. Because the radar supplies its own illumination, it works at night. Because microwaves pass through cloud and rain, it works in any weather. For disaster monitoring, infrastructure inspection, and maritime surveillance, that reliability is the point.

The engineering behind it is striking. Each QPS-SAR satellite carries a deployable antenna 3.6 metres across that folds down to about 80 centimetres in diameter and 15 centimetres tall for launch — a 100-kilogram spacecraft achieving a resolution of less than one metre, in a package the company describes as one-twentieth the mass and one-hundredth the cost of a conventional radar satellite. In spotlight mode, the instrument achieves about 46 centimetres of resolution; in stripmap mode, about 1.8 metres across a wider scene. iQPS has reported successful image acquisition at 46-centimetre resolution from the constellation.

The ambition is a 36-satellite constellation, organised as four orbital planes of nine satellites each, able to observe almost any point on Earth with an average revisit interval of about ten minutes. That is the "near-real-time" service iQPS is selling: not a picture of a place once a day, but a fresh radar image every few minutes, day or night, in any weather. The company began launching the constellation in December 2019 with QPS-SAR-1, Izanagi. As of August 2026, roughly 18 satellites had launched and about 10 were operational. The roadmap targets 24 operational satellites by late 2028 and the full 36-satellite network by 2030.

The Mountain God Rises matters to that plan because of where its satellite goes. QPS-SAR-19 will fly to a 575-kilometre orbit at 42 degrees inclination, joining an orbital plane that already hosts a sibling satellite, doubling the coverage in that slot. Its companion, QPS-SAR-20, did the same in an adjacent plane eleven days earlier. The paired flights are how a constellation gets built in practice: not one satellite at a time in isolation, but plane by plane, with each launch thickening the network's coverage until the revisit time falls from hours to minutes.

iQPS is not Rocket Lab's only constellation customer, but it is the most loyal. The Mountain God Rises is the 11th dedicated Electron flight for the company, and eight more are already booked under an agreement to complete the constellation by 2030. For a launch provider, that kind of repeat business is the real prize of the 100-flight milestone: not the number itself, but the proof, flight after flight, that a customer will come back.

## Why 100 matters

A hundred launches is a rare threshold in modern spaceflight, and it is rarer still for a small rocket. The large rockets that dominate the industry — Falcon 9, Soyuz, the Long March family — reached triple digits on the back of government programmes, heavy payloads, and, in SpaceX's case, a reusable booster that turned one rocket into many flights. Electron reached it differently: one small rocket at a time, flying dedicated missions for customers who could never have afforded a rocket of their own.

The comparison that matters most is the one Rocket Lab draws for itself. In its own telling, it took seven years to reach 50 Electron flights, and just two more years to reach 100. That acceleration is the story of the small-satellite market maturing: constellations that were once speculative are now being built, and the launch cadence required to build them has gone from a handful of flights a year to nearly two a month. Electron flew its 99th mission on November 3 and its 100th is scheduled eleven days later — a turnaround that would have been unthinkable in the rocket's early years, when months passed between flights.

There is a counting footnote worth stating plainly. Rocket Lab's tally of 99 includes the HASTE suborbital variant of Electron, and some trackers count only orbital flights, putting the orbital-only number at 90. The distinction does not change the milestone — Rocket Lab calls this its 100th Electron launch, and the mission page says so — but it is a reminder that launch counts, like all statistics in spaceflight, depend on what you count. The record that matters to customers is the one in the table: 95 successes and 4 failures in 99 flights, a reliability record that has kept the manifest full.

The milestone also lands at an interesting moment for the company. Rocket Lab reported its third-quarter 2026 results on November 9, and its next big rocket, Neutron, has slipped to a 2027 debut while the company works through reliability priorities. Electron, in the meantime, is doing the work: it is the rocket paying the bills, flying the manifest, and proving the launch cadence that Neutron will eventually need to match at a larger scale. The company has also moved beyond launch into spacecraft manufacturing — it built the twin ESCAPADE orbiters now cruising toward Mars — and Electron's 100th flight is, in that sense, a celebration of the business the company started with, not the one it is becoming.

For the wider industry, the lesson of Electron's first 100 flights is that small launch can be a real business, not just a demonstration. The rocket did not replace ridesharing — most small satellites still fly as secondary payloads on big rockets — but it proved that dedicated small launch could survive commercially, at a cadence and reliability that customers would pay for. That is a thinner claim than "revolution," and a more durable one.

## What to watch on Saturday

The launch is scheduled for 6:30 p.m. NZDT on Saturday, November 14 — 12:30 p.m. WIB the same day in Indonesia, 05:30 UTC, and 9:30 p.m. Pacific on Friday evening in the United States. Rocket Lab has not announced a backup window, and the company will confirm the attempt on launch day. The flight profile is standard for an iQPS mission: liftoff from Launch Complex 1, a climb to the northeast from Mahia, stage separation, and deployment of QPS-SAR-19 into its 575-kilometre orbit at 42 degrees, where the satellite will unfold its 3.6-metre radar antenna and begin commissioning.

What happens after liftoff will be familiar to anyone who has watched Electron fly: a small rocket doing a small job with outsized precision. But the number on the fairing is what people will remember. When the 100th Electron clears the pad at Mahia, it will carry with it the first decade of the small-satellite era — the test flight that missed orbit, the first commercial launch eight years ago to the day, the helicopter catch that didn't work out, the recovered engine that flew again, and the twenty flights of 2026 that turned a milestone into a routine.

Rocket Lab has spent the week celebrating the number. It dressed the rocket up for launch 100, printed limited-edition T-shirts, and posted that the 100th Electron had left the factory and was headed for the pad. That is marketing, but it is also a fair reflection of what the number means: for a company that started by promising to open space to small satellites, the 100th flight is the receipt. The next hundred, if they come, will be built on the manifest Electron has already earned — one small rocket, one dedicated orbit, one customer at a time.

## A small rocket's big number

Electron's first flight missed orbit. Its hundredth will carry a satellite that watches the Earth through cloud and night, for a customer that has now flown with Rocket Lab eleven times. That arc — from a test article over the Pacific to a radar constellation in the service of disaster response — is the real story of the 100th launch. The rocket did not get bigger. The market around it did, and Electron grew up inside it, one flight at a time, until the count itself became the news. On Saturday, if the weather and the hardware cooperate, a small black rocket will lift off from a peninsula in New Zealand and make a quiet piece of history: the first commercial small-lift rocket to fly 100 times. The mountain god, as Rocket Lab has named it, is rising — and this time, the whole small-satellite industry will be watching.
