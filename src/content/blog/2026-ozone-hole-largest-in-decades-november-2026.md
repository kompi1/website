The 2026 ozone hole above Antarctica grew into one of the largest seen in nearly half a century of monitoring, satellite data released this month show — and yet the story it tells is more reassuring than alarming. By 20 September 2026, the hole covered 27.3 million square kilometres, an area far larger than Antarctica itself and about 31 percent above the average of the last 20 years for that date. It was the widest the hole has been in a decade. But while the hole sprawled across an unusually large area, it did not dig unusually deep. Measurements from Europe's Copernicus Sentinel-5P satellite show the depth of ozone depletion stayed close to average, and NASA and NOAA scientists, reporting their annual assessment on 4 November, ranked the 2026 hole the seventh largest in 48 years of monitoring while confirming that the ozone layer's long recovery remains on track.

That combination — wide but shallow — is what makes the 2026 season scientifically interesting. It is a weather story more than a chemistry story. An unusually strong and persistent polar vortex kept the Antarctic stratosphere cold enough for ozone destruction for longer than usual, letting the hole expand beyond its normal extent. The chemicals that destroy ozone, meanwhile, are scarcer in the atmosphere than they were at their peak a quarter of a century ago, thanks to the Montreal Protocol. The result is a hole that looks dramatic on a map but is, in the words of the scientists who track it, a reminder that the ozone layer is healing — unevenly, and with large year-to-year swings, but healing all the same.

## A hole nearly as big as the record

Two independent monitoring systems converged on the same verdict this autumn: the 2026 hole was big. The European Space Agency, drawing on data from the Copernicus Sentinel-5P satellite and the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service, reported on 26 November that the hole reached 27.3 million square kilometres by 20 September — around 6.5 million square kilometres above the average of the last 20 years for that time of year. NASA and NOAA, using their own satellite record and balloon measurements, put the greatest single-day extent slightly differently, at 27.1 million square kilometres on 18 September. The small difference reflects different instruments and measurement days, not disagreement: both place the 2026 peak at roughly 90 percent of the largest ozone hole ever observed, the record set on 9 September 2000, when the hole stretched to nearly 30 million square kilometres.

Averaged over the whole peak depletion season, from 7 September to 13 October, the hole covered about 25 million square kilometres — 9.6 million square miles, more than twice the area of the United States. By that seasonal measure, NASA and NOAA rank 2026 as the seventh largest hole since monitoring began in 1979, and the largest in a decade. The German Aerospace Center, analysing the same Copernicus data in October, noted that the hole had already reached around 25 million square kilometres by 12 September, about 5 million square kilometres above the average for that period, and kept growing faster than average through the rest of the month.

The contrast with recent years is stark. The 2025 ozone hole was the fifth smallest since 1992, and the 2024 hole was close to average. In a single year, the Antarctic ozone hole swung from one of the smallest on record to one of the largest. That swing is the first thing to understand about the ozone hole: its size varies naturally from year to year, driven by the weather of the stratosphere, and a single large year does not reverse a decades-long recovery trend — just as a single small year did not prove the recovery complete.

## Why 2026 grew so large

The culprit, scientists agree, was the polar vortex — the ring of powerful winds that circles Antarctica through the southern winter and spring, sealing off the air above the pole like a wall around a cold room. In 2026, that vortex was unusually strong and persistent. It kept temperatures in the Antarctic stratosphere favourable for ozone depletion for longer, allowing the hole to expand beyond its usual extent.

NASA and NOAA scientists traced the strength of the vortex to the weather far above the southern oceans. Weak planetary-scale weather systems in August and September kept the strongest vortex winds further from the South Pole than usual, enlarging the area over which ozone could be destroyed. Inside a strong, stable vortex, the stratosphere stays cold — cold enough for polar stratospheric clouds to form, and cold enough for those clouds to linger. Those clouds are the stage on which ozone destruction happens, and a longer, colder season means a bigger stage.

The vortex also explains why the hole's growth was so rapid in early September. The Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service recorded a sudden cooling in the first days of the month, with temperatures at about 20 kilometres altitude falling by around 4 degrees Celsius between 4 and 9 September, just as the hole grew fastest. Cold makes big holes, and 2026 had cold in abundance.

## Wide, but not deep

Here is the twist that the headlines often miss: the size of the ozone hole tells only part of the story. Scientists assess its development in two dimensions — its geographical extent, the area, and the degree of ozone depletion, the depth. In 2026, the two measures diverged. The hole covered a larger area than usual, but it was not as deep as other large holes on record.

The depth is measured directly above the South Pole, where NOAA scientists launch balloon-borne instruments from the South Pole Baseline Atmospheric Observatory and measure the ozone column with an optical instrument called the Dobson Ozone Spectrophotometer. In 2026, ozone concentrations directly overhead reached their lowest value for the year — 105 Dobson units — on 17 October. That is well above the record low of 92 Dobson units measured in October 2006, and the Copernicus analysis found the total ozone mass deficit for the season close to average values. In plain terms, the 2026 hole spread wide without hollowing out.

That distinction matters because depth is the more sensitive indicator of how much ozone-destroying chlorine remains in the stratosphere. A wide, shallow hole points to weather — a big, cold vortex. A deep hole points to chemistry — abundant chlorine. The 2026 season delivered the first and not the second, which is exactly what scientists would expect in an atmosphere where ozone-depleting substances are declining but the polar vortex can still, in a cold year, throw a large punch.

## How the hole forms

The ozone layer is a thin veil of gas in the stratosphere, roughly 15 to 30 kilometres above the surface, that absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation. Without it, rates of skin cancer and cataracts would climb, crops would suffer, and marine ecosystems would be damaged. The Antarctic ozone hole is a seasonal thinning of that veil, and its formation is one of the best-understood stories in atmospheric science.

It begins with chemicals humans released decades ago: chlorofluorocarbons and related ozone-depleting substances, once common in refrigerators, air conditioners, aerosol sprays and foam blowing. These compounds are so stable that they drift up into the stratosphere intact, where ultraviolet light finally breaks them apart and releases chlorine. A single chlorine atom can destroy many ozone molecules in a repeating catalytic cycle, which is why even trace amounts of these chemicals had an outsized effect.

But chlorine alone does not make a hole. Through the dark Antarctic winter, the polar vortex isolates the air above the pole, and temperatures fall low enough for polar stratospheric clouds to form — wispy, iridescent clouds quite unlike the weather clouds below. On the surfaces of those cloud particles, stable chlorine compounds are converted into reactive forms. Nothing much happens in the dark. Then, as sunlight returns to Antarctica in the southern spring, the reactive chlorine is unleashed, and ozone destruction begins in earnest.

That is why the hole follows such a regular calendar. It typically begins to form in August, grows throughout September and reaches its maximum size between mid-September and mid-October. As temperatures rise in the stratosphere during the southern spring, ozone depletion slows, the polar vortex weakens and eventually breaks down, and by the end of December the hole has usually closed and ozone levels return to normal. In 2026, the vortex stayed strong longer than usual, so the destructive season ran long and wide.

## Timeline: the 2026 Antarctic ozone hole season

- **August 2026** — The ozone hole begins to form as sunlight returns to Antarctica and a strong polar vortex locks cold air over the pole.
- **Early September 2026** — A sudden stratospheric cooling of about 4°C at 20 km altitude accelerates the hole's growth; by 12 September it covers around 25 million km², about 5 million km² above average.
- **18 September 2026** — NASA and NOAA record the year's greatest single-day extent: 27.1 million km², about 90% of the all-time record.
- **20 September 2026** — Copernicus Sentinel-5P data put the hole at 27.3 million km², 31% above the 20-year average for that date.
- **7 September – 13 October 2026** — Peak depletion season; the hole averages about 25 million km², the seventh-largest seasonal mean in 48 years of monitoring.
- **17 October 2026** — Balloon measurements at the South Pole record the year's lowest ozone overhead: 105 Dobson units, well above the record low of 92.
- **4 November 2026** — NASA and NOAA publish their annual assessment: the hole is large, but not deep, and recovery remains on track.
- **26 November 2026** — ESA reports the Copernicus Sentinel-5P view, confirming one of the largest holes in decades.
- **Late December 2026** — The hole is expected to close as the polar vortex breaks down, as it does in a typical year.

## The Montreal Protocol dividend

The most important number in the 2026 assessment may be a hypothetical one. "The 2026 ozone hole is large but could have been 1 to 2 million square miles larger if ozone-depleting substances were as prevalent in the atmosphere today as they were at their peak in 2000," said Paul Newman, a senior scientist at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County, and longtime leader of NASA's ozone research team at the Goddard Space Flight Center. That is up to 3.5 million square kilometres of extra hole — an area larger than India — that did not happen, because the world stopped producing the chemicals that cause it.

The Montreal Protocol, signed in 1987, phased out chlorofluorocarbons and other ozone-depleting substances, and it is widely regarded as the most successful environmental treaty ever negotiated. Nearly every country on Earth ratified it, and the industries that once depended on these chemicals found substitutes. The chemicals already released, however, linger in the stratosphere for decades, which is why the ozone layer's recovery is measured in generations rather than years.

There is a subtler sign of healing that Newman highlighted. "The annual ozone hole has been forming later in the season in recent years, which tells us the ozone layer is healing," he said. A later start means the vortex is breaking down earlier relative to the return of sunlight, or that there is simply less chlorine available to do damage when the Sun comes back. Either way, the seasonal rhythm is shifting in the right direction. NASA and NOAA say the ongoing monitoring shows that the controls established by the Montreal Protocol continue to drive long-term recovery, and that the protective layer remains on track to recover by later this century.

That framing is worth holding onto when a single large year makes headlines. The ozone hole's size in any given year is mostly weather. Its depth, its timing, and its long-term trend are mostly chemistry — and on those measures, 2026 was unremarkable in the best possible way.

## Eyes on the sky

None of this would be known without satellites. Monitoring the ozone hole from space is essential, because the hole sits above one of the most remote and inaccessible regions on the planet, where ground-based measurements are limited to a handful of stations. Satellites provide a global perspective, and by building consistent, long-term records they let scientists distinguish natural year-to-year fluctuations from longer-term trends.

The star of the 2026 European assessment is the Copernicus Sentinel-5P satellite, launched in October 2017 as the first Copernicus satellite dedicated to monitoring the atmosphere. It carries an advanced multispectral imaging spectrometer called Tropomi, which detects the unique fingerprints of atmospheric gases in different parts of the electromagnetic spectrum, imaging a wide range of pollutants and stratospheric ozone more accurately and at higher spatial resolution than ever before. Tropomi's daily global maps are what let the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service track the hole's growth through September almost in real time.

The record Sentinel-5P extends stretches back nearly half a century, through a relay of instruments. NASA's Total Ozone Mapping Spectrometer flew on Nimbus-7 from 1979 to 2004; the Ozone Monitoring Instrument on the Aura satellite carried the watch from 2005 to 2011; the Ozone Mapping and Profiler Suite on the Suomi NPP satellite took over from 2012 to 2025; and in 2026 the Ozone Mapping and Profiler Suite on the NOAA-20 satellite supplied the American measurements. Keeping that chain unbroken matters: the ranking of any given year — seventh largest, fifth smallest — is only meaningful because the same quantity has been measured the same way, year after year, for 48 years.

The satellite fleet that watches Earth keeps growing in other directions too. ESA's Swarm trio maps the planet's magnetic field and the hidden iron of the mantle, as described in <a href="/blog/esa-swarm-deep-earth-iron/">Iron From Space: How ESA's Swarm Satellites Mapped the Hidden Metal Heart of Earth's Mantle</a>, and new ocean-monitoring spacecraft such as <a href="/blog/china-haiyang-2e-ocean-satellite/">China's New Eye on the Ocean: Haiyang-2E Launches to Watch the World's Seas</a> extend the same idea — that orbit is the best vantage point for understanding a whole planet — to the seas. Even missions headed elsewhere still use Earth as a laboratory: <a href="/blog/europa-clipper-earth-flyby-december-3-2026/">Europa Clipper Earth Flyby December 3, 2026: NASA's Giant Probe Borrows Our Planet's Gravity</a> will swing past our planet next week, its instruments briefly turned homeward.

Looking ahead, the watch will get sharper. The Copernicus Sentinel-5 instrument, flying on the MetOp Second Generation A-type satellites, will provide long-term global measurements of atmospheric composition, while the upcoming Altius mission will deliver detailed vertical profiles of ozone and other atmospheric gases — showing not just how much ozone there is, but where exactly it sits in the column. Together, these missions will help scientists monitor the evolution and recovery of the ozone layer with greater accuracy, and satellite data fed into atmospheric models will keep testing whether international protection efforts are working as intended.

## What happens next

As December advances, the 2026 ozone hole will do what it does every year: fade. Rising stratospheric temperatures will slow the chemistry, the polar vortex will weaken and break apart, and ozone-rich air from lower latitudes will mix back over the pole. By the end of the month, ozone levels above Antarctica should be back to normal, and the hole will be gone until next August.

The question scientists will carry into 2027 is not whether the hole will return — it will — but how large the next one grows, and whether the slow healing trend continues. A cold, stable vortex can produce another large hole in any year, and the atmosphere still carries a legacy burden of ozone-depleting chemicals that will take decades to clear. But the direction of travel is now set by policy as much as by weather: every year, there is a little less chlorine in the stratosphere than the year before, and every large hole is a little smaller than it would have been without the treaty that banned the chemicals that caused it.

The 2026 season will be remembered as the year the ozone hole roared back to near-record size — and as the year the data showed, more clearly than ever, that size is not the whole story. A wide hole with a shallow heart is a weather event, not a verdict. The verdict, written in 48 years of satellite measurements and in the slow, measurable decline of chlorine overhead, is that the ozone layer is coming back. It is coming back unevenly, with dramatic years like this one along the way, but it is coming back — and the satellites watching from above will be there to measure every step of it.
