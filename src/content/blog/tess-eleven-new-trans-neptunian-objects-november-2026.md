For eight years, NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite stared at the stars and waited for planets to cross in front of them. That was its job, and it did it well. But all the while, the telescope was quietly recording something else: faint, slow-moving specks drifting across its images, far beyond Neptune, in the cold outer reaches of the Solar System. Nobody was looking for them. Now somebody has.

A paper submitted to the arXiv preprint server on November 13, 2026, reports a blind search through eight years of TESS full-frame images that turned up eleven new trans-Neptunian objects — worlds beyond Neptune that no one knew existed — plus eight more candidates that still need confirmation. The largest of the new objects may be roughly 910 kilometres across, a substantial body by the standards of the distant outer Solar System. None of them was found by pointing a telescope at the right place at the right time. All eleven were recovered from data that had been sitting in an archive for years, some of it for nearly a decade.

It is a discovery made entirely in hindsight, and that is what makes it remarkable. The objects were always there in the images. The light they reflected had been captured, stored, and filed away. What changed was the method: a way of subtracting the stars, stacking the leftovers, and following faint trails across years of observations until a smudge became a world with an orbit. The result is not just eleven new entries in a catalogue. It is proof that one of astronomy's most productive planet hunters has been doubling as a deep survey of the outer Solar System all along — and that its archive may hold more worlds still waiting to be found.

## A Telescope Built for Planets

TESS — the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite — was never meant to look at the outer Solar System. It is an MIT-led NASA Explorer mission, launched on April 18, 2018, aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket from Cape Canaveral, with a single, sharply defined purpose: find planets orbiting bright, nearby stars by watching for the tiny, regular dips in starlight that occur when a planet passes in front of its star. Its principal investigator is George Ricker of MIT, and the mission was selected for development in 2013 as a successor to the Kepler space telescope, widening the search from one patch of sky to nearly the whole celestial sphere.

The spacecraft carries four identical wide-field cameras with a combined field of view of 24 by 96 degrees — a swath of sky roughly the size of a handspan held at arm's length, multiplied many times over. Together the cameras hold 192 megapixels of detectors. TESS flies in a highly elliptical 13.7-day orbit around Earth, swinging from a perigee of about 108,000 kilometres out to an apogee of about 373,000 kilometres, and it observes one strip of sky for about 27 days — a period astronomers call a sector — before moving on to the next. Its prime mission was a two-year survey of some 200,000 bright stars, and it has kept going in extended operations ever since, covering more than 85 percent of the sky.

Every image TESS takes serves the transit search first. But a telescope that photographs the whole sky every few weeks, for years on end, cannot help recording everything else that moves or changes in its field of view. NASA itself describes TESS as a mission that, during its extended observations, also finds and monitors objects that change in brightness, from nearby asteroids to pulsating stars and distant galaxies containing supernovae. This new study pushes that secondary role much farther from the Sun than anyone had taken it before — out past Neptune, to distances of up to 57 times the Earth–Sun distance, where sunlight takes nearly eight hours to arrive and the objects themselves are so faint that they sit roughly 800,000 times below the brightness of the dimmest star the unaided human eye can see.

The search was led by Te Han, working with Jeroen Audenaert, Avi Shporer, Daniel Muthukrishna, and George Ricker. Their paper, "Eleven Trans-Neptunian Objects and Eight Candidates Discovered by TESS," was submitted on November 13, 2026. It describes a blind search — the team did not start from known orbits and check whether TESS happened to have seen the objects. They searched the data for moving objects without knowing in advance where any of them might be. That is a much harder test, and the reason the result matters: a blind search that works can be run again, on more data, and can find things nobody predicted.

## How You Find a World You Cannot See

Finding a trans-Neptunian object in TESS data is an exercise in subtraction and patience. The objects are faint — visual magnitudes between V=20.8 and V=22.1 — and they move slowly, crawling across the sky at a rate that can be measured in arcseconds per day rather than degrees. In any single TESS exposure, an object that faint is buried in noise. It cannot be seen at all. The trick is to combine many exposures so that the object's faint light adds up while the random noise averages away.

The first step is to remove the stars. The researchers modelled and subtracted the stars catalogued by the European Space Agency's Gaia mission, using a software package called the TESS-Gaia Light Curve package. Gaia has measured the positions and brightnesses of more than a billion stars with extraordinary precision, so its catalogue can predict, star by star, what TESS should see in a given patch of sky. Subtracting that prediction leaves behind a residual image: mostly noise, plus anything that moved or changed — asteroids, variable stars, supernovae, and, if the search works, distant Solar System bodies.

The second step is shift-and-stack. The team stacked the residual images along curved trial paths — the paths an object at a given distance and speed would follow across the sky. If the guessed path matches a real object's motion, its faint light piles up in the same place in every stacked frame, while the background noise, which is random, averages out. A body too dim to see in any single exposure can then stand out in the combined image. It is the same principle as a long exposure in photography, except that the "exposure" is assembled from images taken weeks or months apart, and the camera is, in effect, tracking an object whose orbit is not yet known.

The third step is linking. A signal in stacked data is not a discovery yet. Each of the eleven new objects had to clear more checks: each was detected blindly in between two and five separate TESS sectors, meaning the same moving speck showed up independently in observations taken at different times. And each was also found independently in archival ground-based images, which gave observed orbital arcs of 2 to 20 years. Those long arcs are what turn a faint smudge into an object with a usable orbit. Without an arc measured over years, an object is a curiosity; with one, it is a world whose path around the Sun can be calculated and predicted.

The search covered full-frame images taken from July 25, 2018, through November 6, 2026 — essentially the whole of TESS's life so far — and focused on fields near the ecliptic, the plane in which most Solar System bodies orbit. In total that came to 572 CCD fields across 95 observing sectors. It is a large search, but it is also a careful one: the authors validated their pipeline by checking that it could recover the objects already known to be out there, and the result of that validation is one of the most convincing parts of the paper.

## The Haul at a Glance

The eleven new objects share a family resemblance, and their shared properties say something about the population they come from. They are faint, with visual magnitudes from V=20.8 to V=22.1. They lie between 25 and 57 astronomical units from the Sun — one astronomical unit being the distance from Earth to the Sun — which puts the farthest of them nearly twice as far out as Neptune, which orbits at about 30 astronomical units. Their orbits are noticeably tilted, with inclinations of 16 to 27 degrees to the plane of the planets, and they sit 9 to 26 degrees from the ecliptic. The largest of them lies just 5 degrees from the Galactic plane, in one of the most crowded, star-choked regions of the sky — exactly the kind of place where a faint moving object is hardest to spot, and where a method that subtracts the stars first earns its keep.

|---|---|
| Number confirmed | 11 (plus 8 candidates) |
| Visual magnitude | V = 20.8 to 22.1 |
| Distance from the Sun | 25 to 57 AU |
| Orbital inclination | 16 to 27 degrees |
| Distance from the ecliptic | 9 to 26 degrees |
| Estimated diameter | About 120 to 910 km (assuming albedo 0.08) |
| TESS detections per object | 2 to 5 separate sectors |
| Observed orbital arc | 2 to 20 years (with ground-based archives) |
| Largest object | About 910 km across, 5 degrees from the Galactic plane |

The size estimates come with an important caveat, and the authors state it plainly. The paper gives diameters of roughly 120 to 910 kilometres, but those figures assume a surface albedo of 0.08 — that is, they assume each object reflects about 8 percent of the sunlight that hits it. Brightness alone cannot tell a small, reflective object apart from a larger, darker one, so the real sizes will shift if the actual albedos are different. Even so, the top of that range is large. An object near 900 kilometres across would be a substantial body by trans-Neptunian standards — comparable in scale to some of the largest known residents of the Kuiper Belt, the doughnut-shaped ring of icy bodies beyond Neptune that includes Pluto and other dwarf planets. Better size estimates will require measuring albedos directly instead of assuming one value for every object, and that is work for follow-up observations with other telescopes.

The tilt of the orbits is worth pausing on. Most of the planets orbit within a few degrees of the ecliptic plane, and many known Kuiper Belt objects stay close to it too. A population with inclinations of 16 to 27 degrees is dynamically hot — its members were stirred up, gravitationally, at some point in the Solar System's history. Each newly catalogued object adds a data point to the question of how that distant population is distributed, and tilted orbits are exactly the kind of clue that theorists use to reconstruct the migration of the giant planets billions of years ago. The Minor Planet Center's list of trans-Neptunian objects, which includes bodies such as Pluto and Sedna, keeps the orbital elements of the known population; eleven new entries, all of them tilted, all of them faint, sharpen the picture of what lives out there and what it has been through.

## The Candidates Still Waiting

Alongside the eleven confirmed objects, the search turned up eight additional candidates. They were linked across two or three TESS sectors, but the archives the authors examined did not give them a secure independent recovery, so they remain candidates rather than discoveries. That distinction matters, and the authors draw it carefully. A candidate is a real signal that has not yet earned an orbit: it could be a genuine trans-Neptunian object whose earlier sightings sit in images nobody has checked yet, or it could be something else that mimicked the motion of a distant body across a handful of sectors.

The eight candidates are the obvious next step. Confirming them will mean digging through more archival ground-based images, or taking new observations targeted at their predicted positions. Either way, the work is now well defined: the candidates have predicted paths, and a telescope pointed at the right place at the right time can settle the question. If even a few of them confirm, the haul from this single archival search grows larger still.

There is a useful lesson in the way the authors handle the boundary between discovery and candidate. A blind search that claimed everything it found would be less trustworthy, not more. By insisting on independent recovery in ground-based archives before promoting an object to a discovery, the team built a quality cut into the result — and then measured how that cut performs, which is what makes the next section of the paper the strongest evidence that the eleven are real.

## Proof the Method Works

A pipeline that turns up new objects also needs to find the ones already known. The paper reports that the search recovered 314 known trans-Neptunian objects and Centaurs — the wandering bodies that cross between the giant planets. The authors put the empirical recovery fraction at 50 percent at V=21.6, measured before quality cuts. In other words, the pipeline catches about half of the known objects at that brightness. The new objects have magnitudes of V=20.8 to 22.1, so they straddle that 50 percent point. TESS is working near the limit of what it can detect here, and objects at the faint end of that range are the ones a survey is least likely to have caught earlier. The authors conclude that the results demonstrate the effectiveness of TESS for finding bright outer Solar System objects and extending their observational arcs.

That validation matters because it answers the natural objection to an archival discovery: how do you know the pipeline is not inventing objects out of noise? The answer is that the same pipeline, run over the same data, recovered 314 real objects whose orbits were already known, at exactly the rate its sensitivity predicts. A method that hallucinates would not reproduce the known catalogue so faithfully. A method that works this well on known objects can be trusted, within its stated limits, on unknown ones.

Then there is the precovery result — the part of the paper that reads like a time machine. Nine of the 314 known objects TESS recovered were officially discovered after TESS had already photographed them. The most notable is 2026 ED138, a large body beyond Pluto reported by the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, which TESS imaged 6.8 years before its discovery. That object is a story in its own right: our earlier report on the <a href="/blog/rubin-2026-ed138-dwarf-planet-candidate/">2026 ED138 Dwarf Planet Candidate: Rubin's First Giant World Beyond Pluto</a> described a body 600 to 1,500 kilometres across, found hiding in the crowded Milky Way plane. TESS had been looking at that same crowded patch of sky for years, and the light from 2026 ED138 was already in its archive, waiting for someone to ask the right question.

This is not a sign that anyone missed something obvious. It shows what an archive can hold. When an imaging dataset covers the sky repeatedly over many years, it captures objects long before anyone knows to look for them. Recovering those earlier sightings — astronomers call it precovery — can stretch an orbital arc back by years and tighten the orbit considerably. A dataset that does that well can also produce discoveries of its own, which is what this paper reports. And the practical value runs both ways: an object first reported by another facility, like Rubin, can be traced back nearly seven years in TESS images, which extends its observed arc. TESS's eight-year record gives astronomers somewhere to look for earlier observations of objects that newer surveys report.

## Why Eleven Faint Dots Matter

It is fair to ask why eleven faint dots, each one barely brighter than the noise floor of a planet-hunting camera, should matter to anyone who is not a specialist in outer Solar System dynamics. The answer has three parts, and none of them is about the eleven objects individually.

First, each one is a data point. The outer Solar System is the least explored region of our own cosmic neighbourhood, and its population is still being mapped. Every new object with a measured orbit refines our understanding of how many bodies of each size live out there, how tilted their orbits are, and how they are distributed with distance from the Sun. Those distributions are the fossil record of the Solar System's early history: the giant planets migrated, the Kuiper Belt was stirred, and the survivors carry the scars in their orbits. Eleven tilted, faint objects do not rewrite that history on their own, but they add weight to it — and they were found in a part of the sky, near the Galactic plane, where surveys have historically struggled, which means they correct a bias in the map as well as extending it.

Second, the discovery changes what TESS is. A mission built to find exoplanets has now been shown to work as a deep survey for bright outer Solar System objects, using data that already exists and costs nothing new to collect. That is a powerful argument for archival science: the most expensive part of astronomy is building and launching the telescope, and once the data exists, new methods can extract new science from it indefinitely. The same logic has already paid off elsewhere in the archive era. Our report on the <a href="/blog/kuiper-belt-primordial-heavy-water-november-2026/">Kuiper Belt Primordial Heavy Water November 2026: Twelve Dust Grains Point to an Ancient Ocean Beyond Neptune</a> showed how twelve microscopic dust grains could carry the chemical fingerprint of an ancient water reservoir beyond Neptune — small evidence, large conclusion. The TESS result belongs to the same family: modest data, carefully processed, yielding a result no one planned for when the data was taken.

Third, the method points forward. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory is now surveying the sky deeper and faster than any facility before it, and it will report new outer Solar System objects by the thousands. Every one of those objects will have a past — earlier sightings, perhaps years earlier, sitting in archives like TESS's. The ability to reach back into an eight-year record and extend an orbital arc by years is not a curiosity; it is infrastructure for the Rubin era. An object discovered this year with a two-year arc is interesting. The same object with a nine-year arc, stitched together from TESS precovery, is a solved orbit.

There is a broader pattern here that is worth naming. Astronomy's great surveys increasingly do their best work twice: once for the science they were designed to do, and once again, years later, for science their designers never imagined. Kepler's data yielded planets, then stellar seismology, then supernovae. TESS's data yielded exoplanets, then asteroids and supernovae, and now worlds beyond Neptune. The interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS, which we covered in <a href="/blog/3i-atlas-interstellar-comet-one-year-later/">One Year After Its Solar Plunge, Interstellar Comet 3I/ATLAS Is Fading Into the Dark</a>, was itself recognised by re-examining survey images taken before anyone knew it was there. The sky does not wait for permission to be interesting, and the archives do not forget.

## TESS and the Outer Solar System: A Timeline

The discovery compresses eight years of patient observation into a single paper. The milestones behind it stretch back further still.

## From Launch to Discovery

- **2013** — TESS is selected for development as a NASA Explorer mission, led by MIT, to survey the brightest nearby stars for transiting exoplanets.
- **April 18, 2018** — TESS launches aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral and settles into its 13.7-day high-Earth orbit.
- **July 25, 2018** — The full-frame image archive that this search mines begins; over the following eight years TESS will photograph the sky sector by sector, 27 days at a time.
- **2020** — TESS completes its two-year prime mission, having surveyed some 200,000 bright stars, and continues into extended operations that keep the archive growing.
- **2019–2026** — In the background, the images accumulate. Among them, unnoticed, are the faint trails of eleven objects beyond Neptune — and, 6.8 years before anyone reports it, the light of 2026 ED138.
- **November 6, 2026** — The search's data window closes, covering 572 CCD fields across 95 TESS sectors.
- **November 13, 2026** — Han, Audenaert, Shporer, Muthukrishna, and Ricker submit "Eleven Trans-Neptunian Objects and Eight Candidates Discovered by TESS" to arXiv, reporting eleven new worlds and eight candidates.

## What Comes Next

The immediate future of this work is straightforward to describe and hard to do. The eight candidates need confirmation: archival ground-based images, or new observations pointed at their predicted positions. Better size estimates need albedo measurements, which means observing the objects at wavelengths and geometries that reveal how reflective their surfaces are, rather than assuming a single value of 0.08 for all of them. And the pipeline itself can be run again — on more TESS sectors as they arrive, on fields farther from the ecliptic, and with refined trial paths that reach fainter still.

The deeper question is how many more worlds the archive holds. The search recovered about half of the known objects at V=21.6, which means it missed about half of them too — and anything fainter than the new objects' limit of V=22.1 was out of reach entirely. A method that finds eleven worlds while missing half of the known ones at the same brightness is a method with obvious headroom. More sensitive stacking, better star subtraction, and longer baselines as TESS keeps observing will all push the limit fainter. The Kuiper Belt is thought to contain far more objects in the 100-kilometre class than in the 900-kilometre class, so every step fainter should yield more worlds, not fewer.

There is also the question of what else is hiding in the same images. The shift-and-stack technique does not care what it is looking for, only how the target moves. The same archive that yielded eleven trans-Neptunian objects could, with different trial paths, yield distant Centaurs, slow-moving asteroids, or objects on orbits no one has thought to search for. The blind search is the point: when you stop assuming you know where things are, the data gets a chance to surprise you.

For the wider field, the timing could hardly be better. Dedicated surveys are about to flood the catalogues with new outer Solar System objects, and each of them will need an orbit, and each orbit will be better for having a longer arc. TESS, a telescope that was never supposed to look out there at all, has just shown that it can supply those arcs — and that it can find new worlds of its own while it is at it. The planet hunter looked up from its stars, glanced at the dark between them, and found that the dark was full of worlds. It had been full of them all along.
