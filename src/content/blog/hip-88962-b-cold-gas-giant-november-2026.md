Sixteen years of patient watching have caught a planet that, by the standard rules of planet formation, should not be there. Astronomers using the Planet Finder Spectrograph on the 6.5-metre Magellan Clay Telescope in Chile have found a gas giant nearly five times the mass of Jupiter circling a small orange dwarf star about 89 light-years from Earth — and the giant's mass, its distant orbit, and its wildly stretched path together defy the explanation that accounts for almost every giant planet known.

The planet, HIP 88962 b, takes roughly 19 years to complete a single orbit. Its path carries it as far as about 10.6 astronomical units from its star and as close as about 1.7, a swing so extreme that its eccentricity — the measure of how far an orbit departs from a circle — is 0.718, where zero would be a perfect circle and one would be a parabola. It is a cold giant, far from the warmth of its star, and it is heavy: 4.83 times the mass of Jupiter, orbiting a star with only 62 percent of the Sun's mass. The discovery, published in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters* on 17 November 2026 after being posted to arXiv on 11 November, describes the planet as one "whose existence confounds the conventional wisdom about the formation of gas giant planets."

The team of ten, led by Alan Boss of Carnegie Science in Washington, with colleagues at Carnegie's observatories in Pasadena, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and Tsinghua University, did not photograph the planet at all. No telescope alive today could pick its faint light out of the glare of its star at that distance. Instead, they measured the star's wobble — the tiny back-and-forth motion imposed on HIP 88962 by the planet's gravity — and they measured it for so long that the wobble finally traced out a full, slow rhythm. Forty-seven sets of observations on 41 nights, spread from 2010 to 2025, caught a velocity shift of 94.9 metres per second repeating every 19.3 years. That signal, combined with position measurements from the European Space Agency's Hipparcos and Gaia satellites, pinned down the planet's true mass and its nearly edge-on orbit.

The result matters because it is not a curiosity at the margins. It is a direct challenge to the dominant theory of giant-planet formation, and it arrives at a moment when astronomers are realising that the census of planets beyond our Solar System is still badly incomplete — biased, in particular, against exactly the kind of world HIP 88962 b is: massive, cold, distant, and slow.

## A Wobble Sixteen Years in the Making

The radial-velocity method is the oldest workhorse of exoplanet science, and it is also the most patient. A planet does not only orbit its star; the star and planet orbit their common centre of mass, so the star traces a small circle of its own. As it moves toward us and away from us, its light shifts almost imperceptibly toward the blue and then toward the red. A spectrograph splits that light finely enough to measure the shift, and from the size and rhythm of the shift astronomers can work out the planet's minimum mass and its orbital period — provided they watch long enough.

"Detecting planets by precision velocities requires following the planet for most of the orbital period, preferably for more than one full orbit," said R. Paul Butler, a staff scientist at Carnegie Science's Earth and Planets Laboratory and a co-author of the study, in an email. "So if you want to detect long period planets, you need to monitor the stars for years and decades."

HIP 88962 b is a textbook case of that demand. Its year lasts about 7,055 days — nearly two decades — so a survey that runs for a few years sees only a fragment of one orbit, a slow drift that is easy to mistake for something else or to miss entirely. An earlier search had already missed it: a 2021 survey using the CHIRON spectrograph in Chile found no sign of a planet, but its ten observations spanned only 799 days, far too short a baseline to catch a wobble that takes 19 years to repeat. The Magellan team, by contrast, kept returning to the star for sixteen years, accumulating 47 sets of observations on 41 nights between 2010 and 2025, until the signal emerged unambiguously: a velocity swing of 94.9 metres per second, repeating every 19.3 years.

Butler was the one who found it. Boss, a staff scientist emeritus at Carnegie Science's Earth and Planets Laboratory and the paper's lead author, said in an email that Butler "found the planet in his PFS data and realized it was an outlier, hard to form by core accretion." He added: "He challenged me to argue how it probably formed and then to lead the paper-writing effort."

Radial velocity alone, however, gives only a minimum mass. The measured wobble tells astronomers the product of the planet's mass and the sine of its orbital tilt, and if the orbit happens to be nearly face-on to Earth, the true mass could be far larger than the minimum. To break that ambiguity the team turned to astrometry — precise measurements of the star's position and motion across the sky — from ESA's Hipparcos and Gaia satellites. The star's sideways motion betrayed the tilt of the orbit: 91 degrees, give or take 22, which is nearly edge-on as seen from Earth. With the tilt known, the minimum mass of about five Jupiters became a true mass of 4.83 Jupiter masses, and the orbit's shape came into focus: a semi-major axis of 6.15 astronomical units, give or take 0.5, and an eccentricity of 0.718, give or take 0.02.

There are caveats, and the authors do not hide them. The sixteen years of data cover less than one full orbit, and fits that add a long-term trend to the velocity curve give periods up to more than twice as long, though the team kept its original solution. Gaia's individual measurements are not yet public, so the team built simulated ones from the mission's forecast of when and how it scanned the star. And the star itself is restless. Its velocity data scatter by about six metres per second around the single-planet fit, against about three for four quiet comparison stars, and its calcium emission — a marker of magnetic activity — is about twice theirs. Data from NASA's TESS satellite suggest the star rotates roughly every 17 days. A possible second planet with a 4.11-day orbit showed up in the leftover signal, but the authors write that they "do not consider the 4.1 day signal to be a robust planet candidate." The scatter may instead be stellar activity, and the single-planet fit, including a jitter of about seven metres per second, yields a chi-squared of 0.995 — a statistically comfortable fit.

## HIP 88962 b at a Glance

The numbers that define the system are striking in combination, even though each one alone would be unremarkable. It is the combination — a heavy planet, a light star, a wide orbit, and a stretched ellipse — that makes HIP 88962 b an outlier.

| Property | HIP 88962 b | Comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Host star | HIP 88962, K7V orange dwarf | Sun is a G2V yellow dwarf |
| Host star mass | 0.62 solar masses (± 0.084) | Sun: 1.00 solar masses |
| Distance from Earth | About 89 light-years | — |
| Planet mass (true) | 4.83 Jupiter masses | Jupiter: 1.00 Jupiter masses |
| Planet minimum mass | About 5 Jupiter masses (M_p sin i) | — |
| Semi-major axis | 6.15 AU (± 0.5) | Jupiter: 5.2 AU |
| Orbital eccentricity | 0.718 (± 0.02) | Jupiter: 0.049 (nearly circular) |
| Orbital period | About 19.3 years (about 7,055 days) | Jupiter: 11.9 years |
| Radial-velocity amplitude | 94.9 metres per second | Jupiter tugs the Sun by about 12 m/s |
| Orbital inclination | 91° (± 22°, nearly edge-on) | — |
| Observations | 47 sets on 41 nights, 2010–2025 | 16 years of monitoring |
| Discovery instrument | Planet Finder Spectrograph, 6.5-m Magellan Clay Telescope | — |

The table makes the puzzle visible. Jupiter, the largest planet in our Solar System, orbits at 5.2 astronomical units on a nearly circular path and takes 11.9 years to go around the Sun. HIP 88962 b sits a little farther out, at 6.15 astronomical units, but it weighs almost five Jupiters, it circles a star barely more than half the Sun's mass, and its orbit is so elongated that its distance from its star swings from roughly 1.7 astronomical units at closest approach to roughly 10.6 at its farthest. A planet on such a path spends most of its long year far from its star, drifting through the cold outer reaches of its system, and then sweeps in close for a brief, faster passage. Nothing in our Solar System behaves like that: even Mercury, the most eccentric of the eight planets, has an eccentricity of only about 0.21.

The mass ratio is what troubles theorists most. The planet holds about 0.8 percent of its star's mass. Kaitlin Kratter, a professor of astronomy at the University of Arizona who was not involved in the study, said in an email that when considering the viability of gravitational instability versus core accretion, "one of the most important parameters is the system mass ratio." She wrote: "For this system, M_planet / M_star ~ .008. This is in a tricky regime of parameter space where both mechanisms struggle. It's still low mass ratio for gravitational instability, but very high for core accretion. It would be equivalent to an 8 Mjup planet in our solar system."

## Why the Authors Rule Out the Usual Route

Most giant planets are thought to form by core accretion, a bottom-up process. Rocky material collides and sticks, building a solid core about ten times the mass of Earth, and once that core is massive enough its gravity pulls in the surrounding hydrogen and helium, ballooning into a giant. The process works well close to a star, where solid material is dense, and it works well around Sun-like stars, where there is plenty of it. Around a small star, far out, it should struggle: the disk of planet-building material is thinner, the orbital times are longer, and the raw ingredients are scarcer.

The authors tested that expectation against published computer models of core accretion for low-mass stars, and the models said no. A 2021 population study by Burn and colleagues produced essentially no planets heavier than about one Jupiter beyond one astronomical unit for stars of 0.5 or 0.7 solar masses, the new paper says. A 2022 study led by Schlecker, using the same model, found no massive gas giants with orbits longer than 1,000 days around such stars. HIP 88962 b takes about 7,055 days. The giants that did form in the Burn models had average eccentricities of 0.19 and 0.14 — far below this planet's 0.718. On every axis the models were tested on — mass, distance, period, eccentricity — the planet lands where core accretion says it should not be.

The authors favour another idea: gas disk gravitational instability. In this picture, a young star's disk of gas does not patiently assemble a core. Instead, part of the disk becomes heavy and cold enough to collapse under its own gravity, forming a giant planet directly, in a single gravitational convulsion — more like a star forming than a planet growing. The paper cites Boss's own 1997 paper in *Science* as the source for the mechanism, and the three modelling studies it cites in support are also his, one with a co-author. Those models formed giants of about one to 20 Jupiter masses around 0.5 solar-mass stars, with clumps starting at four to 10 astronomical units — a range that comfortably contains HIP 88962 b's 4.83 Jupiter masses at 6.15 astronomical units.

That lineage is worth noting honestly. The case for disk instability rests on the planet's mass and orbit not matching core-accretion models, and the authors concede that the supporting models for their favoured mechanism are less complete. "While none of these GDGI models performed a population synthesis model on the scale of the core accretion models previously described, it is clear that the GDGI mechanism is quite capable of having formed an exoplanet similar to HIP88962 b," they write. The paper is not claiming a proof. It is claiming that one mechanism fails clearly and the other can, in principle, succeed — and that the failure of the first is itself a finding.

Kratter's reading adds a useful complication. She pointed out that for solar-type stars, the mass range this planet lives in is strangely empty: "There seems to be a dearth of objects between roughly 4-7 Jupiter masses, and the properties of objects below the gap are systematically different from ones above the gap. For lower mass stars, the gap could easily shift to lower masses, suggesting that at ~5 Mjup, this objects falls in the 'high mass' population, and fits better with GI than with core accretion." The high eccentricity, she added, "is a puzzle for either mechanism." And on the deeper question she was blunt: "For any individual planet, it's hard to say we will ever know for *sure* how something formed." Nevertheless, she wrote, "I think it's quite reasonable to think this object could be one of the 'failed binaries' formed through disk fragmentation that we've hypothesized about."

That phrase — "failed binaries" — captures the stakes. If HIP 88962 b formed by disk fragmentation, it sits on the borderline between planet and star: a body that began to form the way a companion star forms, but stopped short. The line between a massive planet and a brown dwarf, the failed star that never ignited, has always been blurry, and objects like this one blur it further. The paper lists earlier cases read the same way, including a 3.6 Jupiter-mass planet around the M dwarf GJ 463 reported by Sozzetti in 2023. HIP 88962 b is heavier, farther out, and more eccentric than that case, and around a star only slightly more massive — which is why the authors present it as the strongest example yet of a cold giant that core accretion cannot explain.

## Two Ways to Build a Giant

The argument over HIP 88962 b is, at heart, an argument about how the universe builds its largest planets, and it is worth laying the two rival accounts side by side, because the planet's numbers discriminate between them more sharply than most discoveries do.

Core accretion is the establishment view, and it has earned its position. It explains the Solar System's giants, including the details that matter: Jupiter and Saturn are enriched in heavy elements relative to the Sun, exactly as a model that starts with a rocky core predicts, and the correlation between a star's metal content and its likelihood of hosting a giant planet is one of the most solid results in exoplanet science. The mechanism is slow — building a ten-Earth-mass core can take millions of years — but protoplanetary disks live for a few million years, so the timing usually works, at least close in and around Sun-like stars. Its weakness is at the edges: far from the star, where orbital periods are long and solid material is sparse, the clock runs out before the core is finished. Around low-mass stars the problem is worse, because the disks are less massive to begin with. HIP 88962 b sits at both edges at once.

|  | Core accretion | Disk gravitational instability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it works | Rocky core grows by collisions, then captures gas | A cold, massive patch of the gas disk collapses directly |
| Timescale | Millions of years | Thousands of years or less |
| Favoured location | Close to the star, dense solid material | Far from the star, cold outer disk |
| Favoured host star | Sun-like and heavier stars | Works around low-mass stars too |
| Typical eccentricity | Low (models average 0.14–0.19) | Can be high; fragmentation is violent |
| HIP 88962 b verdict | Models produce no such planet | Models produce 1–20 Jupiter-mass giants at 4–10 AU |

Disk instability, by contrast, is fast and violent. If a young disk is massive and cold enough, its own gravity overwhelms the forces holding it smooth, and it fragments into clumps that collapse into giants in thousands of years rather than millions. It naturally produces massive planets far from their stars, and it naturally produces eccentric orbits, because the fragments interact gravitationally as they form. Its weakness is the opposite one: it needs a disk heavy enough to be unstable, and not every disk qualifies; and because the collapse is so fast, the resulting planet should have a composition close to the disk's gas, without the strong heavy-element enrichment that core accretion predicts. That is why Kratter noted that the best evidence for core accretion in general is "the very substantial metal enrichment of gas giants, as evidenced by their high densities," and that density information "is really only accessible for systems that are close in, transit, and also have RV measurements." HIP 88962 b does not transit, and at 89 light-years it will not be weighed by any transit. Its density cannot be measured, so the composition test that would settle the argument is out of reach for this system — for now.

That is also why the eccentricity matters. A planet formed gently by core accretion, embedded in a gas disk that damps its motion, should end up on a near-circular orbit. An eccentricity of 0.718 is a scar. It suggests a violent history: a close encounter with another massive body, a scattering event that flung the planet onto its stretched path, or formation in a fragmenting disk where gravitational kicks were part of the process from the start. The leftover scatter in the velocity data — about six metres per second, twice the scatter of quiet comparison stars — hints that there may be more to find in this system. It could be stellar activity, and the star's doubled calcium emission and roughly 17-day rotation period support that reading. But it could also be a second planet, and the authors note that evidence of several more cold gas giants around K7 dwarfs awaits confirmation by future Magellan observations. If a sibling giant is hiding in the residuals, a past gravitational encounter between the two would be a natural explanation for the stretched orbit, and it would make the system a laboratory for how giant planets scatter each other.

## A Sixteen-Year Hunt: Timeline

The discovery was not a single night's work but a slow accumulation, and its chronology shows how long-period planet hunting actually proceeds — in years, not nights.

- **1997** — Alan Boss publishes the disk gravitational instability mechanism in *Science*, proposing that giant planets can form by direct collapse of a gas disk.
- **2010** — The Planet Finder Spectrograph begins monitoring HIP 88962 from the Magellan Clay Telescope at Las Campanas Observatory in Chile.
- **2021** — A survey using the CHIRON spectrograph observes HIP 88962 ten times over 799 days and finds no sign of a planet — a baseline too short to catch a 19-year wobble.
- **2021** — Burn and colleagues publish a core-accretion population study that produces essentially no planets heavier than about one Jupiter beyond one astronomical unit around 0.5–0.7 solar-mass stars.
- **2022** — Schlecker's study, using the same model, finds no massive gas giants with orbits longer than 1,000 days around such stars.
- **2023** — Sozzetti and colleagues report a 3.6 Jupiter-mass planet around the M dwarf GJ 463, an earlier case read as a possible disk-instability product.
- **2010–2025** — The Magellan team accumulates 47 sets of PFS observations on 41 nights, tracing the star's 94.9 metres-per-second wobble through most of one 19.3-year orbit.
- **11 November 2026** — The paper, "Magellan PFS Discovery of a Cold Gas Giant Exoplanet Likely Formed by a Gas Disk Gravitational Instability," is posted to arXiv.
- **17 November 2026** — The study is published in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*.
- **28 November 2026** — The discovery reaches the wider public as astronomers weigh its challenge to planet-formation theory.

The timeline carries a quiet lesson that runs through the whole story: the exoplanet census is biased toward the quick and the close. Transit surveys find planets that cross their stars often, and radial-velocity surveys find planets whose orbits repeat within the span of a typical observing programme. A planet with a 19-year orbit is nearly invisible to both. The 2021 CHIRON non-detection is the proof — a competent survey looked at the right star and found nothing, because it looked for two years at a planet that takes nineteen. How many HIP 88962 b-like worlds are hiding in existing data, their signals dismissed as slow drifts or stellar noise? The authors' answer is implicit in their observing strategy: keep watching. Sixteen years of data, covering less than a single orbit, was enough to find this one. The next ones will take just as long.

That patience is also a warning about the paper's own uncertainties. Because less than one full orbit has been observed, the period is not nailed down; alternative fits that include a long-term trend stretch it to more than twice as long. Continued monitoring should clarify the orbit, and the authors say so plainly. The star's mass, on which the whole argument depends, was not measured directly either. It was estimated from the star's colour through a temperature-to-mass relation, with an uncertainty of 0.084 solar masses — about 14 percent of the value. If the star were heavier than estimated, the planet's mass would rise with it, and the case against core accretion would shift. These are not flaws in the work; they are the ordinary limits of a measurement made at the edge of what patience can buy, and the paper states them.

## What Comes Next

The most decisive test of the disk-instability story may not come from HIP 88962 at all. Boss wrote that "one strong observational test for disk instability will be the results of the Roman microlensing survey … which may uncover large numbers of distant gas giants that core accretion fails to predict." The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, whose first images are expected in December 2026, will survey the crowded heart of the Milky Way by microlensing — watching for the brief brightening of a background star when a foreground planet's gravity bends its light. Microlensing is sensitive to exactly the planets other methods miss: cold, distant, and unbound from the need to transit or to tug their star on a human timescale. If Roman finds distant giants to be common, especially around low-mass stars, the core-accretion-only picture will be hard to sustain. If it finds them rare, HIP 88962 b will look more like a fluke, a system shaped by an unusual history rather than a common process.

Closer to home, the Magellan programme continues. The paper says continued monitoring should clarify the planet's orbit, and the hint of a second planet in the residuals gives the team a concrete reason to keep watching. The authors also note that evidence of several more cold gas giants around K7 dwarfs awaits confirmation by future Magellan observations — a sentence that reads, to anyone who follows this field, as a promise of more papers in the same vein. Each additional system will test whether HIP 88962 b is a one-off or the first recognised member of a population that the old models said should not exist.

There is a wider context, too, and it is the reason this discovery travelled beyond the journals within days. The same month, astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope recovered a hidden giant planet, <a href="/blog/51-eridani-b-flip-jwst-hidden-planet-november-2026/">51 Eridani b</a>, from two-year-old data with a new glare-removal technique, and other teams reported new long-period giants from archival radial-velocity data. Webb's studies of nearer worlds have been just as revealing: observations of <a href="/blog/toi-6478-b-atmosphere-november-2026/">TOI-6478 b</a> recorded the strongest sub-Neptune atmospheric signal yet, while <a href="/blog/ltt-1445-a-b-cosmic-shoreline-november-2026/">LTT 1445 A b</a>, our nearest rocky neighbour, turned out to have no air at all. The pattern is consistent: the inventory of giant planets is still growing, and it is growing at the edges — in old data, at long periods, around small stars. The telescopes already built hold planets we have not yet seen, and the methods to find them are catching up with the data.

HIP 88962 b will not be visited, photographed, or weighed. It is 89 light-years away, it does not transit, and its year is longer than a childhood. What it offers instead is a test — a single, stubborn data point that refuses to fit the standard model and therefore forces the model to change. Sixteen years of watching a small orange star wobble have produced a planet that should not exist, and the most interesting thing about it is not the planet itself. It is what its existence implies: that the universe builds giants in more than one way, that the census of worlds is still incomplete, and that patience — sixteen years of it, on 41 nights — remains the most powerful instrument in astronomy.
