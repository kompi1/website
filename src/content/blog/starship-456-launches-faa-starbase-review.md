SpaceX wants to launch Starship more than once a day. The federal government has now formally begun asking whether the South Texas coast can handle it.

On October 14, 2026, the Federal Aviation Administration published a Notice of Intent in the Federal Register announcing it will prepare a Programmatic Environmental Impact Statement — a full-scale environmental review — for SpaceX's proposal to expand Starship-Super Heavy operations at the company's Starbase site in Boca Chica, Texas. The filing, listed under docket FAA-2026-12808, lays out a request that would take the world's largest rocket from its current authorization of 25 launches and 50 landings a year to a maximum capacity of 456 launches and 912 landings annually. That is an eighteen-fold jump, and one of the most aggressive flight-rate requests any launch operator has ever put before American regulators.

Nothing about the October 14 notice approves the plan. It opens a review, not a green light. But it is the first procedural step toward something SpaceX has been telegraphing for years: transforming Starbase from a rocket test site into an operational spaceport closer in tempo to a commercial airport — the pace required, in the company's telling, to build a city on Mars, populate its Starlink mega-constellation, and land NASA astronauts on the Moon.

## What SpaceX is actually asking for

The proposal is structured in two phases, and the numbers matter precisely because they are upper bounds for environmental analysis, not a flight schedule.

Phase 1 would raise the ceiling to up to 100 launches and 200 landings per year — 100 Starships and 100 Super Heavy boosters — permitted on any day of the week and at any time of day or night. Phase 2 is the headline figure: up to 456 launches originating from the Boca Chica site per year and up to 912 landings there annually, with launches restricted to Monday through Friday but permitted at any hour. The FAA describes Phase 2 as something that would be implemented "within several years," contingent on the vehicle maturing and the environmental review itself.

A critical nuance: the 912-landing ceiling counts landings of vehicles regardless of where they launched from. SpaceX intends for Starbase to eventually receive returning vehicles that lifted off elsewhere — its planned Florida operations at Kennedy Space Center's Launch Complex 39A, for example — so the landing number is not simply the number of launches doubled. The figures bound the analysis; they do not describe expected traffic.

Against the current baseline, the scale of the ask becomes stark. Starbase is presently authorized for 25 orbital launches and 50 total landings a year — 25 Starships and 25 Super Heavies — under a 2025 tiered environmental assessment and mitigated finding of no significant impact. Phase 1 would quadruple the launch rate; Phase 2 would multiply it more than eighteen times over.

The filing also covers where the hardware comes down. Super Heavy boosters would return to land at the launch site, soft-land in the Gulf, or be expended in flight. Starship upper stages would land at the launch site, soft-land in the Gulf or in the open ocean, or be expended in the open ocean. The agency's notice also contemplates additional ocean landing and vehicle expenditure locations, subsequent license modifications or renewals, and the temporary airspace closures that accompany high-cadence operations.

## Why SpaceX needs this cadence

SpaceX's ambitions are not secret. Starship is the vehicle Elon Musk's company is building to carry out its three largest commitments: deploying tens of thousands of Starlink internet satellites, landing NASA astronauts on the Moon under the Artemis program, and ultimately sending people to Mars. Each of those missions consumes flight rate like fuel.

Consider Starlink. Investment bank Morgan Stanley, in a research note to investors on October 15, 2026, estimated that each Starship launch carrying Starlink satellites is equivalent to a month or more of Falcon 9 launches. After Starship Flight 14 successfully deployed 26 of the larger, heavier Starlink V3 satellites — each carrying roughly ten times the downlink capacity of the V2 Mini satellites now flying on Falcon 9 — the bank expects Flight 15 to repeat or exceed that load, with Starship eventually able to carry up to 60 per flight. At that scale, hundreds of Starship flights a year begin to look less like an exaggeration and more like the arithmetic of the business plan.

Consider the Moon. Starship is the vehicle behind the Artemis lunar lander contract, and each crewed landing is expected to require a string of tanker flights to refuel the lander in orbit. NASA's schedule currently points to an Artemis III orbital rehearsal no earlier than June 2027 and a landing attempt on Artemis IV early in 2028. Refueling that lander could demand dozens of Starship launches compressed into weeks. A site cleared for only 25 launches a year would make such a campaign a regulatory ordeal every single time.

And consider Mars. Musk has spoken for a decade about flying thousands of Starships to the Red Planet during the brief transfer windows that open every 26 months. Whether or not that vision arrives on his timeline, the flight rate is the architecture: Starship is designed for minimal post-flight refurbishment, with both stages returning to the launch tower to be caught by mechanical arms and flown again. A reusable vehicle that cannot fly often is a contradiction, and SpaceX is now asking regulators to bless the tempo its design was built for.

## What a programmatic EIS means

The choice of review vehicle matters. A Programmatic Environmental Impact Statement is broader than the site-specific Environmental Assessments the FAA has used for Starbase in the past. It evaluates a whole operating program — hundreds of launches and recoveries a year at the same coastal site — rather than analyzing one flight at a time. The shift to a PEIS reflects the scale of the question: what happens to a place when the largest flying vehicle in history comes and goes almost daily?

The FAA has opened public scoping, the phase where citizens, scientists, and stakeholders tell the agency which environmental concerns the study must examine. Comments are due by November 30, 2026, with public scoping meetings announced as part of the process. Only after scoping will a draft PEIS be written — and the PEIS process typically takes longer than a standard environmental assessment, meaning the path to any approval for the full 456-launch cadence is likely to stretch well into 2027 or beyond.

The areas the FAA expects to study read like a map of South Texas anxieties: noise, air quality, wildlife, coastal resources, visual effects, and what the notice calls "socioeconomics and children's health and safety risks." For its analysis, the agency will assume that half of all launches and landings happen during the day, defined as 7 a.m. to 10 p.m., and half at night, from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. That assumption matters enormously for residents near the site, because the night half is where a rocket that generates the acoustic power of a small volcano intersects with sleep, wildlife, and a tourism economy that sells dark skies.

## A decade of environmental fights at Boca Chica

Starbase did not arrive at this moment by accident, and it did not arrive quietly. The Boca Chica launch site sits on the narrow strip of coastal Texas between Brownsville and the Rio Grande, hemmed in by the Boca Chica State Park, the Las Palomas Wildlife Management Area, and the Lower Rio Grande Valley National Wildlife Refuge. It is a place of endangered ocelots and aplomado falcons, of sea turtles nesting on the Gulf sand, and of a beach whose closures during launch operations have angered environmental groups, local residents, and astronomers for years.

The site's environmental history is a saga of its own. An earlier programmatic environmental assessment, completed in 2022, allowed Starship's orbital test campaign to proceed with more than 75 mitigation conditions attached — measures covering everything from bird monitoring to limiting debris on the surrounding refuge. Each escalation of the program since has been fought over. When the FAA finalized its 2025 tiered assessment raising Starbase to 25 launches and 50 landings annually, it did so over objections that launch infrastructure, road closures, and the Starship tower's sheer footprint were already remaking a fragile coastal landscape.

Those fights explain why the PEIS matters and why it will take so long. A full environmental impact statement opens every assumption to litigation-grade scrutiny. Environmental organizations have repeatedly used the National Environmental Policy Act process to force disclosures about Starship's effects on wetlands, wildlife, and public access to the beach. A proposal for 456 launches a year — more than one a day on average, five days a week — is a target unlike anything those groups have had to confront before.

It also explains the peculiar details of the filing. The Monday-through-Friday restriction on Phase 2 launches is the kind of condition that suggests somebody — the company, the agency, or both — has been thinking about what it means to live next to an almost-daily rocket program. A beach community can, in principle, plan its weekends. What it cannot do is plan anything at all if the launch schedule never sleeps.

The filing's quietest detail may be its most telling for the region's waters. The proposal asks the FAA to evaluate not just launch-site landings but soft landings in the Gulf and the open ocean, plus additional ocean landing and vehicle expenditure locations — bureaucratic language for places where rocket stages come down on purpose, or where they are deliberately discarded. Each Gulf splashdown is an event with its own logistics: recovery ships, temporary closure of marine areas, and the acoustic signature of a booster decelerating into the water. For a coast whose economy runs on fishing, shrimping, and beach tourism, the environmental statement's treatment of the water may matter as much as its treatment of the sky.

## The "No Action" alternative — and why Boca Chica stays the site

Every environmental impact statement must consider alternatives to the proposal, including the option of doing nothing. Here the FAA's notice lists only one alternative to the proposed expansion: "No Action," which would keep SpaceX at the 25-launch, 50-landing cadence authorized under the 2025 assessment. The notice explicitly says that other launch locations are not reasonable alternatives because Starbase already has the infrastructure — the towers, the propellant farms, the integration facilities, the roads, the workforce, the literal company town.

That single sentence is doing a lot of work. Starbase is not merely a launch pad; it is the factory that builds the rockets. The Starfactory production facility, the launch towers, the test stands, and the shipyard of barges and tankers form an integrated manufacturing complex that SpaceX has spent a decade building. Moving Starship operations elsewhere to dodge environmental constraints would mean rebuilding the industrial base itself — a prospect the company, with 14 test flights behind it and a Mars architecture ahead of it, has no interest in entertaining.

The company has, of course, been building a second home. Construction at Kennedy Space Center's Launch Complex 39A in Florida — the same pad that sent Apollo astronauts to the Moon and hosted three decades of Space Shuttle missions — has been underway for years. Reporting in October 2026 has floated December 10 as a possible target for the pad's debut Starship flight, though the company itself has not confirmed a date and its own published updates still describe Florida operations as an ambition whose original timeline has slipped. When 39A does come online, Starbase's role as a receiving site for vehicles launched elsewhere — the reason the landing ceiling is so much higher than the launch ceiling — becomes central to the whole architecture.

## The economics of almost-daily launches

Strip away the rockets and what remains is an industrial planning problem. Morgan Stanley's October 2026 research note put the logic in investor language: each Starship Starlink launch equals a month or more of Falcon 9 launches, and with up to 60 satellites per flight possible, the vehicle "could profoundly evolve investor expectations about SpaceX's role in converting energy into intelligence at scale." The bank maintained its $300 share price target for the company — shares were trading around $163 — on the premise that launch cost falling by another order of magnitude, combined with mass to orbit increasing by a similar factor, creates markets that do not currently exist.

Whether or not one buys the bank's framing, the flight economics are what make 456 launches plausible. Starship and Super Heavy are designed to require minimal refurbishment between flights — no engine teardown, no months in a hangar — because at hundreds of flights a year, there is no hangar big enough. The FAA's notice quotes the proposal's own premise: "As the vehicle continues to mature and reliability improves, launch and landing rates are expected to increase." The reliability curve is the schedule.

There is a precedent for skepticism here. Every one of SpaceX's past cadence projections — for Falcon 9, for the original Starship test campaign — has arrived later and messier than promised. The gap between "authorized to launch 456 times" and "launching 456 times" is the gap between a regulatory ceiling and an operational reality that requires a mature, reusable vehicle flying without incident week after week. The filing is an environmental analysis of a maximum, not a promise of a minimum. Treating it as a flight schedule would be a category error.

## What happens next

The timeline now belongs to the environmental process. Public scoping runs through November 30, 2026, and the meetings and comments will shape what the draft PEIS studies. After the draft comes more public comment, a final document, and then — and only then — a decision on whether, and under what conditions, the higher cadence is authorized. Every phase of that sequence can be litigated, and at Starbase, litigation has been a constant companion.

Phase 1, the 100-launch step, is the nearer-term question: a quadrupling of today's authorization that SpaceX could plausibly use within the next year or two as Starlink deployments, the Florida campaign, and the Artemis refueling architecture demand more flights. Phase 2 — the 456 number that has generated the headlines — is described as arriving "within several years," which in launch-industry terms means sometime in the early 2030s at the earliest, and only if everything about the vehicle, the business, and the environment cooperates.

What is certain is the direction of travel. The world's most active launch company has told the government, in writing, that its future requires one of its sites to fly more than once a day. The government has agreed to study whether that is possible. Between those two facts lies every hard question about the century ahead: how much of the sky we are willing to fill, how much noise we are willing to make, and how many wild places we are willing to trade for the speed to leave this planet.

The answers will take years. The launches, meanwhile, will continue — 25 a year for now, under the old ceiling, while the paper for the new one piles up in the Federal Register. That is how the space age advances in 2026: one docket number at a time.
