Some of the biggest black holes in the universe may have been born in the smallest nurseries. New research based on observations from NASA's James Webb Space Telescope argues that the dense young star clusters inside two of the earliest galaxies ever seen — the Firefly Sparkle and the Cosmic Gems arc — were the ideal factories for intermediate-mass black holes, the long-sought "heavy seeds" that could grow into the supermassive black holes anchoring galaxies today. The study, led by Viola Bocchi of Sapienza University of Rome and published in the journal *Astronomy and Astrophysics*, does not claim that Webb actually spotted any of these black holes. Instead, it uses the clusters Webb did see to calculate what should have formed inside them — and the numbers land squarely in the heavy-seed regime.

That matters because astronomers have a timing problem. Supermassive black holes weighing billions of times the mass of the Sun already existed when the universe was less than a billion years old, and nobody fully understands how they grew so large so fast. If they started as the collapsed cores of ordinary massive stars, they would have needed to feed at or above the maximum sustainable rate for nearly their entire lives, with almost no interruptions — a demanding diet that strains belief. Heavy seeds change the arithmetic. Start with a black hole of a few thousand solar masses instead of a few dozen, and the path to a billion solar masses becomes far less extreme. The trouble has always been finding a place where such heavy seeds could actually form. Bocchi's team says Webb has now shown us two of them, glowing in the light of the universe's first few hundred million years.

## The Two Galaxies That Lit Up the Early Universe

The Cosmic Gems arc and the Firefly Sparkle are both gravitationally lensed galaxies, which means their light has been bent and magnified by the gravity of a massive foreground galaxy cluster on its way to Earth. Lensing acts like a natural telescope stacked on top of a telescope, stretching these tiny, distant objects into arcs and brightening them enough for Webb to resolve structures inside them that would otherwise blur into a single point of light. Without that cosmic magnifying glass, the star clusters at the heart of this research would have been invisible.

The Cosmic Gems arc sits at a redshift of about 10.2, which means Webb is seeing it as it was when the universe was only about 460 million years old — roughly three percent of its current age. The arc was first discovered by the Hubble Space Telescope during its Reionization Lensing Cluster Survey, known as RELICS, and was recognised as a powerful source of radiation during the epoch of reionisation, the era when the first stars and galaxies burned away the fog of neutral hydrogen that filled the young universe. In 2024, Webb took a closer look and found five young massive clusters inside it — the first discovery of such ancient clusters. "The James Webb Space Telescope has revealed a population of dense stellar systems at high redshifts, including the 'Cosmic Gems' arc and the 'Firefly Sparkle'," the authors write, noting that with masses in the range of one hundred thousand to ten million solar masses and half-mass radii of roughly 0.4 to 15 parsecs, "these systems are ideally suited to form intermediate-mass black holes via collision-based models."

The Firefly Sparkle is a second lensed early galaxy, imaged only about 600 million years after the Big Bang, at a redshift of about 8.3. Webb found ten young massive clusters in the Firefly Sparkle, twice the haul of the Cosmic Gems arc. An artist's illustration based on Webb's data imagines the Firefly Sparkle as it would have looked without the stretching and distortion of gravitational lensing: a compact, glittering collection of brilliant young star clusters, accompanied by a pair of companion galaxies. The name is apt. In the telescope's images, the galaxy appears as a sprinkling of bright points, like sparks thrown off a fire, each one a cluster containing enormous numbers of stars packed into a tiny volume.

Both galaxies are more than curiosities. They are snapshots of galaxy assembly in progress, taken at a moment when the universe was still young enough that the first generations of stars were being born, living fast, and dying young. The clusters inside them are not like the globular clusters orbiting the Milky Way today, which are ancient and settled. These are young massive clusters caught in the act of forming, dense with hot, massive stars and almost entirely lacking in heavy elements. That combination — extreme density plus low metallicity — is exactly what the new study says makes them such promising black hole factories.

## How Runaway Collisions Build a Black Hole

The mechanism at the centre of the paper is the runaway collision. In an ordinary star cluster, stars occasionally pass close to one another, and very rarely two of them collide and merge. In a cluster that is dense enough, the odds change dramatically. A massive star near the centre of the cluster sinks inward, pulled by gravity, and begins to sweep up neighbours. Each merger makes it bigger and heavier, which increases its gravitational pull, which makes further collisions more likely. The process feeds on itself, and over a few million years a single object can grow to hundreds or even thousands of times the mass of the Sun. When that bloated star finally collapses, it leaves behind a black hole far heavier than any single star could have produced on its own.

That is the theory, but testing it requires knowing the density and structure of real clusters in the early universe — and until Webb, astronomers had almost no such clusters to study. The new research applies a model based on runaway collisions and mass loss by stellar winds to the fifteen clusters Webb found in the two galaxies, estimating the mass of the black hole each cluster should have produced. Because the clusters are so dense, the standard tool for this kind of calculation — direct N-body simulation, which tracks the gravitational dance of every star individually — was not viable. The researchers instead used an analytic model and then validated its results against direct N-body simulations of compact star clusters, checking that the shortcut did not distort the answer.

The headline numbers are striking. "Our estimates yield typical IMBH masses in the range of about 100 to about 4,000 solar masses, implying typical formation efficiencies at the few percent level," the authors write. In other words, a few percent of each cluster's total mass could have ended up in a single central black hole — an efficient conversion, but one that still leaves the vast majority of the cluster's stars untouched. The range matters because it straddles the boundary between the two ways astronomers think black hole seeds form. At the low end, around one hundred solar masses, the seeds are comparable to the black holes left behind by the universe's hypothetical first stars, the so-called Population III stars, which were likely extremely massive. At the high end, around a few thousand solar masses, the objects sit firmly in what researchers call the "heavy seed" regime — heavy enough to take a real shortcut toward supermassive status.

To understand why the result is significant, it helps to place intermediate-mass black holes on the map. Stellar-mass black holes, the kind left behind when massive stars die, typically weigh between a few and a few dozen solar masses. Two processes create them: massive stars between about 8 and 50 solar masses either explode as supernovae, with their cores collapsing into black holes, or collapse directly; and pairs of neutron stars can merge into a black hole. Supermassive black holes, by contrast, weigh millions or billions of solar masses and sit at the centres of galaxies, including the Milky Way. Between those two populations lies the intermediate-mass range, roughly 100 to 100,000 solar masses, and the observational evidence for black holes in that range is still thin, though persistent.

The evidence that does exist has been accumulating. A 2024 paper focused on Omega Centauri, a globular cluster on the outskirts of the Milky Way, arguing that a group of fast-moving stars in its centre can only be explained by an intermediate-mass black hole of about 8,200 solar masses. Gravitational waves have provided another line of evidence: a 2020 paper presented what remains the only direct evidence of an intermediate-mass black hole, when the ripples showed two black holes of 85 and 66 solar masses merging to create one of about 142 solar masses. What has been missing is a clear picture of where such objects are born — and whether the early universe made them in sufficient numbers to seed the supermassive black holes Webb keeps finding at high redshift. The new study offers a candidate birthplace, and it is one Webb can actually see.

## Why the Cosmic Gems Clusters Win the Mass Race

Not all fifteen clusters are equal, and the differences between the two galaxies are where the story gets interesting. The clusters in the Cosmic Gems arc produced the heaviest black holes in the study, with masses ranging from 1,600 to 2,700 solar masses. The reason is their extreme compactness. "The extreme compactness of the Cosmic Gems clusters, with a half-mass radius of about 1 parsec, facilitates the formation of black hole seeds with high masses of 1,600 to 2,700 solar masses," the authors write. The half-mass radius is the radius of the sphere that contains half of a cluster's total mass, and it measures how tightly packed the cluster is. At about one parsec — roughly 3.26 light-years — the Cosmic Gems clusters cram hundreds of thousands to millions of solar masses of stars into a volume a few light-years across. In such a crush, collisions are not rare accidents but a regular feature of cluster life, and the runaway process can proceed to its full conclusion.

Low metallicity is the second ingredient, and it may be just as important. "The low metallicity, at most about two percent of the Sun's, is a critical factor for retaining the seed mass despite stellar winds," the authors write. Metallicity, in astronomy, means the abundance of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium. In the early universe, few stars had lived and died, so few heavy elements existed, and the clusters in these galaxies are correspondingly metal-poor. That matters because massive stars lose mass through powerful stellar winds, and those winds are driven by heavy elements in the star's atmosphere absorbing radiation. With almost no metals, the winds are weak, the growing central star keeps almost all of the mass it gains through collisions, and the eventual black hole is far heavier than it would be in a metal-rich environment like the present-day Milky Way.

The combination explains why the Cosmic Gems clusters outperform their Firefly Sparkle counterparts. Compactness sets the collision rate, and low metallicity sets how much of the collided mass survives. Both galaxies' clusters are metal-poor, but only the Cosmic Gems clusters are compact enough to push the runaway process into the thousands-of-solar-masses range. It is a neat illustration of how the physics of black hole formation depends on the environment in which stars are born — and how the early universe, with its dense, pristine clusters, was a far more favourable place to build heavy black hole seeds than the universe is today.

The broader context makes the result even more intriguing. Astronomers think the supermassive black holes seen in the modern universe grew through a long, drawn-out process known as the hierarchical black hole merger framework, in which smaller black holes merge over cosmic time and grow by feeding on surrounding gas. Some of the most massive supermassive black holes appear to weigh billions of solar masses. Starting from seeds of a few thousand solar masses, as the Cosmic Gems clusters could provide, eases the burden on that framework enormously: the growth required is still large, but it no longer demands near-perfect feeding efficiency across billions of years. And the connection reaches beyond these two galaxies. Webb has repeatedly found surprisingly massive black holes in the early universe, including the enigmatic "little red dots" — compact, red objects whose spectra are often interpreted as being powered by supermassive black holes. "Our results suggest that dense massive star clusters could provide possible massive seeds from which the supermassive black holes in the little red dots may have grown," the researchers write.

## Firefly Sparkle: Smaller Seeds, Same Story

The Firefly Sparkle's ten clusters tell a different, but complementary, story. "In contrast to the Cosmic Gems, these clusters exhibit significantly larger radii, with half-mass radii of about 4 to 6 parsecs, for typically lower total masses of one hundred thousand to one million solar masses," the authors write. Larger and less massive means less dense, and less dense means fewer collisions. The cores that form are less massive, limiting the potential for the runaway collision process to create the heaviest black holes. The black hole masses the team estimates for the Firefly Sparkle range from about 180 to 810 solar masses — the lower boundary of the intermediate-mass range, but still well above what any single star could leave behind.

That lower range is significant in its own right. As the authors note, the lower bound of their estimates, around one hundred solar masses, is comparable to the black holes that would be formed by massive Population III stars, the universe's first stars. Population III stars are hypothetical in the sense that none has ever been observed directly, but theory predicts they were extremely massive, lived short lives, and collapsed into black holes of roughly one hundred solar masses. If the Firefly Sparkle clusters were producing seeds of that size, they would be an alternative route to the same starting point — one that does not require the first stars to have been quite so massive or quite so efficient at leaving black holes behind. But the upper bound of the study's estimates, the team stresses, "extends significantly higher, allowing the most massive clusters to produce objects firmly in the 'heavy seed' regime." The overall picture is a spectrum: the same process, operating in clusters of different densities, produces seeds ranging from a hundred solar masses to a few thousand. The universe, it seems, had more than one way to get the black hole growth story started.

There is also a lesson in the contrast between the two galaxies about what Webb is actually measuring. The clusters in both galaxies are sources of ionising radiation, the energetic ultraviolet light that helped clear the fog of the early universe during reionisation. The new research shows that not only were all these young massive clusters sources of ionising radiation, but they may also have been the birthplaces of intermediate-mass black holes. A single population of objects, in other words, may have done double duty in the early universe: lighting it up and seeding its largest black holes. That dual role makes young massive clusters a particularly attractive target for future observations. If the same clusters that reionised the universe also seeded its black holes, then studying them tells astronomers about two of the biggest open questions in cosmology at once.

The timing of the observations adds to the significance. Webb's look at the Cosmic Gems arc in 2024 was the first discovery of such ancient clusters, and the Firefly Sparkle observations followed in the same wave of early-universe discoveries that has defined the telescope's first years of science. Every time Webb points at a lensed galaxy from the first billion years, it seems to find structure that was invisible to Hubble: clusters, clumps, and knots of star formation that resolve into individual building blocks of galaxies. The fifteen clusters in this study are only the beginning. As Webb and future telescopes survey more lensed galaxies, the sample of young massive clusters from the early universe will grow, and with it the ability to test whether the runaway collision story holds up across a wider range of environments.

## A Possible Answer for Webb's Little Red Dots

One of the most talked-about puzzles of the Webb era may also be connected to these clusters. Since the telescope began science operations, astronomers have been puzzling over the "little red dots": compact, red objects seen in the early universe whose spectra are often interpreted as being powered by supermassive black holes. They are far more numerous than expected, and their apparent black holes seem too massive for their host galaxies, challenging models of how black holes and galaxies grow together. Several explanations have been proposed, including that the dots are something else entirely, but the leading interpretation remains that they are young galaxies hosting actively feeding black holes.

The new research offers a pathway. If dense young massive clusters were churning out heavy black hole seeds in the first few hundred million years, then the little red dots could be the galaxies where those seeds had already begun to grow, fed by the gas-rich environments of their young hosts. "Our results suggest that dense massive star clusters could provide possible massive seeds from which the supermassive black holes in the little red dots may have grown," the researchers write. It is not a proof — the study does not observe the little red dots themselves, and the seeds it predicts are inferred, not detected — but it is a concrete, testable link between two of Webb's most surprising discoveries. If the connection is real, then the little red dots are not a problem for black hole growth models, but a confirmation of them: the seeds were simply heavier, and earlier, than anyone had assumed.

The idea also fits with a separate line of Webb research reported this week, in which Johns Hopkins researchers tested whether one specific little red dot, called QSO1, could contain a primordial black hole — a theoretical object that predates the existence of stars. QSO1 existed just 700 million years after the Big Bang and is almost entirely made of hydrogen and helium, the first elements that formed during the beginning of the universe. The two studies approach the same mystery from opposite directions: one asks whether the first black holes were born before stars, the other asks whether they were born inside the first star clusters. Both may be partly right, and both underscore how much of the early black hole story remains to be written. What the Firefly Sparkle and Cosmic Gems work adds is a mechanism rooted in objects Webb has actually observed, with masses that can be calculated from measured cluster properties rather than assumed from theory alone.

For context, Webb's early-universe haul has been rewriting the timeline of cosmic history almost since the telescope's first images. <a href="/blog/webb-earliest-supernova-grb-250314a-730-million-years/">Webb's earliest supernova, GRB 250314A, seen just 730 million years after the Big Bang</a>, showed that massive stars were already dying explosively in the universe's infancy. <a href="/blog/jwst-three-black-holes-j0148-4214/">Webb's discovery of three black holes in the system J0148-4214</a> revealed that black hole mergers were already under way at high redshift. And <a href="/blog/3c-120-black-hole-jet-2026-xrism-hot-inner-flow/">the 3C 120 black hole jet studied by XRISM</a> showed how the hot inner flows around black holes shape the galaxies they inhabit. Each of these findings adds a piece to the same puzzle: the first billion years were far more active, and far more structured, than pre-Webb models predicted.

## What the Model Cannot Yet Prove

The researchers are candid about the limits of their work, and those limits matter. The study's results are estimates, not observations. Webb did not observe any intermediate-mass black holes in the Firefly Sparkle or the Cosmic Gems arc; it observed the clusters, and the team calculated what should have formed inside them. Many properties of these clusters remain unknown, including their rotation and their internal structure, and both could change the outcomes and lower the estimated black hole masses. A rapidly rotating cluster, for instance, would resist the central concentration that drives runaway collisions, while a different internal arrangement of stars could slow the process.

The initial mass function — the distribution of stellar masses with which a cluster is born — is also unknown, and it could be top-heavy, meaning the cluster started with a larger proportion of massive stars than a typical cluster today. "This possibility, along with a possible primordial mass segregation, would favour the formation of massive intermediate-mass black holes, though we did not explore it here in detail," the authors explain. Primordial mass segregation, in which the heaviest stars are born already concentrated toward the centre of a cluster, would accelerate the runaway process from the start. If either effect is real, the team's estimates may be conservative rather than optimistic — but until the initial mass function of these ancient clusters can be measured, the uncertainty cuts both ways.

There is also the question of survival. Even if a heavy seed formed inside a young massive cluster, it would have to survive the cluster's later evolution, the death of its most massive stars, and the eventual assembly of the cluster into a larger galaxy, all while growing by feeding on gas and merging with other black holes. The study addresses the formation of the seeds, not their subsequent billion-year journey to supermassive status. That journey remains the domain of simulations and of observations of black holes at intermediate redshifts, where Webb and other telescopes are beginning to fill in the timeline. The seeds are a beginning, not a complete story.

Those caveats do not diminish the result; they define the next steps. Researchers will dig deeper into these young massive clusters and try to fill in the blanks that remain, and the sample will grow as Webb observes more lensed galaxies from the same era. Future instruments may even be able to detect the seeds directly: a heavy seed black hole feeding on gas would glow in X-rays, and the next generation of X-ray observatories could search for that glow in the very clusters Webb has mapped. Gravitational wave detectors, too, could one day hear the mergers of intermediate-mass black holes, providing the direct evidence that has so far been limited to a single event. For now, the study gives astronomers something they did not have before: a specific, observable population of objects, with measured masses and sizes, that can plausibly produce heavy black hole seeds.

## Why This Matters for the First Billion Years

The first billion years of the universe were a construction site. Galaxies were assembling from smaller pieces, the first stars were igniting and exploding, the fog of neutral hydrogen was lifting, and at the centres of the growing galaxies, black holes were beginning their long climb toward supermassive status. For years, the story of that climb had a gap at its start: the seeds were assumed, not observed, and the assumption that they were light — the collapsed cores of single stars — made the speed of the climb hard to explain. The Firefly Sparkle and Cosmic Gems research fills that gap with something concrete. It says the seeds were heavy, that they formed inside objects Webb can see and measure, and that the process that made them — runaway collisions in dense, metal-poor star clusters — is a natural consequence of how stars formed in the early universe.

"Our results suggest that these dense, metal-poor clusters are viable factories for heavy seeds that are capable of growing into the supermassive black holes observed in the early universe," the authors conclude. It is a careful claim, hedged in the language of plausibility rather than proof, but it reframes the question. Instead of asking how light seeds grew impossibly fast, astronomers can now ask how heavy seeds grew at a reasonable pace — and they can look for the answer in the clusters Webb is already finding. The little red dots, the surprisingly massive early black holes, the rapid appearance of quasars in the first billion years: all of them become easier to understand if the universe started with seeds of thousands of solar masses rather than dozens.

There is a broader lesson, too, about what the Webb era has done to astronomy. Before the telescope launched, the first billion years were largely a theoretical realm, modelled in simulations and glimpsed in the faintest smudges of Hubble images. Now they are an observational science, populated by named galaxies with measured properties: the Firefly Sparkle, with its ten glittering clusters; the Cosmic Gems arc, with its five ultra-compact ones; and more arriving with every deep survey. Each new object narrows the space in which theories can hide, and each new measurement turns a speculation into a calculation. The heavy-seed story is one such calculation, and it points to a universe that was building its largest structures from the very beginning — not gradually, from tiny beginnings, but swiftly, in dense and brilliant clusters that lit up the dark.

The next time Webb points at a lensed galaxy from the dawn of time, astronomers will be looking at its star clusters with a new question in mind — not just how many stars they contain, or how much light they emit, but what is growing quietly at their centres. Somewhere in those glittering points of light, if Bocchi and her colleagues are right, the ancestors of the largest black holes in the universe were taking shape: heavy seeds, born in collision and darkness, waiting for the billion years of feeding and merging that would turn them into the giants that anchor the galaxies we see today. The universe, it turns out, did not start small. It started dense, and bright, and in a hurry — and we are only now learning to read the sparks it left behind.
