On the night of Tuesday, November 17, Earth will drive head-on into a river of comet dust, and the sky will answer with the fastest meteors of the year. The Leonids of 2026 will not be a storm — no one who studies the shower expects one — but they will be something rarer in its own way: a clean, dark, honest display from the most storied meteor shower in astronomy, arriving sixty years to the month after the most violent Leonid storm ever counted.

A Leonid is a grain of dust smaller than a grain of sand, shed by a comet that last visited the inner solar system in 1998. It meets Earth almost head-on at about 71 kilometres per second, burns up some 80 to 100 kilometres overhead, and in its last fraction of a second can outshine the stars around it, sometimes leaving a glowing train that hangs in the sky for minutes. Under a dark sky at the peak, an observer can expect about ten of them an hour — one every few minutes, each one a small piece of a comet's history arriving at 71 kilometres a second.

That modest rate is the whole point of this year's story. The Leonids are famous precisely because they sometimes do the impossible: in 1833 they fell like snow over North America and turned meteors from weather into astronomy; in 1966 they fell at forty a second over Arizona and left seasoned observers speechless. In 2026 they will do neither. What they will do is arrive on schedule, under a Moon that gets out of the way at midnight, with their radiant climbing high into a fully dark sky before dawn. For anyone willing to set an alarm and step outside, that is more than enough.

## When to Watch: The Night of 17–18 November

The International Meteor Organization's 2026 calendar puts the Leonid maximum on November 17 at about 23:45 UTC — 06:45 WIB on Wednesday, November 18, for observers in Indonesia. The shower is active from November 6 to November 30, but the night of November 17 into the pre-dawn hours of November 18 is the peak, and the mornings immediately before and after it are the backups. The American Meteor Society's calendar names the night of November 16–17 as its peak night instead, a reminder that the shower's maximum is broad rather than a single sharp minute; the practical advice is the same either way, and it is to treat both pre-dawn mornings as prime, with the night of the 17th first among them.

One detail matters for anyone reading the clock literally. The predicted maximum at 23:45 UTC falls in the early evening for North America — before the constellation Leo has even risen — so few meteors are visible at the exact moment of maximum from the United States. What counts is not the clock time of the peak but the hours when the radiant is high, and those come after midnight everywhere. An alternative model by the Russian meteor researcher Mikhail Maslov places the gentle maximum of the shower's background dust about eight hours earlier, at about 16:00 UTC on November 17. The two predictions disagree by hours, and the shower is broad enough that the difference hardly matters: the night of November 17–18 is the night, wherever you are.

The expected rate is about 15 meteors per hour under ideal conditions — the zenithal hourly rate, or ZHR, which counts what a single observer would see with the radiant directly overhead under a perfectly dark sky. Nobody actually gets those conditions, so the realistic numbers are lower: modelling for a dark site before dawn gives about ten meteors an hour under a very dark sky, three to four from countryside near a town, and one or two from the edge of a city, with perhaps fifty per cent uncertainty either way. From a bright city centre, the count may be lower still. Cloud is the bigger risk in mid-November at northern latitudes, so the forecast deserves a check — and the following night, November 18–19, is worth keeping in reserve even though activity by then will have fallen to about a quarter of its peak. One model even predicts a small extra sprinkle at about 02:50 UTC on November 19, from dust the comet shed in 1699.

## The Moon Cooperates This Time

Moonlight is the great enemy of meteor watching, and in 2026 it is an unusually forgiving one. The Moon reaches first quarter on the morning of November 17 — 11:48 UTC, according to NASA's catalogue of Moon phases — and is a little over half lit, about 55 per cent, across the peak nights. But a first-quarter Moon sets early. In the northern mid-latitudes it sets in the late evening, around midnight, just as Leo is climbing out of the east. In the southern hemisphere it sets an hour or two after midnight. Either way, the hours that matter most — the last dark hours before dawn, when the radiant stands highest — are moonless.

That timing is what makes this a good year to bother, even at a modest rate. Many showers peak under a bright Moon that washes out all but the brightest meteors; the Leonids of 2026 peak with the Moon already below the horizon for the whole of the prime window. The advice from every guide is the same: do not bother looking at nine in the evening. Set an alarm, wait for the Moon to set, and go out after midnight, when the sky is dark and Leo is high.

## Why the Leonids Are the Fastest Meteors in the Sky

At about 71 kilometres per second — roughly 255,000 kilometres per hour — the Leonids are the fastest meteors in the International Meteor Organization's whole list of showers. A Leonid covers a hundred kilometres in under a second and a half. The reason is geometry, and it is unusually clean: the comet's dust meets Earth almost head-on.

Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle travels around the Sun in the opposite direction to the planets, on an orbit tilted 162.5 degrees — so far over that astronomers call it retrograde. On the night of the maximum, Earth moves along its orbit at about 30 kilometres per second, while a grain of the comet's dust at the same distance from the Sun moves at about 41 kilometres per second in nearly the opposite direction. Because the two meet almost head-on, their speeds roughly add up: about 71 kilometres per second. A Leonid is, in the most literal sense, a head-on collision.

That speed explains two things about the shower. The first is that it belongs to the morning: in the hours after midnight you stand on Earth's leading side, the side driving forward along its orbit, and the Leonid radiant lies within about ten degrees of the point Earth is heading toward. The radiant rises late in the evening and stands highest in the last dark hours precisely because it sits almost exactly in Earth's path. The second is that each meteor carries an extraordinary amount of energy for its size. The particles are tiny, most of them smaller than a grain of sand, but at this speed each kilogram of dust carries about 2.5 gigajoules of energy — some 600 times as much per kilogram as TNT, and about four times as much per kilogram as a Geminid arriving at 35 kilometres per second. All of that energy is spent high in the atmosphere, and the streak you see is that energy being released. The brightest Leonids leave faint glowing trains, often blue-green, that can hang in the sky for minutes and change shape in the high-altitude winds.

## Where to Look: Find the Sickle, Then Look Away

The radiant — the point in the sky from which the meteors appear to fly out — lies in the head of Leo the Lion, in a curve of stars shaped like a backwards question mark and known as the Sickle. The shower is named after Leo, and the Sickle is also how you tell a Leonid from a sporadic meteor, one that belongs to no shower at all: trace a meteor's streak backwards, and if it leads to the Sickle, it was a Leonid. The radiant sits near Regulus, Leo's brightest star, about a fist's width away at arm's length.

But you do not stare at the radiant to see the shower. A meteor near the radiant is coming almost straight at you, so its streak is short; a meteor appearing further from the radiant is foreshortened less and draws a longer line across the sky. NASA's own advice is to look away from the radiant for that reason. In the northern hemisphere, face east or south-east, so that the radiant is in your part of the sky, but do not fix your gaze on it; lie back, look up, and let your eyes take in as much of the sky as they can. Give them up to half an hour to adapt to the dark first — that adaptation time is not optional, and a phone screen will undo it in a second.

Leo rises in the east late in the evening — between about 10:20 and 11:10 p.m. in the United States and Europe — and climbs all night. At 59 degrees north, the radiant stands about 28 degrees up a couple of hours after midnight, about 43 degrees two hours later, and about 51 degrees when astronomical dawn begins. The best hours, at almost any northern latitude, are the last three before dawn: the radiant is high, the Moon is long gone, and the sky is still fully dark.

The shower can be seen from both hemispheres, but not equally. The radiant lies 22 degrees north of the celestial equator, so it climbs far higher in the north. From the latitude of Sydney, Cape Town, Buenos Aires or Santiago, about 34 degrees south, it can get no higher than about 34 degrees, and it would reach that only after sunrise, in daylight; when astronomical dawn begins there, it stands only about 17 to 19 degrees above the horizon. Southern observers will still see Leonids, but fewer, in a shorter dark window with the radiant low. South of 68 degrees south, the radiant never rises at all.

## A Timeline of the Great Leonid Storms

The Leonids are not one of the year's richest showers. They are famous because, every few decades, they become the richest display in the sky. The table below is the shower's record — the nights that made it legend, and the long quiet stretches between them.

## 1833: The Night Meteor Science Began

People had seen Leonid storms long before anyone understood them. Astronomers in China and observers in what is now Egypt and Italy recorded one in the year 902. In 1799, Alexander von Humboldt and Aimé Bonpland watched one from Cumaná in Venezuela: Humboldt wrote of thousands of fireballs and shooting stars falling one after another for four hours, and Bonpland said there was not a patch of sky three Moon-widths across that was not filled with meteors.

Then came the night of November 12–13, 1833. Over eastern North America, the Leonids fell in numbers nobody alive had seen. In Boston the meteors were reckoned to fall at about half the rate of snowflakes in an ordinary snowstorm. From the rate as the storm died down, one contemporary estimate put the total at 240,000 meteors visible in nine hours — an estimate, not a count, but it puts the rate at tens of thousands an hour. A man in Washington wrote in his diary that it frightened the people half to death. Churches filled; enslaved people in the American South, who had no other calendar to mark it by, remembered it for generations as the night the stars fell.

Denison Olmsted, a professor at Yale, noticed something that made the night matter for science. The meteors were not falling at random: they all streamed out of one point in Leo, and as the night went on that point moved across the sky with the stars. Anything in the atmosphere would have stayed put relative to the ground and gone round with Earth. Something that kept pace with the stars had to lie beyond the atmosphere, out in space. Olmsted gathered reports from observers in America and Europe and set out the argument in the American Journal of Science in 1834. Before that night, astronomers had thought meteors were atmospheric phenomena, like rain or snow. After it, they were astronomy.

## 1966: Forty Meteors a Second

November 2026 marks the sixtieth anniversary of the most violent Leonid storm ever counted, and it is worth pausing on what that night was like — because no one who sees the 2026 shower should mistake a quiet year for a dull shower.

On November 17, 1966, fortunate observers in North America witnessed 40 to 50 meteors streaking across the sky every second. The display continued for about fifteen minutes, and a later analysis of radar records found that the core of the storm lasted less than half an hour — the rate stayed above half its peak for 23 minutes. At Kitt Peak in Arizona, a team led by Dennis Milon counted 33 Leonids in their first hour; about three and a half hours after they had started counting, at 11:54 UTC, the rate hit 40 meteors a second and stayed near that for about twenty minutes. At Table Mountain Observatory in California, observer Jim Young reported a peak of about 50 meteors per second and recorded 22 bright fireballs in 90 minutes. How that night should be expressed as a zenithal hourly rate is still argued over, and estimates differ by a factor of ten; what is not in doubt is what was counted on the mountain.

Later orbital calculations linked the 1966 storm to a dense trail of particles released by Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle during its 1899 return. There is a quiet irony in that: 1899 was the year everyone expected a storm and none came — scientists in Paris even sent up a balloon carrying Dorothea Klumpke, the first astronomer to watch for meteors from the air. The storm never came, but the comet's 1899 passage laid down the dust that gave the storms of both 1966 and 1999. The dust behind the great storm of 1966 left the comet in the very year that had disappointed everyone.

The 1866 storm over Europe had already taught astronomers the shower's clock. In the early 1860s, Hubert Anson Newton at Yale used accounts of earlier Leonid displays to predict that another storm would occur in 1866 — and the storm returned as expected, before the parent comet had even been found. Ernst Tempel discovered the comet in 1865, and Horace Tuttle independently discovered it in 1866. Within months, astronomers saw that the Leonid stream followed the same path as the comet, and in 1867 the connection was established. Along with the Perseids, the Leonids became one of the first two meteor showers ever tied to a particular comet.

## 1998–2002: Predictions, Fireballs, and Flashes on the Moon

By the late 1990s the worry was not for observers but for spacecraft. Ahead of the 1998 shower, controllers turned the Hubble Space Telescope so that its back faced the stream for ten hours, with its solar panels edge-on to it, although they put the chance of a hit at less than one in ten thousand. That year the shower brought a different surprise: a burst of bright fireballs about 16 hours before the predicted maximum, from dust shed in 1333 and held together ever since by a 5:14 resonance with Jupiter — the dust goes round the Sun five times while Jupiter goes round fourteen times, so Jupiter's pull keeps repeating in the same place and keeps the dust bunched together instead of letting it spread out.

The year 1999 was the first Leonid storm predicted correctly in advance by modelling where each old dust trail lies. The models said when Earth would meet the 1899 trail, and on November 18, at about 02:00 UTC, it did: a ZHR in the thousands, between about 3,700 and 5,000 depending on how the count is made. Until the end of the twentieth century, astronomers still did not know how to forecast meteor shower activity accurately; only when computers became powerful enough, by the end of the 1990s, did researchers manage to calculate Leonid activity years ahead. The 1999 storm was the first to be confidently predicted using this method, and nowadays predictions of Leonid outbursts run down to ten minutes.

That night also gave something nobody had seen before. Leonids do not only strike Earth; they strike the Moon too, with nothing there to slow them. Watching the unlit part of the Moon's face, a team led by José Ortiz recorded five brief flashes, each lasting less than a fiftieth of a second — the first impact flashes from meteoroids on the Moon to be confirmed beyond doubt.

The stream stormed again in 2001 and 2002: in 2001, two storms in one day, one over the Americas and one over East Asia and Australia, each with a ZHR above 1,000; and in 2002, two peaks with a ZHR of about 2,350 and 2,660. Since then there has been none — and there will be none in 2026.

## The Comet Behind the Shower: 55P/Tempel-Tuttle

The parent of the Leonids is comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, whose nucleus is about 3.6 kilometres across. It goes round the Sun every 33.2 years. It last passed closest to the Sun on February 28, 1998, and its next return is due on May 20, 2031. Each time the comet comes close to the Sun, it sheds dust that stays on its orbit as a narrow, dense trail. Young trails are still tight and close to the comet, so Earth tends to meet them in the few years either side of the comet's return — which is why Leonid storms come in clusters roughly 33 years apart. But not every return brings one. Whether Earth goes through the heart of a trail depends on how the pull of the planets, Jupiter above all, has shifted that trail since it was released. In 2026 the comet is far from the Sun, and Earth passes no dense part of its young trails: the shower will be made mostly of old, spread-out dust, with only minor contributions from a few trails.

That 33-year rhythm is the reason the Leonids are a generational shower. A child who saw the 1966 storm is now in their seventies; a teenager watching in 2026 will be in their forties when the next storm window opens. Comets, unlike meteor showers, are solid objects with their own stories — and the study of interstellar visitors such as <a href="/blog/3i-atlas-interstellar-comet-one-year-later/">One Year After Its Solar Plunge, Interstellar Comet 3I/ATLAS Is Fading Into the Dark</a> shows how much a single comet can teach us about the material the solar system is made of. Tempel-Tuttle is a home-grown comet, but its dust carries the same lesson: every meteor is a sample of a body we will never touch, delivered to our sky at 71 kilometres per second.

## When Is the Next Storm? The Models Disagree

A meteor storm is usually taken to mean a ZHR of at least 1,000. The comet returns in 2031, and the years around it are the obvious place to look — but the people who model the trails do not agree on what they will bring.

For 2027, the International Meteor Organization's calendar lists several possible small outbursts, each up to a ZHR of roughly 40 to 50 — a modest lift, not a storm. For the early 2030s, Maslov's calculations give strong but not storm-level years: a ZHR of up to about 400 in 2033, 500 in 2034 and 350 in 2035, with his next true storm coming only in 2094. A study by S. Abe, posted in August 2026 and not yet peer-reviewed, finds much more: a ZHR of about 1,400 in 2033, from the same 1899 trail that gave the 1999 storm, and two strong peaks with a ZHR of about 1,400 and 1,200 in 2034. The Farmers' Almanac, for its part, notes that the next predicted Leonid storm falls in the 2033 to 2034 return, when Earth is forecast to pass through several debris trails shed by the comet in 1699, 1767, 1866 and 1932.

So the honest answer is that nobody can promise the next storm. One model puts it seven years away, another nearly seventy. What the models do agree on is that the years around 2033 will be well above an ordinary year — and that 2026 is not one of those years. That disagreement is not a failure of the science; it is the science working as it should, with rival calculations waiting for the sky to judge between them. The 1999 storm was the first forecast the models got right, and every return since has sharpened them a little further.

## How to Watch: A Simple Plan

Watching a meteor shower needs no equipment, and the Leonids need less than most — but a small amount of planning turns ten meteors an hour from a rumour into a memory. The shower's quieter cousins reward the same patience: the Orionids, debris from Halley's Comet, peak each October, and our guide to <a href="/blog/orionid-meteor-shower-2026-peak/">Orionids 2026: Halley's Comet Debris Peaks This Week — How to Watch October's Meteor Shower</a> covers the same ground for a shower that shares the Leonids' pre-dawn habits.

First, pick the night: Tuesday, November 17 into Wednesday, November 18, with the morning of November 17 as the backup and the night of November 18–19 as the reserve. The best hours are after midnight through the last dark hour before dawn, roughly midnight to 5:30 a.m. local time. Second, pick the place: the darker the better, with an open view of the eastern sky, away from streetlights and house lights. From a city, expect one or two an hour; from a dark site, ten. Third, dress warmly and bring something to lie on — November nights are cold, and meteor watching is done lying down, looking up. A reclining chair or a blanket on the ground beats standing and craning your neck. Fourth, let your eyes adapt: give them twenty to thirty minutes in the dark, and keep your phone in your pocket. A single glance at a bright screen resets the adaptation.

Face east or south-east, where Leo rises, but do not stare at the radiant; sweep your gaze across a wide patch of sky and let the meteors come to you. The longer, brighter meteors will be obvious; the faint ones reward the patient. If you want to record what you see, note the time and brightness of each meteor — the American Meteor Society collects fireball reports from the public, and every counted meteor sharpens the models a little. Wide-field survey telescopes such as the one described in <a href="/blog/vera-rubin-observatory-watching-sky/">The Telescope That Watches Everything: Vera Rubin's First Year Rewriting Astronomy</a> are now watching the sky every night in a way no human observer can, but they cannot replace the simple act of lying under a dark sky and looking up.

And if the clouds come, do not despair. The Leonids will be back next November, and the November after that, and the dust that makes them has been falling for more than a thousand years. The shower that fell like snow over Boston in 1833 and gave forty meteors a second over Arizona in 1966 is the same stream that will give you one meteor every few minutes on a cold morning in 2026 — a grain of dust from a comet last seen near the Sun in 1998, meeting Earth head-on at 71 kilometres per second, burning up a hundred kilometres above your head, and reminding you, for the price of an early alarm, that the sky is still capable of falling.
