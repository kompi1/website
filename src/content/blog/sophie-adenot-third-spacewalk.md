At 21:29 Central European Summer Time on Tuesday, 1 September 2026, the hatch of the International Space Station's Quest airlock swung shut behind two spacesuited figures, and a remarkable fortnight in orbit came to an end. ESA astronaut Sophie Adenot and NASA astronaut Jessica Meir had just completed US EVA-99, a six-hour and forty-nine-minute excursion that was Adenot's third spacewalk in fifteen days — and her last of the mission.

For Adenot, the outing marked a transformation six months in the making. She had arrived at the station in February as a rookie on her first spaceflight. She would leave it as the European with the longest single spaceflight in history, a veteran of nineteen hours and forty-two minutes of extravehicular activity, and — most visibly — as the leader of the spacewalk. On EVA-99 she wore the red stripes of EV-1, Extravehicular Crew Member 1, while Meir, a veteran on her seventh career spacewalk, followed as EV-2 in an unmarked suit.

The spacewalk was also the sixth all-female EVA in the history of human spaceflight, and the 33rd conducted by a European astronaut. But the statistics, impressive as they are, only hint at the substance of what happened outside the station that day: a dense, demanding maintenance campaign that touched nearly every corner of the orbital outpost, from the docking ports where visiting spacecraft arrive to the particle-physics instruments hunting for dark matter.

## The day's work: a station-wide maintenance run

The day began, as spacewalk days always do, long before anyone went outside. Adenot and Meir were helped into their Extravehicular Mobility Units — the bulky American spacesuits that have been the workhorses of station EVAs for more than two decades — by crewmates Jack Hathaway and Anil Menon. The "campout" protocol, suit checks, and tool staging consumed the morning. Then, at 14:41 CEST, both astronauts switched their suits to battery power, the formal start of the EVA, and pushed out of the Quest airlock into the vacuum.

From there, the two spacewalkers split up to cover their separate work sites, a choreography planned down to the tether routing. Adenot translated — in spacewalk parlance, moved hand-over-hand along the station's exterior rails — to the forward port of the Harmony module. There she replaced a failed retroreflector, a small but critical navigational aid that visiting spacecraft use to fix their position and orientation during the final metres of rendezvous and docking. With commercial crew and cargo vehicles arriving on a regular cadence, keeping the station's docking aids in working order is not glamorous, but it is the kind of quiet maintenance on which the entire traffic pattern of the outpost depends.

While Adenot worked the Harmony port, Meir rode the Canadarm2 robotic arm out to the site of a failed high-definition camera and swapped it for a spare. The arm, operated from inside the station, gave her a stable platform and a commanding view of the truss — a reminder of how modern spacewalks blend human dexterity with robotic reach.

Midway through the excursion, Adenot returned to the airlock to fetch the sample-collection kit for NASA's MicroOrganisms experiment. The investigation asks a question that sounds simple and turns out to be fiendishly hard to answer: do microorganisms escape through the station's life-support vents, and if so, how far do they travel? Adenot collected swab samples from around the airlock and from her own gloves, building the dataset that researchers on the ground will use to map the microbial footprint of a crewed spacecraft — work with direct relevance to planetary protection and to the design of future deep-space habitats.

She then relocated and installed a series of power and thermal jumpers, reconfiguring the station's electrical and thermal plumbing to enhance system capabilities. Meir, meanwhile, turned her attention to the Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer, the large particle-physics detector mounted on the station's exterior, beginning preparations for a future upgrade of the instrument that studies cosmic rays and hunts for signs of dark matter. She also relocated a portable foot restraint, staging equipment in preparation for the next spacewalk in the sequence.

## A stuck shoulder, 400 kilometres up

Spacewalking is unforgiving, and EVA-99 provided a late reminder. Toward the end of the spacewalk, Adenot reported that she had lost mobility in her left shoulder joint — the bearing that lets a suited astronaut raise and rotate the arm. In a bulky pressurised suit, a frozen shoulder joint is more than an inconvenience; it limits reach, complicates translation, and can turn simple tasks into struggles.

Mission Control made the cautious call: Adenot should hold position until Meir could reach her. Meir made her way over, unzipped part of the arm assembly on Adenot's suit, and found the culprit — a cable snagged between the shoulder insulation and the rotation joint, held in place by nothing more than friction. She freed it, the joint moved again, and the two finished the EVA's remaining tasks and made their way back inside.

The incident was handled with the calm professionalism that long-duration crews are selected and trained for, but it underscored a truth about EVA that no amount of rehearsal in the Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory can fully erase: the suit is a spacecraft in its own right, and spacecraft have failure modes. That both astronauts and the ground team diagnosed and resolved the problem without cutting the spacewalk short speaks to the depth of the training pipeline behind every EVA.

## The sixth all-female spacewalk

EVA-99 also entered the history books as only the sixth all-female spacewalk ever conducted. The first, in October 2019, saw NASA astronauts Christina Koch and Jessica Meir exit the station together; they repeated the feat several times into early 2020. Meir has now taken part in four of the six all-female EVAs in history, and the outing was her seventh spacewalk overall — placing her third among female NASA astronauts in career EVA count, behind Peggy Whitson and Suni Williams.

For Adenot, the symbolism was layered. Just weeks earlier, on 18 August, she had become the first French woman ever to conduct a spacewalk, and the second European woman to do so after Samantha Cristoforetti's excursion in July 2022. There was a technical distinction worth noting: Cristoforetti had conducted her spacewalk from the station's Russian segment wearing a Russian Orlan suit, while Adenot became the first European woman to spacewalk in an American EMU — the suit used for USOS-side EVAs.

By September, the novelty of "firsts" had given way to something arguably more meaningful: routine excellence. Adenot wasn't a passenger on EVA-99. As EV-1, she led it.

## Three walks, fifteen days

The numbers behind Adenot's EVA campaign tell a story of escalating endurance. Her first spacewalk, on 18 August, ran six hours and twenty-three minutes. Her second, on 25 August, lasted six hours and thirty minutes. The third, on 1 September, stretched to six hours and forty-nine minutes — each longer than the last, totalling nineteen hours and forty-two minutes in the vacuum, all within a span of fifteen days.

The first two excursions were conducted alongside NASA astronaut Anil Menon, and their principal objective was the replacement of a failed SGANT antenna — the space-to-ground antenna mounted on the station's Z1 truss segment that handles a share of the outpost's communications traffic. Removing a failed antenna and installing its replacement is the kind of heavy, fiddly hardware work that spacewalks exist for: bolts torqued by gloved hands, connectors mated by feel, a refrigerator-sized unit manoeuvred along the truss with millimetric care.

In the days before that first excursion, Adenot and Menon had organised tools inside the Quest airlock while station commander Jessica Meir and flight engineer Jack Hathaway rehearsed the Canadarm2 manoeuvres that would support the spacewalkers. The preparation is invisible to the public and decisive to the outcome; EVAs are won or lost in the days before anyone opens the hatch.

Between the spacewalks, life aboard the station continued its demanding rhythm. Adenot kept up her onboard exercise research, running on the station's COLBERT treadmill while wearing the "EasyMotion-2" suit, which delivers electrical impulses to trigger muscle contractions during the workout — part of the ongoing effort to understand and counteract the physiological toll of long-duration weightlessness. She also carried forward a packed science programme that included EchoFinder, a device for autonomous medical ultrasounds to study the effects of weightlessness on herself and her crewmates; PhysioTool, measuring a suite of physiological factors; and ChlorISS, an experiment run in partnership with French schools observing how light and gravity shape the growth of Arabidopsis thaliana and Brassica rapa, two members of the mustard family. She even tested a prototype spacesuit, the "EuroSuit," and supplemented the standard onboard menu with a selection of French dishes — including lobster bisque and foie gras — created by Michelin-starred chef Anne-Sophie Pic.

## The epsilon mission: eight months that rewrote records

To appreciate the spacewalks, it helps to see the mission they crowned. Adenot launched on 13 February 2026 aboard a SpaceX Dragon with her Crew-12 crewmates — NASA astronauts Jessica Meir and Jack Hathaway, and Roscosmos cosmonaut Andrei Fedyaev. Her mission, named epsilon, was her first trip to space.

By August, with half a year in orbit behind her, she had settled into the peculiar double life of the long-duration astronaut: operator of a quarter-century-old orbital facility by day, research subject and science investigator by every other hour. Then came the assignment every astronaut trains for and few get to do three times. In the space of a fortnight, she went from rookie to spacewalker to spacewalk leader.

The mission's duration itself became a record: nearly eight months in space, making Adenot's the longest single spaceflight by a European astronaut. Long-duration missions are where the human spaceflight programme learns what it most needs to know for the journeys ahead — to the Moon's south pole, to Mars, and beyond. Every month in orbit refines the models of bone loss, radiation exposure, immune function, and psychological resilience that will decide whether those deeper journeys are survivable. Adenot's eight months, capped by three demanding EVAs, represent one of the richest single-mission datasets ESA has ever collected.

## Why this maintenance matters

It is easy, watching the highlight reels, to treat station EVAs as ceremonial — flags and footprints. The reality of US EVA-99 was closer to plumbing and electrical work, performed in the most hostile environment humans routinely visit. And that is precisely the point.

The retroreflector Adenot replaced is a passive device — no power, no moving parts — but visiting vehicles depend on it during the final approach to the station. As the traffic pattern around the ISS grows more complex, with crew and cargo vehicles from multiple providers arriving throughout the year, the reliability of docking aids becomes a safety-critical concern. The high-definition camera Meir replaced feeds the situational awareness that flight controllers and crews rely on during robotics operations and vehicle arrivals. The power and thermal jumpers Adenot installed reconfigure the station's lifeblood systems. The Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer work keeps alive one of the most ambitious physics experiments ever flown — a detector that has been sifting cosmic rays for evidence of dark matter and antimatter for over a decade.

None of these tasks makes headlines on its own. Together, they are the reason the International Space Station, now well into its third decade of continuous occupation, remains a functioning research facility rather than a derelict. The station's longevity is not an accident of robust engineering alone; it is the product of hundreds of EVAs like this one, each replacing, repairing, and upgrading the hardware that age and the space environment degrade.

## A milestone for Europe — and for France

For ESA, Adenot's EVA campaign carried institutional weight. European astronauts have conducted 33 spacewalks in the station's history, a modest share of the total, and each one represents years of investment in training infrastructure — from the Neutral Buoyancy Facility to the suit technicians and EVA flight controllers who make the excursions possible. Having a European astronaut not just participate in but lead a complex US EVA, as EV-1, is a visible return on that investment.

For France, the symbolism was sharper still. Adenot is the first French woman to walk in space, in a country whose space programme — through CNES and its partnership with ESA — has been a pillar of European spaceflight for six decades. Her appointment as a chevalier of the National Order of the Legion of Honour on 14 July 2026, France's national day, recognised a career that had already made her one of the country's most prominent scientific ambassadors; the spacewalks that followed wrote the achievement in vacuum.

There is also a quieter significance in the composition of EVA-99's crew. Six all-female spacewalks in the history of the programme is still a small number, but the trajectory matters: from the first Koch–Meir excursions in 2019–2020, through the intervening years, to an EVA led by a European woman in 2026. The pipeline of women in the astronaut corps, in mission control, and in the engineering teams that build the suits and plan the timelines has been widening for a generation. EVA-99 was one more data point in that trend — and, for the young scientists watching in France and across Europe, a vivid one.

## The physical ledger

Three EVAs in fifteen days also exact a toll that rarely makes the press releases. A spacesuit is pressurised to about a third of an atmosphere, which means every flex of a finger, every turn of a wrist, every grip on a handrail is performed against the suit's own resistance. Astronauts routinely return from long EVAs with sore hands, bruised fingertips, and aching shoulders; the shoulder joint that seized on Adenot is the same assembly that absorbs thousands of load cycles across a single excursion.

That Adenot's three spacewalks grew progressively longer — 6:23, then 6:30, then 6:49 — is a testament less to luck than to conditioning. EVA fitness is its own discipline, built through hundreds of hours in the Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory and maintained in orbit through the punishing daily exercise countermeasures that long-duration crews follow. By the third walk, Adenot wasn't just enduring the suit; she was directing the EVA in it. The red stripes of EV-1 are earned long before they are worn.

## What comes next

With the three spacewalks complete, Adenot's mission entered its final phase. The return to Earth — the fiery re-entry, the splashdown, the recovery teams waiting beside the Dragon capsule — would close out a flight that began as a rookie assignment and ended as a record-setting expedition.

For ESA, the lessons of epsilon will be studied for years: the medical data from eight months in orbit, the operational experience of three EVAs in fifteen days, the science return from a packed experiment manifest. For the broader programme, Adenot's campaign is a reminder of what the International Space Station is for. It is not only a laboratory, though the science it produces is world-class. It is a school — the place where astronauts learn, in the hardest possible classroom, how to work outside a spacecraft. Every lunar surface EVA of the Artemis era, every eventual excursion on Mars, will trace its lineage to afternoons like 1 September 2026: two figures in white suits, Earth wheeling silently below, doing the maintenance that keeps humanity's foothold in orbit alive.

Sophie Adenot went to space a rookie. She came home a spacewalker — three times over, and the last time, in the lead.
