Four days after one classified Space Force mission left the same pad, another is ready to go. On Saturday night, November 28, a SpaceX Falcon 9 is scheduled to lift off from Space Launch Complex 4E at Vandenberg Space Force Base in California carrying USSF-380, a classified payload for the United States Space Force. The four-hour launch window opens at 8:39 p.m. Pacific time, and the first-stage booster assigned to the flight, B1082, will be flying for the 27th time — a number that, only a few years ago, would have sounded like science fiction for an orbital-class rocket stage.

Almost everything about the payload itself is secret. The Space Force has said nothing public about what USSF-380 carries, what orbit it is bound for beyond low Earth orbit, or what it is supposed to do once it gets there. That silence is normal for this class of mission. What is unusual is the pace around it. USSF-380 is the latest launch in a classified constellation campaign that has put at least half a dozen missions into orbit since mid-August, each one adding radar-carrying satellites to a network designed to watch aircraft, drones, and cruise missiles from space. If the pattern of the previous flights holds, Saturday's launch will add another batch of satellites built on SpaceX's Starshield bus to a constellation that independent trackers already count in the hundreds.

The launch is also a measure of how routine the extraordinary has become at Vandenberg. The same pad, SLC-4E, hosted USSF-266 on Tuesday, November 24. Four days later it is expected to host USSF-380. A week earlier, the same pad turned around in 32 hours, 3 minutes, and 21 seconds between USSF-317 and NROL-136 — a record for the site. The cadence is no accident: the Space Force is building a proliferated constellation, and proliferated constellations are built one launch at a time, as fast as the pads will allow.

## A four-hour window on Saturday night

SpaceX is targeting Saturday, November 28, for the USSF-380 launch from Space Launch Complex 4E at Vandenberg Space Force Base in California. The four-hour launch window opens at 8:39 p.m. Pacific time and runs until 12:39 a.m. Pacific on Sunday, November 29. In Coordinated Universal Time, that is 04:39 UTC on November 29. For readers in Jakarta, the window opens at 11:39 a.m. WIB on Sunday, November 29.

If Saturday's attempt does not go, there is a backup opportunity on Sunday, November 29, at 6:26 p.m. Pacific time. SpaceX will broadcast the launch live, with its webcast beginning about 10 minutes before liftoff on the company's website and on X at @SpaceX. As with other classified national security missions, the webcast is expected to end shortly after the first stage lands, without confirming deployment of the payload. That is standard practice: the Space Force does not announce when, or even whether, a classified satellite has separated from the rocket's upper stage.

People living near the coast in Santa Barbara, San Luis Obispo, and Ventura counties may hear one or more sonic booms during the launch, SpaceX has warned, although what any individual resident experiences will depend on weather and other conditions. The warning matters because Falcon 9's first stage will be coming back: after separating from the second stage, B1082 will fly itself to a landing on the droneship *Of Course I Still Love You*, stationed downrange in the Pacific Ocean. The return involves an entry burn and a landing burn, and the sonic booms that sometimes accompany a returning booster are part of the soundtrack of a Vandenberg night launch.

The mission is classified, and the Space Force has published no press kit, no payload description, and no orbital parameters. The one official source of information about the flight is SpaceX's own mission page, which confirms the date, the window, the booster, and the recovery plan, and little else. Everything beyond that — what the satellites are, how many there are, and what they are for — comes from launch trackers, government contracting documents, and the pattern of the missions that came before.

## Booster B1082: flight number 27

The workhorse of Saturday's flight is a familiar one. B1082, the first-stage booster assigned to USSF-380, will be making its 27th flight. It previously launched USSF-62 — the mission that carried the Weather System Follow-on Microwave satellite, WSF-M 1, for the Space Force — along with OneWeb Launch 20, NROL-145, Transporter-18, and 22 Starlink missions. That manifest is a small history of the Falcon 9 program in itself: a military weather satellite, a commercial broadband deployment, a National Reconnaissance Office mission, a rideshare flight, and nearly two dozen Starlink launches, all flown by the same piece of hardware.

Twenty-seven flights is a remarkable number, but it is no longer the record. B1081, the booster that flew USSF-266 from the same pad four days earlier, was on its 29th flight. Other boosters in the fleet have flown more than 30 times. The reusability that SpaceX promised when Falcon 9 first began landing in 2015 has become, by 2026, a matter of bookkeeping: the question is not whether a booster can fly again, but how many times, and how quickly it can be turned around between flights.

The flight profile for Saturday is the standard Falcon 9 sequence. About 38 minutes before liftoff, the SpaceX launch director verifies the go for propellant load. RP-1 kerosene and liquid oxygen loading begin at T-35 minutes, with second-stage liquid oxygen following at T-16 minutes. The engines chill at T-7 minutes, the flight computer takes over final prelaunch checks at T-1 minute, and the nine Merlin engines ignite three seconds before liftoff. Max Q, the moment of peak mechanical stress on the vehicle, comes about 67 seconds into flight. Main engine cutoff follows at about 2 minutes 26 seconds, stage separation three seconds later, and second-stage engine start at 2 minutes 36 seconds. The payload fairing separates at about 2 minutes 57 seconds. The first stage begins its entry burn at about 6 minutes 4 seconds, ends it at 6 minutes 27 seconds, begins its landing burn at 8 minutes 2 seconds, and is scheduled to touch down on the droneship at 8 minutes 25 seconds after liftoff.

## What USSF-380 probably is: the SBST constellation

The most credible open-source assessment of USSF-380 is that it belongs to the Space Force's Space-Based Sensing and Targeting portfolio, known by the acronym SBST. The assessment rests on the launch's trajectory profile and its stage drop zones, which independent analysts say match previous SBST launches, and on the pattern of the campaign itself. NASAspaceflight's launch preview describes USSF-380 as "believed to be part of its Space-Based Sensing and Targeting (SBST) portfolio," and notes that the satellites are built on the Starshield satellite bus, with the exact number being launched unknown but "believed to be similar to that of a typical Starlink mission, based on Falcon 9's drop zones."

SBST is the Space Force's program to move airborne surveillance into orbit. Its core technology is called Space-Based Airborne Moving Target Indication, or SB-AMTI. The idea is an old one given a new platform: for decades, the United States has tracked aircraft, cruise missiles, and other moving targets with radar aircraft such as the E-3 Sentry AWACS and the E-8 Joint STARS. Those aircraft are expensive, limited in number, and vulnerable. SB-AMTI proposes to do the same job from low Earth orbit, with a constellation of small satellites carrying government-furnished radar payloads.

Each satellite in the constellation carries a radar that illuminates the Earth below and processes the Doppler-shifted returns from moving objects — aircraft, drones, cruise missiles — against the background of stationary ground clutter. A single satellite at roughly 500 kilometres altitude can only see a limited patch of ground at a time; useful illumination geometry covers perhaps 500 to 1,000 kilometres of ground. A single satellite also passes over any given point only intermittently, leaving gaps of around 90 minutes between passes. The constellation solves both problems by numbers: enough satellites, in enough overlapping ground tracks, and the gaps close. Industry modelling cited in reporting on the program suggests that on the order of 150 to more than 300 satellites at this altitude may be needed for continuous, global coverage — the kind that could warn of a cruise missile launch anywhere on Earth within seconds.

That is why the launch cadence matters, and why it has been so fast. The SBST campaign, as tracked in open sources, began in mid-August 2026 with USSF-366, continued with USSF-153 in early September, USSF-259 and USSF-385 in mid-to-late September, USSF-481 on October 15, and USSF-432 in early November. Each mission added Starshield-bus satellites carrying government-furnished radar sensors to low Earth orbit. Open-source orbital trackers had catalogued roughly 239 Starshield-class objects on orbit as of early October, and the earlier missions in the series are reported to have placed about 23 satellites each — roughly 70 satellites across the first three flights of the sequence alone.

The program's pace became possible after Space Systems Command awarded a $4.16 billion other-transaction agreement to SpaceX for SB-AMTI work in May 2026, one of the largest single such awards in Pentagon history. The contract, the cadence, and the constellation are all part of the same bet: that a proliferated network of small sensing satellites can match, and eventually exceed, what Cold War-era airborne radar platforms delivered, and that it can do so at a pace more associated with commercial broadband deployment than with classified national security payloads.

## The SBST campaign so far: a timeline

USSF-380 does not arrive in isolation. It is the latest launch in a campaign that has run, almost without pause, for more than three months. The sequence, as reported in open sources, shows a program accelerating toward a constellation rather than testing a concept.

**August 15–16, 2026 — USSF-366.** The first mission of the series lifts off from Vandenberg, carrying the first batch of Starshield-bus satellites with government radar payloads into low Earth orbit. In publicly available contracting documents, the flight carries the secondary designation R-1.

**September 10, 2026 — USSF-153.** The second flight, R-2, follows less than a month later. Booster B1081, which would go on to fly USSF-266 in November, is among the fleet's most-flown stages.

**Mid-to-late September 2026 — USSF-259 and USSF-385.** Two more launches in quick succession. USSF-385, designated R-3, is reported to have placed about 23 satellites in orbit, bringing the estimated R-series total to roughly 70 satellites before the next flight.

**October 15, 2026 — USSF-481.** The campaign continues into the autumn, with B1081 flying its 28th mission — a flight covered in <a href="/blog/spacex-ussf-481-classified-vandenberg-launch/">SpaceX Launches Classified USSF-481 Mission From Vandenberg as Booster B1081 Flies for the 28th Time</a>. The launch keeps the constellation's buildout on a roughly monthly rhythm.

**November 6–7, 2026 — USSF-432.** The fourth R-series flight, R-4, lifts off from Vandenberg on a Friday afternoon, November 6 Pacific time — November 7 in UTC. Booster B1095 flies its eighth mission and lands on *Of Course I Still Love You*. SpaceX's webcast ends shortly after the booster touches down, without confirming satellite deployment, as is standard for classified missions. The mission is covered in <a href="/blog/ussf-432-launch-success-november-7-2026/">USSF-432 Falcon 9 Launch November 7 2026: Classified Space Force Payload Reaches Orbit</a>.

**November 24, 2026 — USSF-266.** A Falcon 9 launches from SLC-4E at 11:08 a.m. Pacific time, carrying another classified Space Force payload. Based on its drop zones, analysts assess it as most likely part of the SB-AMTI program. Booster B1081 flies its 29th flight and lands on *Of Course I Still Love You* about eight minutes after liftoff. The full account is in <a href="/blog/ussf-266-launch-result-november-24-2026/">USSF-266 Launch Result November 24 2026: Falcon 9 Lands Booster B1081 on 29th Flight</a>.

**November 28, 2026 — USSF-380.** Saturday's flight, from the same pad, four days after USSF-266. If it follows the pattern, it will add another batch of radar-carrying satellites to the same constellation.

Six missions in roughly four months is a pace more associated with commercial broadband deployment than with classified national security payloads, and that is the point. A proliferated constellation is not built at a research pace. It is built the way Starlink was built: one launch at a time, as fast as the rockets and the pads allow, until the numbers add up to coverage.

## A pad working at wartime tempo

The tempo at SLC-4E deserves attention on its own. Vandenberg's busiest pad has hosted USSF-317 on November 21, NROL-136 on November 22, USSF-266 on November 24, and now USSF-380 on November 28. The gap between USSF-317 and NROL-136 — 32 hours, 3 minutes, and 21 seconds — set a pad turnaround record for the site. The four-day gap between USSF-266 and USSF-380 is, by that standard, almost leisurely.

That cadence is possible because the Falcon 9 system is designed for it: a reusable first stage, a pad that can be reset in days rather than weeks, and a launch provider that flies the same vehicle dozens of times a year. It is also possible because the demand is there. The Space Force is not the only customer keeping Vandenberg busy — Starlink launches fly from the same pad between national security missions — but the classified campaign is the reason the pad's schedule in the second half of 2026 has looked like a drumbeat.

The comparison with the mission four days earlier is instructive. Both flights used the same pad, the same rocket, the same droneship, and boosters on their 27th and 29th flights respectively. Both carried classified payloads assessed as part of the same program. The difference is the booster: B1081, which flew USSF-266, is one of a small number of boosters to have flown from all of SpaceX's currently active Falcon 9 pads, with a manifest that includes Crew-7, CRS-29, PACE, Transporter 10 and 13, EarthCARE, NROL-186 and NROL-48, and USSF-153 and USSF-481. B1082, which flies USSF-380, has a quieter but no less varied history, mixing military, commercial, and rideshare work. Together, the two boosters have flown 55 times.

## Why a radar constellation in low orbit

To understand why the Space Force is in such a hurry, it helps to understand what SB-AMTI is for, and what it replaces. Airborne moving-target indication — tracking things that move against the clutter of the ground — has been a military capability since the Second World War. The modern form of it is the radar aircraft: the E-3 Sentry, with its rotating radar dome, and the E-8 Joint STARS, with its ground-surveillance radar. Both are based on 1960s and 1970s airframes. Both are expensive to fly and maintain. Both are, in a conflict with a peer adversary, large, slow, and impossible to hide.

A satellite constellation changes the geometry. A radar in low Earth orbit looks down on the whole planet, one patch at a time, and it cannot be shot down by a fighter or a surface-to-air missile. The trade-off is coverage: a single satellite sees a small patch, and it revisits any given point only once every 90 minutes or so. The answer is numbers — 150 to more than 300 satellites, by industry estimates — and numbers are what the SBST campaign is delivering, one Falcon 9 at a time.

The satellites themselves are built on the Starshield bus, SpaceX's military adaptation of its Starlink satellite design. Starshield was announced publicly in December 2022, although the company had entered a $1.8 billion classified contract for the work in 2021. The bus carries Earth-observation sensors, communications payloads, and hosted instruments for government customers; its primary customers include the Space Development Agency, the National Reconnaissance Office, and the United States Space Force. The NRO's proliferated architecture, which began launching with NROL-146 in May 2024, is the largest Starshield deployment, with hundreds of satellites now in orbit. SBST is a parallel effort under the Space Force, using the same industrial base for a different mission: not imagery, but radar tracking of moving targets.

The distinction matters because it explains the secrecy. The Space Force has declined to provide any public details about the SBST payloads, their orbits, or their intended capabilities, as it has for every mission in the series. What is known comes from drop zones, contracting documents, and the satellites that amateur trackers catalogue after launch. The R-series designations — R-1 through R-4 — come from publicly available government contracting documents compiled by Spaceflight Now, not from any official announcement. The program's existence is an open secret; its details are not.

## What happens after liftoff

For the public, the visible part of USSF-380 will last about eight and a half minutes: liftoff, the climb through Max Q, stage separation, the booster's entry burn and landing burn, and touchdown on the droneship. SpaceX's webcast will cover that, and then, in all likelihood, it will end. The second stage will continue to orbit with its payload, and the Space Force will say nothing about whether deployment succeeded.

That is not a cause for suspicion; it is the routine of classified launches. The same pattern held for USSF-266 four days earlier, for USSF-432 earlier in the month, and for every SBST launch before them. Independent trackers will, in the days after launch, begin cataloguing new objects in low Earth orbit, and their number and orbital parameters will tell the story the official channels will not: how many satellites went up, and where they are. Until then, the honest statement is that USSF-380 is believed to be part of the SBST portfolio, based on its drop zones and its place in the campaign sequence, and that the Space Force has confirmed nothing.

There is a second milestone worth watching on Saturday night, and it belongs to the booster. B1082's landing, if successful, will be one more data point in the Falcon 9 recovery record — and the record is about to cross a round number. The recovery attempt scheduled for the following day's CRS NG-25 cargo mission to the International Space Station will be Falcon 9's 700th attempted landing, with 682 successes banked so far. USSF-380's landing is the one just before that milestone, which makes it, in a small way, part of the countdown to it. A booster flying for the 27th time, landing on a ship in the Pacific, on the eve of the fleet's 700th recovery attempt: the numbers have become so large that they risk sounding ordinary, and that is itself the story.

Sunday's CRS NG-25 mission, for reference, will launch from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral at 10:50 a.m. Eastern time, carrying Northrop Grumman's Cygnus XL cargo spacecraft — named *SS James "Jim" Lovell* after the Apollo 8 and Apollo 13 astronaut who died in August 2025 — to the International Space Station. Its booster, B1096, will be flying for the ninth time and will return to land at Landing Zone 40. The two missions, a day apart on opposite coasts, are a snapshot of what the Falcon 9 fleet does in 2026: classified constellations from Vandenberg, cargo to the space station from Florida, and a booster fleet that treats its 27th flight as routine.

## What to watch

If you are watching on Saturday night, the details are simple. The window opens at 8:39 p.m. Pacific time, 11:39 p.m. Eastern, and 11:39 a.m. WIB on Sunday in Jakarta. The webcast begins about ten minutes before liftoff. The booster landing, if all goes to plan, comes about eight and a half minutes after liftoff. If you live in Santa Barbara, San Luis Obispo, or Ventura counties, listen for the sonic booms.

If the launch slips, the backup opportunity is Sunday, November 29, at 6:26 p.m. Pacific time. And if it flies, the result will not be announced by the Space Force; it will be inferred, over the following days, from the new objects that appear in the public satellite catalogues, and from the silence that follows. That silence is, by now, part of the mission's signature — a constellation being built in the open, one launch at a time, with nothing said about what it sees.

## The constellation that does not announce itself

USSF-380 is, on paper, a small event: one rocket, one classified payload, one booster landing on a ship. But it is the seventh launch in a campaign that has, in a little over three months, put a new military sensing architecture into orbit at a pace the Pentagon has rarely achieved for anything. The satellites are built on a commercial bus. The rocket is a commercial vehicle flying its 27th mission. The pad turned around in four days. The only thing that is not commercial about the whole enterprise is the secrecy — and even the secrecy is, by now, a familiar part of the pattern.

The Space Force is betting that a proliferated constellation of small radar satellites can do what a handful of radar aircraft did for half a century, and do it better: persistently, globally, and without putting aircrew at risk. Whether that bet pays off will not be decided on Saturday night. It will be decided over the coming months, as the constellation fills out, as the gaps between passes close, and as the number of satellites in orbit climbs toward the 150 to 300 that analysts say continuous coverage requires. Saturday's launch is one more step toward that number — one more Falcon 9, one more booster landing, one more batch of satellites that the Space Force will not talk about. The launch itself, at least, will be visible to anyone watching the sky over Vandenberg. Everything after that is, for now, a matter of counting the objects that appear in orbit, and waiting for the next one.
