For eight years, BepiColombo had been falling toward the Sun. On Saturday, November 21, 2026, the falling finally stopped — but not in the way anyone at mission control had planned. At 18:24 UTC, as the spacecraft swept just 635 kilometres above Mercury's scorched surface, its four axial thrusters fired to brake it into orbit around the smallest planet in the Solar System. The burn was supposed to last 37 minutes. It stopped early. When the spacecraft's signal returned through ESA's deep-space antenna at New Norcia, the telemetry told a story no controller wanted to read: BepiColombo had entered safe mode, its manoeuvre interrupted, its fate uncertain.

Hours later, the picture changed completely. The burn had delivered around 77 percent of its planned velocity change — and that, it turned out, was enough. Mercury's gravity had caught the spacecraft anyway. At around 01:00 local time in Darmstadt, Germany, ESA's BepiColombo Flight Dynamics Manager, Frank Budnik, delivered the words the team had waited eight years to hear: "We confirm Mercury orbit capture!"

BepiColombo is now only the second mission in history to orbit Mercury, after NASA's MESSENGER, which circled the planet from 2011 to 2015. It is the first to arrive carrying two orbiters at once — ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, nicknamed Mio — and the first mission ever to enter orbit around another planet using a technique called weak stability capture. The spacecraft is safe, in a stable orbit, and on track for the separation of its two science orbiters on December 9. The science phase begins in April 2027.

It was, in the end, a successful arrival. But it was also a reminder of how little margin there is at Mercury, and how much of this mission's design was built around surviving exactly this kind of surprise.

## The night of the burn

The sequence began quietly. At 16:51 UTC on November 21, with all commands already sent in advance, BepiColombo reoriented itself for the manoeuvre. As planned, the spacecraft then went silent: a scheduled loss of contact while mission control at the European Space Operations Centre (ESOC) in Darmstadt waited for the spacecraft to carry out its instructions on its own. Nobody on Earth could intervene. For the next few hours, the mission was entirely in the hands of its onboard software.

At 18:24 UTC, passing 635 kilometres above Mercury, the four axial thrusters began firing. This was the first major manoeuvre in BepiColombo's post-transfer-module configuration — the spacecraft had shed its Mercury Transfer Module (MTM), the big propulsion unit that carried it across the inner Solar System, on September 3, a little more than two months earlier. The 37-minute burn was meant to slow the spacecraft enough for Mercury's weak gravity to hold it, turning a temporary gravitational capture into a lasting orbit.

Then the burn stopped. When contact was re-established via ESA's Estrack New Norcia deep-space antenna, telemetry showed that the manoeuvre had been autonomously interrupted and the spacecraft had entered safe mode — the protective state in which a spacecraft ceases all non-essential activities when it encounters unexpected conditions. The mission control team had prepared for a triumphant confirmation. Instead, they had a puzzle.

"Upon finding Bepi in safe mode, it was like flipping a switch. Everybody was focused in understanding what had happened," said Ignacio Tanco, ESA's BepiColombo Flight Operations Director.

The team immediately began analysing the spacecraft and trajectory data. Their analysis showed that the burn had ended prematurely, achieving around 77 percent of the total planned velocity change. The next question was the one that mattered most: what orbit had BepiColombo actually ended up in? Reacquiring the signal confirmed the spacecraft was there — but not exactly which orbit it had secured. It took detailed data analysis, and hours of waiting, before a clear answer came through the voice loop from the Flight Dynamics team at around 01:00 local time: Mercury orbit capture confirmed.

The cause, ESA later reported, was a software issue involving attitude guidance and thruster control. Teams at ESOC have been working with lead spacecraft manufacturer Airbus and colleagues across the joint ESA-JAXA mission to investigate it. The glitch cut the burn short — but the mission's design absorbed the blow.

## Why 77 percent was enough

BepiColombo's arrival at Mercury was never designed as a single do-or-die burn. That is one of the most interesting things about this mission, and it is the reason a shortened burn did not become a lost mission.

Mercury's gravity is feeble, and the planet sits deep in the Sun's gravitational well. Getting there is, paradoxically, harder than going to the outer Solar System: a spacecraft falling toward the Sun picks up enormous speed, and it must shed that speed or it will simply overshoot its target. BepiColombo spent eight years doing exactly that — using solar electric propulsion and nine planetary flybys, one at Earth, two at Venus and six at Mercury, to gradually brake its way inward. As Emma Bunce, a plasma physicist at the University of Leicester and one of the mission's leading scientists, put it: "Energetically, it takes more energy to go to Mercury than to go to the outer Solar System."

The arrival itself was designed around a technique called weak stability capture. Instead of arriving fast and braking hard, BepiColombo approached slowly enough that Mercury's gravity could catch it gently, in a loosely bound orbit. The technique had previously been used only by some spacecraft travelling to Earth's Moon; BepiColombo is the first to use it to enter orbit around another planet.

"With this weak gravity capture, BepiColombo would remain captured for several orbits, but only temporarily," explained Frank Budnik. "This is why Mercury orbit insertion is essential." The November 21 burn was the first of a sequence of thruster firings that will secure the orbit and then gradually lower it. Each burn happens at the point of closest approach to Mercury — the "sweet spot" where braking is most efficient. A single hours-long burn would carry the spacecraft well past that optimum point, wasting propellant through what engineers call gravity loss. Several shorter burns, each timed to the closest approach, make the most of the propellant on board — and, crucially, each burn can be replanned based on what the previous one actually achieved.

That flexibility is what saved the mission on November 21. The orbit insertion sequence had been designed from the start to be flexible and robust to unexpected events. BepiColombo ended up in a 75-hour orbit rather than the originally planned 58-hour orbit, but the remaining sequence of manoeuvres has already been adapted to the new trajectory — with no additional thruster burns required. The change does not affect Mio's separation on December 9, nor MPO's ability to reach its planned science orbit.

In other words, the mission had already priced in the possibility that something would go wrong on arrival night. It did go wrong. The price had already been paid.

## The eight-year road to Mercury

BepiColombo's journey began on October 20, 2018, when an Ariane 5 rocket lifted off from Kourou in French Guiana carrying a stack of three spacecraft: the Mercury Transfer Module, the Mercury Planetary Orbiter, and Mio, protected through the cruise by a sunshield. The mission was originally intended to launch on a Soyuz rocket, but the spacecraft grew heavier during design and a heavier-lift launcher was needed. Development delays — problems with solar arrays, propulsion systems and more — pushed the launch target repeatedly, from an initial aim of July 2014 through 2016 and 2017 before the 2018 liftoff.

The cruise that followed was a masterpiece of orbital choreography and a test of patience. BepiColombo looped once around Earth, twice around Venus and six times around Mercury, using each planet's gravity to adjust its speed and trajectory. The first Venus flyby came on October 15, 2020, at an altitude of 10,720 kilometres; the second, on August 10, 2021, passed just 552 kilometres above the planet's clouds. The Mercury flybys began in October 2021 and continued through January 2025, when the sixth and final flyby passed over the planet's sunlit northern hemisphere — and gave the mission's cameras a parting portrait of its destination.

The cruise was not without drama of its own. On May 15, 2024, ESA announced that thruster issues had prevented the completion of a trajectory-correction manoeuvre on April 26, 2024. Later that year, in September 2024, ESA announced that the problem could be compensated for — but only by delaying BepiColombo's arrival at Mercury from December 2025 to November 2026. The mission's schedule slipped by eleven months, and the science team waited another year for a destination that had already been eight years away.

The cruise years were not wasted. With the orbiters bolted together and their instruments largely idle, the mission team found ways to use the long journey. During the flybys, BepiColombo tested its onboard instruments on targets of opportunity. Its X-ray camera, the Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer (MIXS) — the first X-ray imaging device ever sent on an interplanetary mission — was pointed at the Crab Nebula, the remnant of a supernova witnessed on Earth in 1054, to verify that it could see the universe in X-rays. Those early observations, covered in our earlier report on [BepiColombo MIXS Crab Nebula First Observations](/blog/bepicolombo-mixs-crab-nebula-first-observations-november-2026/), were a rehearsal for the science to come — and proof that the instruments had survived the cruise in working order.

The final act of the cruise began on September 3, 2026, when the Mercury Transfer Module separated from the two science orbiters, as described in our report on [Goodbye to the Engine Room: BepiColombo Sheds Its Transfer Module as Mercury Arrival Begins](/blog/bepicolombo-mercury-transfer-module-separation/). The MTM had been the mission's engine room for eight years. Once it was gone, the orbiters were on their own — lighter, more fragile, and committed. There was no going back. Two and a half months later, on November 21, they were captured by Mercury.

## Two orbiters, two jobs

For now, MPO and Mio remain bolted together, travelling as one spacecraft in their 75-hour orbit. That changes on December 9 — December 10 in Japan — when the two orbiters separate and each takes up its own polar orbit. The separation is itself a delicate operation: the two spacecraft have been securely attached for eight years, through launch vibrations, extreme temperatures and the radiation of deep space, and they must now disconnect gently. A specially designed spring mechanism will release Mio and, as it does, twist to start the Japanese orbiter spinning. It is, as one engineer put it, a mechanism the team has thought very carefully about — because it only gets one chance to work.

Once separated, the two orbiters do different jobs from different vantage points. MPO, the ESA orbiter, is the surface-facing spacecraft. It will descend gradually until March 2027, when it reaches its science orbit between 400 and 1,500 kilometres above Mercury, and from there its cameras, spectrometers, altimeter and radio science experiment will map the planet's heavily cratered surface, its composition and temperature, its topography and its gravitational field — including a measurement that will determine the size of its core. Mio, the JAXA orbiter, spins at about 15 revolutions per minute and swings out to roughly 11,000 kilometres to study Mercury's magnetic field, its magnetosphere and the plasma environment around the planet. Its 15-metre wire antennas and 5-metre magnetometer booms must be extended over about a month after separation, and its commissioning phase will continue through March.

Together, the two orbiters carry 16 instruments — 11 on MPO and 5 on Mio — and a science team of almost 600 researchers. "Our almost 600-strong science team will be delighted to see BepiColombo pass this key milestone and begin revealing more about Mercury, from its interior and surface to the space around it," said Geraint Jones, ESA's BepiColombo Lead Project Scientist.

The approach to orbit insertion was previewed in our earlier report on [BepiColombo Mercury Orbit Insertion November 21 2026](/blog/bepicolombo-mercury-orbit-insertion-november-21-2026/), which described the weak-stability capture plan before the burn took place. What happened on the night was messier than the plan — and the plan, fortunately, had room for mess.

## The mysteries waiting at Mercury

Mercury is the least-explored of the Solar System's rocky planets, and it is strange in almost every way a planet can be strange. It is the closest planet to the Sun, yet it holds water ice in the permanently shadowed craters at its poles. Its surface temperature swings from a fierce 430 degrees Celsius in daylight to minus 180 degrees at night — the largest temperature range of any planet. It whips around the Sun in just 88 Earth days, yet it turns so slowly on its axis that a single day there lasts 59 Earth days. And despite being barely larger than Earth's Moon, it has a magnetic field — a global, Earth-like magnetic field generated, scientists believe, by a liquid outer core surrounding an iron core so large it fills most of the planet's interior.

Those contradictions are exactly what BepiColombo was built to resolve. Only three missions have ever visited Mercury. NASA's Mariner 10 flew past it three times in 1974 and 1975. MESSENGER orbited it from 2011 to 2015 and mapped the whole surface, discovering the polar ice, confirming the huge iron core and finding strange, shallow depressions called hollows scattered across the surface — bright, fresh-looking pits that may be forming even now, as volatile material escapes into space. MESSENGER ended its mission in 2015 with a planned impact on Mercury's surface. Since then, no spacecraft has orbited the innermost planet.

BepiColombo will pick up where MESSENGER left off, with better instruments and two vantage points instead of one. Its science goals read like a list of Mercury's open questions: why does a planet so small have such a large iron core, and what does that say about how the rocky planets formed? Why does it have a magnetic field when its neighbour Venus does not, and how has that field changed over time? Where did the polar ice come from, how has it survived for so long in the fiercest sunlight in the Solar System, and what is it made of? What are the hollows, and what do they reveal about the planet's surface chemistry? And how does Mercury's thin exosphere — a wisp of atoms knocked off the surface by sunlight and the solar wind — interact with the solar wind streaming past it?

The answers matter beyond Mercury. The innermost planet is an extreme case of planet formation: a world that kept an oversized iron core and lost much of its rocky mantle, possibly in a giant impact early in its history. Understanding how Mercury ended up that way sharpens our understanding of how Earth, Venus and Mars formed — and of how rocky planets form around other stars, where Mercury-like worlds may be common. BepiColombo's southern-hemisphere coverage will also be better than MESSENGER's, whose highly elliptical orbit favoured the north; the new mission's more circular polar orbits will give the first even look at the whole planet.

The environment will not make it easy. Mercury's proximity to the Sun means intense solar radiation and brutal thermal swings, and BepiColombo's sunshield and thermal design have been tested for eight years just getting there. The orbiters' separation events — including the shedding of the transfer module in September — leave holes in the protective coverings that must close, and each milestone carries its own risk. But the spacecraft has now survived the hardest test of the arrival sequence: a burn that went wrong, in the dark, 200 million kilometres from home, with no one able to help.

## What happens next

The capture is only the beginning of the arrival sequence. The months between now and April 2027 are a carefully choreographed series of burns, separations and deployments, each of which must work for the science phase to begin.

First comes the separation of the two orbiters on December 9–10, the mission's next major milestone. Mio's protective sunshield, known as MOSIF, will remain attached to MPO until it is ejected on December 16. From there, further thruster burns will guide MPO toward its final orbit by March 2027, each burn timed to the point of closest approach and each one replanned from the results of the last — the same dynamic sequence that absorbed the shortened insertion burn. Mio's wire antennas and magnetometer booms will deploy over about a month, and commissioning of both spacecraft will continue through March.

If all goes to plan, the nominal science phase begins on April 6, 2027, more than eight years after launch, and runs for at least a year, with a planned extended mission through 2029. The first science results — maps of the surface, measurements of the magnetic field, the first close look at the polar ice and the hollows — will arrive through 2027 and beyond.

There is a quiet irony in how this arrival unfolded. BepiColombo was designed to be caught gently by Mercury's gravity, using a technique so delicate it had never been tried at another planet. The insertion burn that was supposed to lock in that capture was cut short by a software glitch, the spacecraft went into safe mode, and mission control spent hours not knowing which orbit their spacecraft was in. And yet the design held: the weak capture caught the spacecraft, the flexible burn sequence absorbed the shortfall, and no extra propellant was needed. The mission that spent eight years braking arrived the same way it travelled — slowly, carefully, and with room for something to go wrong.

On November 21, 2026, after a journey of almost 10 billion kilometres, BepiColombo stopped falling toward the Sun and started circling the smallest planet in the Solar System. The burn was short. The orbit is real. And in December, when two spacecraft that have travelled together for eight years finally part ways above Mercury, the science — the reason for all of it — can begin.
