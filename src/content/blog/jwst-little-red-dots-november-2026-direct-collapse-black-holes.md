For four years, the James Webb Space Telescope has been showing astronomers something it was never expected to find: hundreds of tiny red specks in the infant universe, each one no wider than a few hundred light-years, each one shining as brightly as a galaxy thousands of times its size. They appeared in Webb's deepest images almost as soon as the telescope began science operations in 2022, and they have refused to fit any neat explanation ever since. They were too bright to be ordinary young galaxies, too red to be ordinary quasars, and too quiet in X-rays and radio waves to be the ravenous black holes they otherwise resembled. Astronomers nicknamed them "little red dots," and the name stuck precisely because nobody knew what else to call them.

A study published on November 10, 2026, in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters* may finally have cracked the case. Led by Daniel Whalen of the University of Portsmouth's Institute of Cosmology and Gravitation, the team used advanced cosmological simulations to show that little red dots are most likely primeval galaxies hosting black holes born by direct collapse — the long-predicted, never-before-seen process in which a supermassive primordial star collapses straight into a black hole, skipping the slow growth that other black holes need. If the model is right, Webb has been photographing the births of the first supermassive black holes, less than a billion years after the Big Bang, and the dots' strange colours are the fingerprints of that birth: dense gas wrapping the young black hole, trapping its X-rays while letting other light escape, and a crowded cluster of stars shining blue beside it.

It is a claim about the very beginning of the largest structures in the universe — about how something with the mass of ten million suns could exist when the cosmos was still in its first few hundred million years. And it arrives at a moment when Webb is steadily rewriting that early history, one impossible object at a time.

## The dots that should not exist

The mystery began with Webb's first deep looks at the early universe. The telescope's infrared eyes can see light that left its source more than 13 billion years ago, stretched by the expansion of space into the red and infrared. In those images, among the smudges of the first galaxies, astronomers kept finding something odd: point-like sources, barely resolved even by Webb, glowing red and surprisingly bright. The objects were first reported in a preprint in June 2023 and published in a peer-reviewed journal in March 2024. As of 2025, astronomers have catalogued more than 300 of them, and they appear to have existed between 600 million and 1.6 billion years after the Big Bang, with most clustered around 600 million years — a time when the universe was less than five percent of its present age of 13.8 billion years.

Everything about them was contradictory. A galaxy only a few hundred light-years across — a speck beside the Milky Way's 100,000-light-year span — should not be able to shine as brightly as a galaxy many times its size. Yet the dots did. Their spectra, the rainbow of their light broken into its component colours, had an unusual V shape: blue at one end, red at the other, with a dip in between that no standard model of a galaxy or a quasar could reproduce. And when astronomers pointed X-ray and radio telescopes at them, expecting the loud high-energy signature of a feeding black hole, they heard almost nothing.

"The LRDs have been one of the most surprising discoveries made by the James Webb Space Telescope," Whalen said. "They are compact, only a few hundred light-years across, but are as bright as much larger galaxies, and their unusual V-shaped spectra have defied the usual astrophysical explanations. Their lack of detectable X-ray and radio emissions has left astronomers struggling to explain what they are."

That struggle produced a parade of candidate explanations. Perhaps the dots were extremely compact collections of stars, burning furiously in a tiny volume. Perhaps they were heavily obscured black holes, their light smothered by dust. Perhaps they were supermassive stars — hypothetical giants hundreds of thousands of times the mass of the Sun — shining briefly before collapsing. Or perhaps they were something stranger still: "black hole stars," objects in which a black hole sits at the heart of a bloated, glowing envelope of gas. Each idea explained part of the evidence. None explained all of it — not the formation, not the abundance, not the lifespan, and not the spectra.

The dots mattered because they were not a curiosity at the edge of the catalogue. They were a population: hundreds of them, spread across Webb's deep fields, present at exactly the epoch when the universe's first giant black holes must have been forming. Astronomers already knew from ground-based telescopes that quasars powered by black holes a billion times the mass of the Sun existed within the first billion years after the Big Bang. That was the famous "too big, too soon" problem: there seemed to be no way for black holes to grow that large that fast if they started small and fed at the leisurely rates allowed by physics. The little red dots looked like they might be the missing link — the seeds, caught in the act — if only anyone could say what they were.

## A birth, not a disguise

Whalen's team attacked the problem the way theorists have attacked the seed problem for decades: with simulations. But instead of asking what kind of object could mimic a little red dot, they asked what kind of object a little red dot would be if it were caught at the moment of its birth. Their answer was the direct-collapse black hole, an idea that has lived in textbooks since the 2000s but has never been observed.

In the direct-collapse scenario, a cloud of pristine hydrogen and helium in the early universe — gas untouched by earlier generations of stars, and therefore free of the heavy elements that normally cool gas and fragment it into small stars — collapses under its own gravity without breaking apart. Instead of forming a cluster of ordinary stars, the cloud forms a single supermassive star, which then collapses directly into a black hole. The process skips the slow ladder of growth entirely: the black hole is born massive, already weighing millions of times as much as the Sun, with a lifetime of feeding ahead of it.

The simulations showed that such a newborn black hole would not look like a quasar. It would remain wrapped inside the enormous disc of gas that created it, and that gas would hide much of its activity from view. Dense gas surrounding the black hole traps high-energy X-rays, which is why the dots are silent to X-ray telescopes, while allowing some ultraviolet, visible, and infrared light to escape — the light Webb sees. And the model supplies the missing piece of the V-shaped spectrum for free: a large neighbouring cluster of stars in the small galaxy produces the blue portion of the spectrum, while the dust-reddened light of the hidden black hole supplies the red.

The numbers the simulations produce match what astronomers actually observe. The black holes in the model weigh around 10 million times as much as the Sun. The star clusters hold around 100 million stars packed into a region only about 500 light-years across, matching the compact sizes measured in real little red dots. And the model reproduces the properties of a wide variety of little red dots observed by Webb, including some of the most distant and extreme examples ever discovered — a breadth that the earlier explanations could not manage.

"What makes this discovery so exciting is that we may be witnessing the birth of the first supermassive black holes," Whalen said. "For decades, simulations have predicted that these objects should exist, and now the James Webb Space Telescope may finally be allowing us to see them."

That word — "may" — carries weight. The new study is a simulation matched against observations, not a photograph of a collapse in progress. No telescope has watched a supermassive star fall into a black hole, and none is likely to: the event would be brief, distant, and hidden behind the very gas that makes the model work. What the team has shown is that direct-collapse birth produces exactly the object Webb has been staring at for four years — size, brightness, colour, silence and all — where every competing idea fails on at least one count.

## Why the old explanations failed

To see why the new model has drawn attention, it helps to walk through the graveyard of earlier ideas — because each one was reasonable, each explained something real, and each eventually broke against a piece of evidence it could not carry.

The simplest idea was that the dots were just very compact, very furious star-forming galaxies: a hundred million stars crammed into a tiny volume, burning bright and fast. The problem was the spectra. Stars alone, however densely packed, do not naturally produce the dots' V shape, with its sharp dip between blue and red. Nor do they easily explain the sheer brightness per unit volume. And a starburst that intense should have blown itself apart with supernovae and winds on a short timescale, making the dots rare and fleeting — yet Webb finds hundreds of them, across a broad stretch of cosmic time.

The second idea was the opposite: the dots were ordinary quasars — feeding supermassive black holes — hidden behind thick dust. Dust reddens light, and the dots are red. But a feeding black hole powerful enough to explain the brightness should leak X-rays, even through dust, and should often show up in radio waves. The dots show neither. Stacking the X-ray data from many dots together, which should reveal even a faint average signal, has repeatedly come up empty. An obscured quasar can hide; it cannot hide that completely, that consistently, across a whole population.

The third idea reached for something more exotic: supermassive stars themselves, shining in the brief interval before they collapse. A star hundreds of thousands of times the mass of the Sun would be dazzlingly bright and compact, and it would live only a short while — which fits the dots' rarity in the later universe. But a supermassive star is a transitional object, a flash in cosmic time, and the dots are too numerous and too long-lived as a population for every one of them to be caught in the same brief moment. And a bare supermassive star does not naturally produce the V-shaped spectrum either.

The most recent and most intriguing rival was the "black hole star": a black hole embedded in a bloated envelope of gas, glowing from within. The idea gained ground in 2026 from one of the most detailed little red dot spectra ever taken, an object called GLIMPSE-17775, discovered by accident while Webb observed the galaxy cluster Abell S1063 and boosted by the cluster's gravitational lensing. That spectrum contained more than 40 spectral lines, including an "iron forest" of 16 iron emission lines, consistent with a rapidly accreting black hole wrapped in a dense cocoon of hot, partially ionised gas. The researchers on that work described the objects as "black hole stars," and the description fit the data beautifully.

Whalen's model does not so much refute the black hole star as absorb it. A direct-collapse black hole wrapped in its birth disc is, in observational terms, very close to a black hole star: a black hole inside a cocoon of gas. The difference is the origin story. In the black hole star picture, the cocoon is a phase a black hole passes through. In the direct-collapse picture, the cocoon is the birthplace itself, the leftover gas of the collapse that made the black hole in the first place. The distinction matters because it decides what the dots become. A black hole star is a curiosity. A direct-collapse black hole is a seed — the starting point of the billion-solar-mass giants that light up the universe a few hundred million years later.

There was also evidence pulling in the direction of black holes from a different angle. Another study this year, led by Pierluigi Rinaldi, used a spiral galaxy nicknamed the "Saguaro" — close enough, at a redshift corresponding to roughly 10.4 billion years ago, that Webb could resolve it properly — to show what a little red dot looks like up close. Its compact, intensely luminous red nucleus matched every known characteristic of a little red dot, and the authors described it as the first empirical demonstration of the objects' nature: a supermassive black hole eating at furious rates, cloaked in thick dust. The Saguaro does not prove the direct-collapse origin, but it strengthens the case that black holes, not stars alone, power the dots — the premise Whalen's model needs.

## Timeline: four years of a mystery

- **December 2021** — The James Webb Space Telescope launches, carrying the infrared vision needed to see the universe's first billion years.
- **July 2022** — Webb begins science operations. In its earliest deep images, astronomers start finding tiny, intensely red points of light that match nothing in the catalogues.
- **June 2023** — The objects are first reported in a preprint. They are quickly nicknamed "little red dots," or LRDs.
- **March 2024** — The discovery is published in a peer-reviewed journal, confirming the dots as a real population, not an artefact of the new telescope.
- **2024–2025** — Follow-up studies pile up contradictions: hundreds of dots, extreme compactness, V-shaped spectra, no X-rays, no radio. The catalogue passes 300 objects.
- **2026** — Two studies sharpen the picture. The GLIMPSE-17775 spectrum reveals the "iron forest" of a black hole inside a gas cocoon, and the Saguaro galaxy shows a little red dot resolved up close as a dust-cloaked, rapidly feeding black hole.
- **November 10, 2026** — Whalen and colleagues publish the direct-collapse model in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*, showing that newborn black holes wrapped in their birth gas reproduce the dots' full range of properties.
- **November 10, 2026** — On the same day, a separate study featured by the American Astronomical Society stacks Webb data to reveal a hidden population of smaller, ordinary black holes in young galaxies — a reminder that the early universe grew black holes by more than one route.
- **Coming years** — NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is slated to survey the early universe in bulk, and the Roman eXtreme Deep Field programme plans to hunt for more little red dots among its targets, testing whether the direct-collapse model holds across a much larger sample.

## The seeds of giants

The little red dots sit at the centre of one of astronomy's oldest arguments: how do you grow a black hole a billion times the mass of the Sun in less than a billion years? The problem is arithmetic. A black hole feeding on gas cannot eat faster than a physical limit set by the pressure of its own radiation — the Eddington limit — and starting from the collapsed core of a single massive star, roughly a hundred solar masses, even feeding at that limit continuously barely gets you to a billion solar masses in the time available. Something has to give: either the seeds were born big, or the feeding was faster than the textbooks allow, or both.

The direct-collapse model is the "born big" answer in its purest form. Instead of a hundred-solar-mass seed growing for hundreds of millions of years, the collapse of a supermassive primordial star hands the universe a black hole already weighing millions of suns. From there, even ordinary feeding is enough to reach a billion solar masses by the time the first quasars appear. That is why the stakes of the little red dot debate are higher than a classification dispute. If the dots are direct-collapse black holes, the seed problem is solved — or at least, one of its solutions is real, and Webb is watching it happen.

Webb has been finding evidence for the other routes, too, and the picture that emerges is of a young universe that grew its black holes by every means available. <a href="/blog/jwst-firefly-sparkle-black-hole-seeds-2026/">JWST's Firefly Sparkle black hole seeds</a> research, published in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* in 2026, found dense young star clusters in two of the earliest galaxies ever seen — the Firefly Sparkle, imaged about 600 million years after the Big Bang, and the Cosmic Gems arc, seen when the universe was only about 460 million years old — that may be factories for intermediate-mass black holes, the middleweight seeds weighing hundreds to a few thousand suns. The clusters in the Cosmic Gems yielded the heaviest of these, between 1,600 and 2,700 solar masses, while those in the Firefly Sparkle ranged from roughly 180 to 810 solar masses. Those are the "born medium" seeds: black holes built up inside crowded star clusters, where stars collide and merge, rather than born in a single collapse.

And a study led by Madisyn Brooks of the University of Connecticut, published in *The Astrophysical Journal* in 2026 and featured by the American Astronomical Society on November 10, found the opposite end of the scale: a hidden group of smaller, more ordinary black holes in young galaxies, revealed by stacking Webb observations from four surveys — CEERS, JADES, RUBIES and GLASS — of galaxies that showed no sign of a black hole on their own. The typical hidden black hole weighed roughly 160,000 to 1.3 million times as much as the Sun, and most were less than one percent of their galaxy's mass — a proportion much like the black holes in galaxies today, and very different from the "monsters" Webb had spotted earlier, some of them a tenth of their whole galaxy's weight. The early universe, it seems, did not only grow monsters. It grew ordinary black holes, too, quietly, in galaxies that gave no sign of them until their light was added together.

The same era also produced stranger arrangements. <a href="/blog/lid-1166-dual-black-holes-z-4-5-november-2026/">LID-1166's dual black holes</a>, seen at a redshift of about 4.5, showed two growing black holes inside a single early galaxy — a reminder that the first galaxies were crowded, violent places where black holes met, merged, and fed together. And <a href="/blog/jwst-3d-dark-matter-map-cosmos-web-november-2026/">Webb's 3D dark matter map from the COSMOS-Web survey</a> has begun to chart the invisible scaffolding those galaxies grew in, the dark-matter web that funnelled gas into the densest knots where the first seeds could form.

Put together, the three strands — direct-collapse giants, cluster-built middleweights, and hidden ordinary black holes — suggest the seed problem was never a single puzzle with a single answer. The early universe tried everything. The little red dots may be the most dramatic of the attempts: the biggest seeds, born in the most violent way, shining briefly and brightly before their gas clears and they settle into the long, quiet business of becoming quasars.

## What would prove it

A model that explains everything is not the same as a model that is right, and the honest test of Whalen's idea is what it predicts that its rivals do not. The most direct test is also the hardest: the X-rays. In the direct-collapse picture, the X-rays are trapped by gas, not absent — which means that with a sensitive enough X-ray telescope, or by stacking the observations of many dots for long enough, a faint glow should eventually emerge, with a particular spectrum shaped by gas absorbing the softer X-rays and letting only the hardest leak through. A continued total silence, even as instruments improve, would point back toward explanations with no black hole at all.

A second test is numbers. Direct collapse requires special conditions — pristine gas, a strong bath of ultraviolet light from neighbouring galaxies to keep the gas hot and prevent it from fragmenting into stars — and those conditions were rare. The model therefore predicts a specific abundance of little red dots as a function of cosmic time: common enough to account for the hundreds Webb has found, but not so common that every early galaxy hosts one. Larger surveys will measure that abundance precisely. NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, launching later this decade, will survey the early universe in bulk, and the Roman eXtreme Deep Field programme — a 386-hour flagship survey led by Haojing Yan with 130 scientists — lists the little red dots among its prime targets. If Roman finds the dots in the numbers and places the model predicts, the case strengthens; if it finds them everywhere, or nowhere, the model will need revision.

A third test is the neighbourhood. The model predicts that each little red dot sits in a small galaxy with a dense cluster of around 100 million stars packed into 500 light-years, producing the blue half of the V-shaped spectrum. Webb's spectrographs can already separate the light of the black hole from the light of the stars in the brightest dots, and as those measurements accumulate, the predicted cluster should show up again and again: same size, same stellar mass, same blue contribution. The Saguaro result — a resolved dot with exactly that kind of compact red nucleus — is a first step in that direction.

There is also a test that does not depend on Webb at all. If direct-collapse black holes were common in the early universe, some of their descendants should survive to the present day as the central black holes of ordinary galaxies — and the relationship between those black holes and their host galaxies should carry a faint memory of a massive birth. The Brooks study's finding that most early black holes were ordinary, less than one percent of their galaxy's mass, already hints that the monster seeds were the exception rather than the rule. Counting how many galaxies carry the signature of a massive seed, and how many carry the signature of a small one, is a census that will take the rest of the decade to complete.

For now, the model stands as the best explanation on the table — the first to reproduce the dots' full range of properties, including the most distant and extreme examples, from a single physical process. That is a high bar, and it is why the paper has drawn the attention it has. But the history of the little red dots is a history of confident explanations breaking against new data, and the astronomers who work on them know it. The dots have surprised Webb before. They may yet do so again.

## Why it matters

It is easy to hear "black hole seeds" as an abstraction, a detail for specialists. It is not. The question of how the first supermassive black holes formed is the question of how the universe got its shape. Black holes are not just cosmic vacuum cleaners. The energy they release as they feed — the light of quasars, the jets that punch across millions of light-years, the winds that blow gas out of galaxies — regulates how galaxies grow, when they stop forming stars, and how the gas between galaxies is heated and enriched. A universe whose black holes were born big would have lit up earlier, heated its surroundings sooner, and built its galaxies differently from a universe whose black holes grew slowly from small seeds. The little red dots are the earliest evidence of which universe we live in.

They also matter for what comes next. The same physics that made the first black holes is the physics that will decide what the Roman Space Telescope sees when it opens its wide eye on the early universe, and what the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory will look for when it tries to image Earth-like planets around Sun-like stars — a mission that depends on the same high-contrast techniques, refined on Webb, that recently pulled the planet 51 Eridani b out of its star's glare. The tools sharpen each other. A better model of how light escapes a cocoon of gas around a newborn black hole is also, indirectly, a better model of how to separate a planet's faint light from its star's overwhelming glare.

And there is a simpler reason the dots have captured so much attention: they are a reminder that the universe still keeps secrets in plain sight. Webb is the most powerful telescope ever flown, and for four years it has been showing astronomers objects that no theory predicted, in numbers no one expected, at an epoch everyone thought was understood. The little red dots were not found at the edge of the observable universe or in some exotic corner of parameter space. They were in the deep fields, in the middle of the images, hundreds of them, waiting for someone to notice that they did not fit. The history of astronomy is full of such moments — the cosmic microwave background found in the static of a radio antenna, the accelerating universe found in the dimming of distant supernovae — and each time, the anomaly turned out to be the story.

For now, the dots have a name for what they are: primeval galaxies hosting black holes born by direct collapse, wrapped in the gas that made them, shining with the light of a hundred million stars beside them. It is a portrait of a birth — violent, brief on cosmic timescales, and hidden behind its own afterbirth — and it fits everything Webb has seen. The next few years will decide whether the portrait is true. Roman will count the dots. Deeper X-ray observations will listen for their trapped light. Webb's spectrographs will keep pulling their colours apart, line by line, until the blue half and the red half give up their separate stories.

If the model holds, then the first time anyone saw a supermassive black hole being born, they did not recognise it. They called it a little red dot, filed it under "unexplained," and moved on to the next image — while in the data, behind the red, a star the size of a solar system was falling into darkness, and a galaxy was lighting up around it. The universe was less than a billion years old. Its largest inhabitants were already being born. And we were watching, four years before we understood what we were seeing.
