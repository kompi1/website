NASA has switched off the science mission of one of the most productive telescopes ever flown to the International Space Station. On November 17, 2026, the agency announced that NICER, the Neutron star Interior Composition Explorer, has ended science operations after eight years of staring at the densest objects in the universe. The little X-ray telescope, about the size of a washing machine and bolted to the outside of the station, outlived its planned 18-month mission by six and a half years, and it leaves behind something no telescope had delivered before: actual measurements of how big a neutron star is.

The ending was not sudden. NICER stopped taking science data in June 2025, when a motor that steers the telescope failed and left it unable to swivel sideways to track its targets. Engineers spent more than a year investigating, photographing the instrument with robotic cameras on the station's exterior, and ruling out external causes before concluding that the motor itself had most likely failed inside the payload. On November 17, NASA made the outcome formal: the science is over, and the mission team will now finish NICER's eight-year archive of observations, decommission the instrument, and eventually detach it from its perch near the station's starboard solar array.

It is a quiet ending for a mission that answered one of the oldest questions in astrophysics with numbers instead of theory. A neutron star packs more mass than the Sun into a ball the size of a city. Until NICER, nobody knew exactly how big that ball was. Now we do — to within about five percent for a typical neutron star — and the answer has already begun to rewrite physicists' understanding of what matter does when it is squeezed harder than anywhere else in the universe.

## A question that sounds simple

NICER was built to answer a question that sounds almost childishly simple: how big is a neutron star? The answer matters because a neutron star is the most extreme matter in the universe that scientists can actually measure. When a massive star dies in a supernova, its core collapses until protons and electrons are crushed together into neutrons, and the result is an object so dense that NASA describes neutron stars as containing the densest matter in the universe that we can measure. A teaspoon of neutron-star matter would outweigh a mountain, yet the whole star is only about as wide as a large city. Knowing its exact size and mass tells physicists how matter behaves at densities no laboratory on Earth can reproduce — whether neutrons stay neutrons under that crushing pressure, or whether they dissolve into a soup of free quarks, or something stranger still.

The problem was that neutron stars are small, far away, and dim in ordinary light. NICER's trick was to watch them in X-rays, and to watch them with extraordinary precision. The telescope carries 56 small X-ray concentrators that funnel photons onto silicon detectors, and it stamps the arrival time of every single photon to within 100 nanoseconds of Universal Time. That timing is the heart of the mission. NICER's targets are pulsars, neutron stars that spin hundreds of times a second and sweep beams of X-rays across space like lighthouses. By recording exactly when each pulse of X-rays arrives, and how the pulse shape changes as the star rotates, scientists can model the hot spots glowing on the star's surface and work backwards to its mass and radius. The technique is called pulse-profile modelling, and NICER was the first instrument built to do it properly.

Pulsars themselves were discovered in 1967 by Jocelyn Bell Burnell, and they have been astronomy's most precise clocks ever since. NICER turned that clockwork into a measuring tape. The mission's two headline results, published years apart, together told physicists something nobody had expected: neutron stars are bigger, and their matter stiffer, than many models predicted.

## The two stars that changed the textbooks

NICER's first great result came in 2019, from a pulsar called PSR J0030+0451, or J0030 for short. Two independent teams analysed NICER's observations of the star and found it was about 1.4 times the mass of the Sun and about 26 kilometres across. It was the first time anyone had measured both the mass and the radius of a neutron star with real precision, and the result immediately narrowed the range of theories about the matter inside. A later reanalysis using six years of NICER observations refined the numbers further, putting the star's mass at 1.43 times the Sun's and its equatorial radius at 12.68 kilometres, with the two competing models of its hot spots converging on a consistent picture.

The second result was even more striking. PSR J0740+6620, known as J0740, is the most massive neutron star with a precisely measured mass: about 2.08 times the mass of the Sun, established by radio observations, sitting more than 3,600 light-years away in the northern constellation Camelopardalis, spinning 346 times every second in a binary system with a white dwarf. When NICER's team measured its size, they found it was roughly the same size as J0030 — about 24.8 kilometres across according to a team led by Thomas Riley and Anna Watts at the University of Amsterdam, and about 27.4 kilometres according to a team led by Cole Miller at the University of Maryland. The two estimates overlap within their uncertainties, and the message of both was the same: a star nearly fifty percent more massive than J0030 is essentially the same size.

"Our new measurements of J0740 show that even though it's almost 50% more massive than J0030, it's essentially the same size," Watts said when the results were presented at a virtual meeting of the American Physical Society in April 2021. That single fact is a hammer blow to theories in which ultra-dense matter is soft and squeezable. If matter collapsed easily under pressure, the heavier star should be noticeably smaller. It is not. The matter inside a neutron star resists compression far more stubbornly than some physicists had predicted, which suggests that even under extreme pressure, matter may not break down into free quarks as readily as expected.

The precision matters as much as the headline. Thanks to NICER, scientists now know the radius of a standard neutron star — one with 1.4 times the Sun's mass — to within an uncertainty of about five percent. Before NICER, that number was a guess spread across competing models. The mission went on to publish radius measurements for five pulsars in total: J0030+0451, J0740+6620, PSR J0437−4715, PSR J1231−1411, and PSR J0614−3329. Each one added another point to the curve that maps a neutron star's mass to its size, and each point tightened the constraints on the equation of state — the physics that describes matter at densities beyond anything a laboratory can create.

NICER's science reached far beyond neutron stars. The telescope's eight years of observations fed more than 300 scientific papers, and NASA credits the mission with revolutionising understanding of black holes, active galaxies, and other energetic phenomena. The same X-ray timing that measured neutron stars also caught the flickering of matter falling into black holes and the flares of distant galaxies, turning a specialised instrument into a general-purpose observatory for the violent X-ray sky. The Chandra X-ray Observatory has spent decades mapping that same violent sky in exquisite detail, and the story of <a href="/blog/at-2016blu-chandra-supernova-impostor-november-2026/">a supernova impostor hiding a black hole</a> shows how X-ray eyes keep rewriting what astronomers think they know about stellar explosions.

## Navigating by dead stars

NICER's most futuristic achievement had nothing to do with neutron-star physics at all. In 2018, a team of NASA scientists and engineers used the telescope to demonstrate, for the first time, fully autonomous navigation by pulsar. The experiment was called SEXTANT, short for Station Explorer for X-ray Timing and Navigation Technology, and it worked on a beautifully simple idea: because pulsars spin with such regularity, their X-ray pulses arrive at a spacecraft at times that depend on exactly where the spacecraft is. Measure the arrival times of pulses from several pulsars, compare them with predictions, and you can work out your position in space — no radio contact with Earth required.

That matters enormously for deep space. A spacecraft travelling to Mars takes minutes to exchange a radio signal with Earth, and a probe heading for the outer planets waits hours. A navigation system that reads the sky itself, using pulsars as a natural GPS, could let future spacecraft steer themselves across the solar system and beyond. NASA has said the technique demonstrated by NICER could be used for travel to Mars and beyond, and the demonstration remains one of the mission's proudest legacies — a piece of pure astrophysics that doubled as a navigation experiment for the next generation of explorers.

The same technology has found uses back on Earth. Equipment developed to test NICER before launch is being incorporated into prototype portable CT scanners, materials analysis systems, and communications systems, a reminder that instruments built to measure dead stars often end up improving life for the living.

## Eight years on the station

NICER's story is also a story about longevity. It launched on June 3, 2017, and was installed on the outside of the station near the starboard solar array, where it had an unobstructed view of the sky and the station's own bulk to shield it from some of the harshest radiation. Its prime mission was just 18 months. It kept going for eight years, through solar storms, station reconfigurations, and two serious hardware problems that would have ended a lesser mission.

The first problem arrived in May 2023, when NICER developed a light leak. Unwanted sunlight began entering the instrument through small areas of damage in its thin thermal shields — the delicate filters that block visible light while letting X-rays through to the detectors. Nighttime observations were unaffected, but during the station's daytime the sunlight flooded the detectors with charge, overwhelming their electronics. The team restricted daytime observations to targets far from the Sun and lowered the instrument's sensitivity during orbital day, recovering about twenty percent of daytime observing time while they worked on a fix.

The fix was gloriously hands-on. In January 2025, NASA astronaut Nick Hague went outside the station on a spacewalk and installed nine patches over the largest damaged areas of NICER's thermal shields. The telescope resumed science operations the same day, and the patches cut the sunlight inside the instrument substantially. Close-up photographs from the spacewalk revealed additional small holes and cracks the team had not been able to see before, and a clever reconfiguration of the detectors' electronics — teaching them to ignore the charge generated by sunlight — recovered still more. By March 2025, when the reconfiguration was completed across all seven of the instrument's measurement units, NICER was collecting observations during more than seventy percent of station daytime. It was a repair carried out 400 kilometres above Earth, on an instrument never designed to be serviced, and it bought the mission another season of science.

## Timeline: NICER's eight years

- **June 3, 2017** — NICER launches aboard a SpaceX Dragon cargo spacecraft and is installed on the International Space Station.
- **July 17, 2017** — Regular science observations begin after commissioning.
- **2018** — The SEXTANT experiment demonstrates the first fully autonomous pulsar-based navigation in space.
- **2019** — Two teams publish the first precise mass and radius for a neutron star, PSR J0030+0451: about 1.4 solar masses and 26 kilometres across.
- **April 2021** — Results for the heavyweight pulsar PSR J0740+6620 are presented: about 2.1 solar masses, yet essentially the same size as J0030.
- **May 2023** — A light leak is discovered in NICER's thermal shields, limiting daytime observations.
- **January 2025** — Astronaut Nick Hague installs nine patches on the damaged shields during a spacewalk; science resumes the same day.
- **March 12, 2025** — A detector reconfiguration is completed, restoring observations during more than 70 percent of station daytime.
- **June 17, 2025** — Science observations are paused after a motor fails, leaving NICER unable to swivel sideways.
- **November 17, 2026** — NASA formally ends NICER's science operations and begins work on the final archive and decommissioning.

## The motor that stopped turning

The second hardware problem was the one NICER could not survive. In June 2025, the telescope was suddenly unable to swivel side to side beyond its current orientation. A telescope that cannot aim is not an observatory: it can only watch whatever drifts through its narrow field of view, and it cannot track the pulsars it was built to measure. The team paused science operations on June 17, 2025, when the motor's degraded performance began affecting the observations, and began an investigation.

Robotic cameras on the outside of the station collected photographs of NICER that helped eliminate external causes — nothing had struck it, and nothing was visibly blocking it. The team coordinated with station personnel on troubleshooting manoeuvres to test what was happening inside the payload. After an extensive investigation through 2025 and 2026, engineers concluded that an internal motor had most likely failed, limiting the telescope's motion. NASA has been careful to keep the diagnosis provisional — engineers "think" the motor failed — but after seventeen months with no path back to science, the agency drew the line.

The telescope's position poses no safety risk to the station or its crew, NASA said, and one of the team's early goals was to move NICER into a stowed configuration in case it needed to accommodate station activities. The failure is a reminder of how unforgiving spaceflight is: a single small motor, one component among thousands, ended a mission that had survived a light leak, a spacewalk repair, and eight years in orbit. Space telescopes end in many ways. Some, like NASA's Swift observatory, fall back to Earth in a blaze after decades of service — the story of <a href="/blog/nasa-swift-observatory-re-entry-november-2026/">Swift's fiery re-entry in November 2026</a> is a reminder that every mission has a final chapter. NICER's ending is quieter: it will be switched off, archived, and eventually unbolted, rather than burning up.

## What happens now

With science operations formally over, the mission and station teams are focused on two jobs. The first is to finalise NICER's eight-year archive of X-ray observations, completing the calibration and ingest of the full dataset into the public HEASARC archive at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center, so that researchers anywhere in the world can keep mining it for years. The second is to decommission the telescope and detach it from the station. NASA has not announced a date for the detachment, and the instrument will remain on its perch until the station team schedules the work.

The archive is where NICER's real afterlife begins. More than 300 papers have already drawn on its observations, and the final, fully calibrated dataset will be richer than anything researchers have had so far — eight years of photon arrival times, stamped to 100 nanoseconds, covering hundreds of targets. The updated analysis of J0030, built on six years of NICER data, showed that more observing time keeps paying off: it resolved tensions between competing models of the star's hot spots and brought the mass and radius into line with results from other pulsars and from gravitational-wave observations. There is every reason to expect the archive to keep producing results long after the telescope itself is gone. That is the pattern with great observatories: the hardware retires, and the data keeps working.

The timing of the announcement also marks a changing of the guard in X-ray astronomy. NICER's measurements of neutron-star radii were the first of their kind, and they set the questions that the next generation of missions will chase. The record of <a href="/blog/treble-clef-radio-phoenix-chandra-galaxy-clusters/">Chandra's decoding of a black hole's echo behind the Milky Way</a> shows how X-ray telescopes keep finding new ways to listen to the universe, and future instruments will build directly on NICER's technique of turning pulsar light into precise measurements of matter itself.

## What NICER leaves behind

NICER's legacy can be counted in several ways. There is the physics: the radius of a typical neutron star now known to five percent, the discovery that the densest matter resists squeezing far more than expected, and five pulsars measured well enough to constrain the equation of state that governs matter at its most extreme. There is the navigation: the first demonstration that a spacecraft can find its own way through space by reading pulsar pulses, a capability that could one day guide ships to Mars and beyond without waiting for instructions from Earth. There is the data: an eight-year archive of X-ray timing that will fuel discoveries for a decade. And there is the example: a mission planned for 18 months that delivered eight years of science, survived a light leak and a spacewalk repair, and kept measuring the universe until a single small motor finally said stop.

The telescope that ends its mission this week was never meant to last this long, and it was never meant to do this much. It was built to answer one question — how big is a neutron star? — and it answered it, then kept answering questions nobody had thought to ask. When it is finally detached from the station and its watch ends, the numbers it gathered will stay: the sizes of dead stars, written in X-ray light, measured to a precision that would have seemed like science fiction when the mission launched. The eye has closed. What it saw will keep teaching astronomers for years.
