Rocket Lab has closed out a record year in style. On 27 November 2026, an Electron rocket lifted off from the company's Launch Complex 1 on New Zealand's Mahia Peninsula carrying a single radar-imaging satellite for Japanese Earth-observation company Synspective, completing the 101st Electron mission and the 22nd Electron launch of the year — more than the small-lift rocket has ever flown in a single calendar year.

The mission, named "Night Owls Never Sleep", delivered Synspective's 14th StriX synthetic aperture radar (SAR) satellite directly into a 569-kilometre low Earth orbit. It came just 13 days after Electron's 100th flight, a pace that underlines how far the world's most frequently flown small-lift rocket has come since its first, tentative test flight in 2017. For Synspective, the flight extended a flawless record: Rocket Lab has now launched all 14 of the company's StriX satellites, without a single failure, and remains the sole launch provider for a constellation that is booked to keep growing through 2031.

## A Thanksgiving-night liftoff from Mahia

The countdown ended at 1:15 p.m. New Zealand Daylight Time on Friday, 27 November 2026 — 00:15 UTC, or 7:15 p.m. Eastern Time on Thursday, 26 November, Thanksgiving Day in the United States. Electron rose from Launch Complex 1, Rocket Lab's private orbital launch site on the Mahia Peninsula on New Zealand's North Island, and flew a southerly track over the Pacific before its kick stage placed the StriX satellite into its planned orbit, 569 kilometres (354 miles) above Earth and inclined 38 degrees to the equator.

The 38-degree inclination is a deliberate choice rather than a default. Most Earth-observation satellites fly in near-polar, Sun-synchronous orbits that pass over every latitude on a regular schedule. A 38-degree orbit cannot see the poles at all, but it sweeps repeatedly over the mid-latitudes — the band where most of humanity lives, builds and farms — giving Synspective more frequent revisits over Japan, East Asia, North America and Europe. It is the same logic that has shaped the company's recent flights: every StriX launch with a published orbit since October 2025 has gone to a tilted plane between 37 and 50.2 degrees, while the earliest satellites in the constellation flew Sun-synchronous. Synspective's first satellite, StriX-1, launched in September 2022 into a 574-kilometre Sun-synchronous orbit; StriX-2 followed in December 2024 at 570 kilometres and 97.3 degrees. The newer, tilted orbits trade polar coverage for denser coverage of the regions the company's customers care about most, and the satellites destined for Sun-synchronous planes are among those Synspective has booked from 2028.

Shortly after deployment, Synspective confirmed that the satellite had reached its target orbit and deployed its solar arrays — the company announced it had "spread its wings" — and said testing and commissioning would run over the coming months to verify the spacecraft's observation capabilities before it enters service.

Rocket Lab's chief executive, Sir Peter Beck, marked the milestone on X with a characteristically brief verdict: "100% mission success!" He added that "'Night Owls Never Sleep' was our 22nd launch of the year — a new annual launch record for Electron."

## What StriX sees that cameras cannot

The payload is the heart of the story, and it is not a camera. StriX carries a synthetic aperture radar, an instrument that builds images from radio waves rather than visible light. A radar satellite transmits microwave pulses toward the ground and records the echoes, synthesising a large virtual antenna from the spacecraft's motion to resolve fine detail. Because microwaves pass through cloud and need no sunlight, a SAR satellite can image the same patch of ground at midnight, in a storm, or through the smoke of a wildfire — exactly the conditions under which an optical satellite is blind.

That all-weather, day-and-night capability is why the StriX constellation exists, and why its customers keep coming back. Synspective describes its satellites as serving urban development planning, construction and infrastructure monitoring, and disaster response. Rocket Lab's own mission summary adds maritime domain awareness and government and commercial intelligence to the list of applications. When an earthquake strikes, a flood spreads, or a volcano erupts, the first usable satellite images often come from radar, because the weather over a disaster zone is rarely cooperative and the need for information does not wait for daylight. The same persistence matters for slower work: tracking millimetre-scale subsidence beneath a dam or a railway embankment, watching a port fill with ships, or measuring how a city spreads across farmland month by month.

The 14th StriX satellite joins a constellation that Synspective is building toward a specific goal: the ability to image any location on Earth within hours, day or night and through any weather. A single radar satellite revisits a given spot only every few days; a constellation of dozens, spread across several orbital planes, can cut that wait to hours. That is the commercial logic behind the 47 missions Synspective has now booked with Rocket Lab, and behind the steady drumbeat of launches — this was the company's 14th dedicated Electron flight, and Rocket Lab says another 33 dedicated missions remain on the manifest, scheduled annually through 2031.

There is a counting quirk worth noting for anyone keeping score of the constellation. Rocket Lab's tally of 14 Synspective launches includes the two early prototype satellites, StriX-α and StriX-β, so it does not line up exactly with Synspective's own satellite numbering. Either way, the operational point stands: every StriX spacecraft ever launched has ridden an Electron, and every one of those launches has succeeded.

## A record year, thirteen days after a record flight

The numbers behind this launch tell the story of Electron's year. "Night Owls Never Sleep" was Rocket Lab's 22nd launch of 2026 and its 101st mission overall, extending Electron's lead as the world's most frequently launched small-lift orbital rocket. It was also, by Rocket Lab's account, a new annual record for the vehicle — more launches in one year than in any year since Electron's first flight in 2017.

The milestone arrives hard on the heels of another one. On 14 November 2026, Electron flew its 100th mission, "The Mountain God Rises", carrying an Earth-observation satellite for the Japanese company iQPS. Rocket Lab says Electron reached 100 launches faster than any other commercially developed rocket, in under a decade, and that 100 additional Electron missions were already booked at the time of the centenary flight. The 99th mission, <a href="/blog/rocket-lab-99th-mission-sea-god-surveys-iqps/">Rocket Lab 99th mission</a> flown earlier in November, also carried an iQPS satellite, meaning three consecutive Electron flights in the space of a fortnight served Japanese Earth-observation customers — a small illustration of how thoroughly the smallsat launch market has tilted toward dedicated, single-customer rides.

The 101-mission total includes both of Electron's careers. According to Space.com, 92 of the 101 missions were orbital flights with Electron, and nine were suborbital flights of HASTE, the suborbital test-bed variant Rocket Lab flies for defence and hypersonics customers. Through the end of 2025, Rocket Lab said Electron had completed 75 successful missions and deployed more than 200 spacecraft, a tally that has grown steadily through 2026 as the cadence climbed.

That cadence is the real story of the year. Electron's first flight, "It's a Test", lifted off on 25 May 2017 and did not reach orbit. The rocket's first successful orbital flight followed in January 2018, and for years the company flew a handful of missions annually while it learned to build, test and launch at scale. The jump to 22 launches in a single year — a new record, set with more than a month of 2026 still to run at the time of the flight — reflects a production and launch operation that has matured from a startup proving a concept into a scheduled service that constellation operators can plan around. For Synspective, that reliability is the product: the company signed a 20-launch deal with Rocket Lab in September 2026, the largest commercial contract for Electron to date, making it Rocket Lab's largest launch customer with 47 total missions booked.

## Electron's road to 101

The 101st flight is the latest stop on a nine-year climb from a single test launch to the busiest small rocket in the world.

- **25 May 2017** — Electron's first flight, "It's a Test", lifts off from Mahia but fails to reach orbit.
- **January 2018** — Electron reaches orbit for the first time, beginning its career as a commercial smallsat launcher.
- **2020** — Rocket Lab becomes Synspective's sole launch provider, beginning a partnership that now spans 14 flights.
- **September 2022** — StriX-1, Synspective's first satellite, reaches a 574-kilometre Sun-synchronous orbit.
- **14 November 2026** — Electron flies its 100th mission, <a href="/blog/rocket-lab-electron-100th-launch-november-14-2026/">Rocket Lab Electron 100th launch</a>, carrying an iQPS Earth-observation satellite — the fastest any commercially developed rocket has reached 100 launches, according to Rocket Lab.
- **27 November 2026** — "Night Owls Never Sleep" becomes the 101st Electron mission and the 22nd launch of the year, a new annual record.

## The owl names

There is a joke hiding in the mission name, and it is one Rocket Lab has been telling for years. StriX takes its name from *Strix*, the genus of owls, and Rocket Lab gives its StriX flights owl-themed mission names — a nod to birds that hunt at night, much as a radar satellite works in darkness. "Night Owls Never Sleep" follows "Ten Owl of Ten" in June 2026, "Viva La StriX" in May 2026 and "Eight Days a Week" in March 2026, among others. The naming habit has become a small signature of the partnership: each new owl marks another satellite in a constellation that is, quite literally, built to see in the dark.

The naming is light-hearted, but the underlying business is serious. Dedicated launch — one customer, one rocket, one orbit — is Electron's defining offer, and it is what sets the vehicle apart from rideshare missions, where a satellite must accept another customer's orbit and schedule. For a constellation operator building out a specific orbital shell, the ability to choose the exact inclination and altitude, and to launch on a date that fits the constellation plan rather than a rideshare manifest, is worth the premium of a dedicated flight. Synspective's 47 booked missions are the strongest evidence that the model works: no other customer has committed to Electron at that scale.

## Why a small rocket's record year matters

Electron's record year is also a marker for the wider smallsat industry. BryceTech reported in its Smallsats by the Numbers 2026 report that a record 4,466 small satellites launched in 2025, representing 98 percent of all spacecraft launched that year, with low Earth orbit broadband megaconstellations accounting for 79 percent of those deployments. Within that flood of small spacecraft, dedicated flights like "Night Owls Never Sleep" serve the operators who cannot use a megaconstellation's orbit or schedule: Earth-observation companies that need specific inclinations, technology demonstrators that need a precise altitude, and government payloads that need a particular plane.

Rocket Lab is not standing still while Electron sets records. The company is developing Neutron, a medium-lift rocket intended to carry much larger payloads and to compete for bigger missions, including constellation deployment at a scale Electron cannot reach. Neutron's debut has slipped to 2027, a <a href="/blog/rocket-lab-neutron-delay-2027/">Rocket Lab Neutron delay to 2027</a> disclosed earlier this year, and the company is also working through a conditional loan of up to 300 million dollars from the Pentagon alongside Stoke Space to support its launch infrastructure. While Neutron is finished, Electron is the workhorse paying the bills and building the customer relationships — and every record year makes the case for the larger rocket stronger, because it demonstrates that Rocket Lab can manufacture, launch and recover at a cadence few other companies can match.

For New Zealand, the launch is also a reminder of how central Mahia has become to the global space industry. Launch Complex 1, a private orbital launch site on a remote peninsula on the North Island, now hosts one of the highest-cadence launch operations in the world. The 101st Electron mission lifted off from the same pad complex that hosted the 100th thirteen days earlier, and the site's ability to turn around missions in days rather than months is a large part of why Electron can sustain a record year.

## Recent StriX launches: the constellation takes shape

The table below puts "Night Owls Never Sleep" in the context of the StriX flights that preceded it. The pattern is consistent: similar altitudes, tilted mid-latitude orbits, and a steady tempo of roughly one launch every few months through 2025 and 2026.

| Mission | Launch | Altitude | Inclination |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Owl New World | 14 Oct 2025 | 583 km | 42° |
| Eight Days a Week | 20 Mar 2026 | 573 km | 50.2° |
| Viva La StriX | 22 May 2026 | 572 km | 44.8° |
| Ten Owl of Ten | 26 Jun 2026 | 552 km | 42° |
| Night Owls Never Sleep | 27 Nov 2026 | 569 km | 38° |

The first two StriX satellites flew differently: StriX-1 went to a 574-kilometre Sun-synchronous orbit in September 2022, and StriX-2 to 570 kilometres at 97.3 degrees in December 2024. The shift to tilted orbits reflects the constellation's maturing design — a mix of orbital planes that together cover the mid-latitudes densely while the Sun-synchronous satellites, including those booked from 2028, provide the polar and global coverage the tilted planes cannot.

## What happens next

The new StriX satellite now enters months of testing and commissioning before it begins routine imaging, joining thirteen siblings already in orbit. Synspective has 33 more dedicated Electron launches booked through 2031, so the owl-named missions will keep coming — and with Electron's 2026 record already set, the question for 2027 is whether Rocket Lab can push the annual tally higher still while it brings Neutron to the pad.

The wider launch calendar gives Electron plenty of company. SpaceX's Falcon 9 is flying at a pace no rocket in history has matched, China's Long March family continues its own high tempo — the Long March 6A lofted the Yaogan-50 04 remote-sensing satellite on 27 November, the same day Electron flew — and new small launchers are fighting for the same constellation customers. But Electron's achievement is distinct: it is the only small-lift rocket to have flown more than 100 times, the only one to have done it in under a decade, and the only one with a single customer committed to 47 flights. The 101st launch did not just add one more satellite to a constellation. It confirmed that a rocket once dismissed as too small to matter has become the metronome of the smallsat era — and that its busiest year is still, by the calendar, unfinished.
