On December 1, 2026, one of the most productive scientific instruments ever flown will stop watching Earth. NASA announced on November 17 that its Terra satellite, the flagship of the Earth Observing System, will cease collecting science data on that date, ending a mission that was designed to last six years and instead ran for 27. The spacecraft's systems are aging and its orbit is drifting, the agency said, and the time has come to bring the mission to a close. The Terra team will use the spacecraft's remaining fuel to lower its orbit and then power off all of its systems in January 2027.

It is a quiet ending for a satellite that never made headlines the way a Mars rover or a moon rocket does, and that is precisely the point. Terra spent more than a quarter of a century doing the unglamorous, indispensable work of measuring the planet we live on: how its forests burn and regrow, how its ice retreats, how dust and smoke cross oceans, how pollution drifts around the globe. Its data have been cited in more than 27,000 scientific publications, informed decisions on public safety, agriculture and water resources, and underpinned commercial, societal and humanitarian applications that most of the people who benefit from them will never hear about. When the last observation is taken on December 1, the satellite will have circled the planet more than 100,000 times.

Terra is not failing. It is being retired, on schedule, after a career that outlasted every expectation its builders had for it. That distinction matters, because the story of Terra is not really a story about an ending. It is a story about what a single well-built spacecraft can do when it keeps working for four and a half times longer than anyone planned — and about what happens to the science when it finally stops.

## The announcement

The news came in a short post on NASA Science on November 17, 2026, written by Kate Ramsayer. "After 27 years of providing insights into Earth's interconnected atmosphere, lands, oceans, and biology, NASA's Terra satellite will stop collecting science data on Dec. 1," the agency said. "Terra, a workhorse of the Earth Observing System, was designed for a six-year mission. After operating for more than four times that, the spacecraft's systems are aging and its orbit is drifting."

The end will come in two steps. First, on December 1 — day 335 of 2026, as NASA's Earthdata office records it — all new observations cease. Some science data products may still be delivered after that date, processed from observations already taken, but Terra will not record anything new. Then the Terra team will carry out the decommissioning process, using the spacecraft's remaining fuel to lower its orbit before powering off all spacecraft systems in January 2027. The last contact with the spacecraft is planned for January 2027.

What does not end is the archive. NASA says Terra's data records will continue to be free and available to the public, and the Earthdata office has been preparing users for the transition for months, publishing guidance on alternative products and holding a webinar on navigating a post-MODIS world. NASA Earthdata currently serves more than 200 unique land products derived from Terra and Aqua MODIS data, and that 27-year historical archive will remain available even as the flow of new observations stops.

The date itself is earlier than the one NASA had been projecting. The agency's history article "Terra: The End of an Era," published in December 2025, had projected Terra's end-of-science activities in February 2027. The November 17 and November 19, 2026 notices superseded that projection and fixed the end of new observations at December 1, 2026. The satellite's own mission website repeated the date in a November 19 post titled "Terra Science Ends on December 1, 2026."

## Five eyes on a changing planet

Terra's power was never a single instrument. It was the combination. The satellite carries five remote sensors, built by the United States, Japan and Canada, that look at the same planet in different ways at the same time — and because they fly together, their measurements can be combined into a picture no single sensor could produce.

The best known of the five is MODIS, the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer, which images the entire surface of the Earth every one to two days in 36 spectral bands. MODIS is the instrument behind the daily global maps of clouds, fires, vegetation, ocean colour and ice that have become the background texture of modern Earth science. It took Terra's first image, and it is the reason researchers could track a wildfire's spread in the morning and measure the smoke it put into the atmosphere in the afternoon.

ASTER, the Advanced Spaceborne Thermal Emission and Reflection Radiometer, was provided by Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. It creates high-resolution images of clouds, ice, water and the land surface across 14 spectral bands, from visible light to the thermal infrared, using three sensor subsystems: visible and near-infrared, shortwave infrared, and thermal infrared. Its shortwave infrared subsystem began to fail in 2008, when increased thermal resistance in its cryocooler drove detector temperatures up; on January 12, 2009, ASTER managers declared the shortwave infrared channel no longer functional, and data gathered after April 2008 was declared unusable. The instrument kept working in its other channels.

CERES, the Clouds and the Earth's Radiant Energy System, measures the balance between the sunlight Earth absorbs and the heat it radiates back to space — the fundamental accounting of the planet's energy budget, and one of the key numbers in understanding how the climate is changing. It flew as two units, FM1 and FM2; the FM2 instrument was shut down in January 2025 as battery constraints tightened, and parts of CERES had already been switched off to conserve power.

MISR, the Multi-angle Imaging SpectroRadiometer, looks at the same scene from nine different angles as Terra passes overhead, which lets scientists measure the height of clouds and plumes, the size and shape of airborne particles, and the structure of vegetation in three dimensions. MOPITT, the Measurements of Pollution in the Troposphere, provided by Canada, tracked carbon monoxide and other pollutants as they moved around the globe; it was switched to safe mode on February 1, 2025 and turned off on April 9, 2025, also to conserve power.

Together, the five instruments gave researchers something no earlier mission had offered: simultaneous measurements of Earth's atmosphere, land and water, taken from the same platform, day after day, for decades. That simultaneity is what turned Terra from a collection of sensors into a system — and it is why the end of the mission is felt across so many fields at once, from wildfire management to climate research to disaster response. The same kind of long, multi-instrument record is what makes satellite studies of the deep Earth possible, too, as in the work using ESA's Swarm satellites to map iron hidden in the planet's mantle, described in our earlier report on <a href="/blog/esa-swarm-deep-earth-iron/">Iron From Space: How ESA's Swarm Satellites Mapped the Hidden Metal Heart of Earth's Mantle</a>.

## A satellite built for six years

Terra began on a cold December evening in 1999. It launched from Vandenberg Air Force Base in California on December 18, 1999, at 18:57:39 UTC, aboard an Atlas IIAS rocket, and was placed into a near-polar, Sun-synchronous orbit at an altitude of about 705 kilometres, crossing the equator at 10:30 in the morning on its descending pass. It began collecting data on February 24, 2000.

The name comes from the Latin word for Earth. NASA held a naming contest among high school students across the United States, and the winning essay was submitted by Sasha Jones of Brentwood, Missouri. The identifier AM-1 refers to that morning equator crossing: Terra was the morning satellite of the Earth Observing System, and it was the flagship and the first satellite of the system. It was followed by Aqua, launched in 2002, and Aura, launched in 2004, which cross the equator in the afternoon.

At 4,864 kilograms — 10,723 pounds — Terra is about the size of a small school bus, and it was built to last six years. Six years was the plan, the budget, and the engineering assumption. Everything after that was borrowed time, and the mission kept borrowing it, year after year, for more than two decades.

The satellite's longevity was not luck. It was the product of a spacecraft that was overbuilt by the standards of its day, an operations team that learned to work around every failure, and a slow, deliberate process of shedding load as the power system aged: instruments switched off one by one, maneuvers skipped, the orbit allowed to drift. By the end, Terra was a diminished version of itself — but it was still taking measurements nobody else could take, and still producing data that researchers relied on every day.

## The record it built

Over a quarter of a century, Terra compiled a detailed record of land use changes, wildfire behavior, dust and smoke transport, atmospheric composition and ice cover. That record is the mission's real legacy, and it is measured not in spacecraft milestones but in the things it let people see and do.

Fire managers used Terra's daily fire detections to track wildfires as they spread and to map the scars they left behind, including the burn scar of California's Kincade fire in 2019. Disaster teams used its images of the Deepwater Horizon oil spill in the Gulf of Mexico on May 1, 2010, and of Hurricane Karl approaching Mexico in September 2010. Volcanologists used it to map the ash that the 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha'apai eruption spread over Australia. On December 18, 2018 — Terra's nineteenth birthday in orbit — its instruments caught a fireball streaking over the Bering Sea. It imaged the solar eclipse of August 1, 2008, sweeping across Russia, Norway and the Arctic Ocean, and the snow that the European winter storms of 2009–2010 laid across Great Britain.

Behind those images is a slower, deeper record. Terra's instruments tracked the spread of pollution around the globe, with studies using its data to examine trends in global carbon monoxide and aerosol pollution. Its measurements of clouds and radiation fed directly into the accounting of Earth's energy balance. Its vegetation and land-cover records documented how forests, farms and cities changed across 27 years — a baseline against which every later measurement is judged. NASA says the data have informed decisions on public safety, agriculture and water resources, as well as a diverse set of commercial, societal and humanitarian applications.

That is what a 27-year record buys: not a single discovery, but the ability to tell change from noise. A five-year satellite can show you a fire. A 27-year satellite can show you whether fires are getting worse, where, and how fast — and it can do it with the same instruments, calibrated the same way, year after year. When Terra stops, that unbroken line ends, and the work of continuing it falls to other satellites, other instruments, and other datasets that will have to be stitched together to match what one spacecraft did alone.

## How it survived so long

Terra's 27 years were not 27 easy years. The spacecraft was hit, hacked, and worn down, and it kept working because its operators kept finding ways around each new problem.

The earliest trouble was almost comic in its persistence. After launch, operators found that high-energy protons — the kind found over the South Atlantic Anomaly and near the poles — could induce single-event upsets that tripped the Motor Drive Assembly's built-in test equipment and shut the assembly down. These false shutdowns happened 12 to 14 times a month, and the operations team eventually automated the recovery to reduce the impact. It was the kind of fault that would have been a crisis on a short mission and became routine on a long one.

In June and October 2008, the spacecraft was targeted by hackers who gained unauthorised access to its command and control systems. They issued no commands, and the mission continued, but the incident is a reminder that even a climate satellite flies through contested territory.

The hardest blow came on October 13, 2009, when Terra suffered a single battery cell failure and a battery heater control anomaly, likely the result of a micrometeoroid or orbital debris strike. Engineers kept the battery warm by using its own charge and discharge cycles — a workaround that kept the spacecraft alive and became part of the mission's operating lore.

Then came the slow decline. On February 27, 2020, the flight operations team conducted Terra's last inclination adjust maneuver, because the satellite's remaining fuel could no longer support them. With no more such maneuvers, Terra's mean local time began to drift starting in April 2021, and with it, the quality of its data began to decrease — the 10:30 a.m. crossing that defined the mission was slipping later into the morning. By October 2022, Terra had started to exceed a 10:15 a.m. crossing, and on October 12 and October 19 the flight operators performed constellation exit maneuvers to lower the orbit to 694 kilometres and leave the constellation it had flown in for two decades. Decommissioning finally began in 2025, with instruments switched off one by one to save power, until the November 2026 announcement set the final date.

## What ends, and what does not

The end of new observations is not the end of Terra's science. The 27-year archive remains, and it will keep producing research for years — NASA notes that the data collected by Terra will ultimately become a long-term global dataset, and the historical record will remain freely available to the public through NASA's Earthdata system and its distributed archive centers.

What changes is the workflow of the people who use the data. NASA's Earthdata office has told users who rely on updated observations to transition to alternative products as soon as possible, and has published transition resources for each of Terra's instruments — ASTER, CERES, MISR, MODIS and MOPITT — pointing to replacement datasets from other platforms. The office's webinar on continuity after MODIS walks users through the alternative products and the tools designed to help them move their algorithms and workflows. With more than 200 unique land products derived from Terra and Aqua MODIS data in circulation, the transition is a substantial undertaking, and it has been under way for months.

The gap Terra leaves is real but not unfillable. Other satellites carry on parts of its work: its sister spacecraft Aqua and Aura, the Suomi NPP and NOAA-20 satellites that carry the next generation of MODIS-like instruments, and the Landsat programme that continues the land record. None of them is Terra, and none of them sees the planet in exactly the same way, with the same five instruments flying together — but together they keep the record going, and the calibration work that links the old record to the new one is part of what the transition effort is for.

## The company it keeps

Terra is not the only veteran NASA spacecraft to bow out in 2026, and the comparison puts its achievement in perspective. In October, NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory made its final observation, ending a mission that outlived its two-year design life more than tenfold — a story we covered in <a href="/blog/nasa-swift-observatory-ends-22-years-gamma-ray-bursts/">After 22 Years Chasing Cosmic Explosions, NASA's Swift Observatory Takes Its Final Bow</a>. Swift's spacecraft will fall back to Earth and burn up within weeks. Terra, by contrast, will be lowered and switched off in a controlled decommissioning, its orbit managed to the end.

| Mission | Design life | Actual service | End |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Terra (EOS AM-1) | 6 years | 27 years | Science ends Dec 1, 2026; powered off Jan 2027 |
| Swift Observatory | 2 years | ~22 years | Final observation Oct 20, 2026; re-entry within weeks |
| Terra vs design | — | 4.5× design life | Controlled decommissioning |
| Swift vs design | — | 11× design life | Uncontrolled re-entry |

The two endings share a pattern that says something about how NASA builds spacecraft: conservatively, with margins that turn out to be far larger than the paper requirement. Terra was built for six years and gave 27. Swift was built for two and gave 22. Both missions were supposed to be brief, and both became institutions, outliving the programmes that launched them and the careers of many of the people who built them.

2026 has been a hard year for NASA's working fleet in other ways, too. In November, the agency confirmed that its first commercial debris-inspection demonstration was over after the SSPICY Otter spacecraft fell silent during commissioning, its radio switched off by its own fault-protection system with no way to switch it back on — a loss we reported in <a href="/blog/nasa-sspicy-otter-communications-loss-november-2026/">NASA SSPICY Otter Communications Loss November 2026 Ends First Debris-Inspection Demo</a>. Terra's ending is the opposite kind of loss: not a failure, but a retirement, chosen and scheduled, after a career that exceeded every expectation.

## Why it matters

There is a temptation to treat the end of a satellite mission as a technical footnote — a date, a decommissioning plan, a note in an archive. Terra deserves better than that, because its work was never really about the satellite. It was about the record, and the record is about the planet.

For 27 years, Terra watched the same Earth every day, with the same eyes, and wrote down what it saw. That continuity is the rarest thing in science: a measurement that does not change while the thing being measured does. It is what lets a researcher say, with confidence, that a forest is smaller than it was, that a glacier is thinner, that the air over a city is cleaner or dirtier than a decade ago. Without it, every new observation is a snapshot with nothing to compare against.

The record will survive the spacecraft. The data are free, the archive is open, and the papers that cite Terra will keep being written for years. But the line itself — the unbroken daily watch — ends on December 1, 2026. The next satellite will pick up the work, and the scientists will stitch the records together, and the planet will keep changing whether anyone is watching or not. For 27 years, Terra was watching. That is a long time for a machine built to last six. It is not long enough for the planet it watched, but it was long enough to change how we understand it — and that is the measure of the mission.
