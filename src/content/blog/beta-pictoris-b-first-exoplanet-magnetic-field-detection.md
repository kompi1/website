For more than three decades, astronomers have catalogued the masses, sizes, and orbits of thousands of worlds beyond our solar system. They have read the chemistry of alien atmospheres, measured winds howling across hot Jupiters, and even mapped the weather on brown dwarfs. But one of the most fundamental properties of any planet — its magnetic field — has remained stubbornly out of reach. No one had ever directly measured the magnetism of a single exoplanet.

That may now have changed. In a paper posted to arXiv on September 15, 2026, a team led by Kevin Ortiz Ceballos of the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics reports that they have caught radio bursts streaming directly from Beta Pictoris b, a young gas giant orbiting a star 63 light-years from Earth. The signals bear the unmistakable signature of auroral emission — the same physics behind Jupiter's polar light shows — and their frequency pegs the planet's magnetic field at roughly 1.25 kilogauss, about 2,000 times stronger than Earth's.

If confirmed, it is the first direct measurement of an exoplanet's magnetic field, and it opens an entirely new window on the interiors of distant worlds.

## A radio signal with a planetary fingerprint

The discovery came from MeerKAT, the South African Radio Astronomy Observatory's array of 64 radio dishes in the Northern Cape. MeerKAT is among the most sensitive radio telescopes on Earth, and for this project it was pointed at Beta Pictoris b across a series of observing sessions in two different frequency bands. In every session, the planet glowed.

"It turns out it's actually active most of the time," says co-author Edo Berger, an astronomer at Harvard University and the Center for Astrophysics, Harvard and Smithsonian. Sometimes the planet flared up in what Berger describes as a dramatic huff; even in its quieter moments, less energetic radio signals kept emanating from the system.

The researchers concluded that the radio emission originates from a process called electron cyclotron maser instability — ECMI for short. It is the same mechanism that produces the auroral radio bursts of Jupiter, Earth, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, as well as some planet-like cool brown dwarfs. When charged particles accelerated along a planet's magnetic field lines spiral into the atmosphere near the poles, they emit radio waves at a frequency set directly by the strength of the magnetic field. In other words, the radio frequency is a speedometer for magnetism.

At the highest radio frequency detected from Beta Pictoris b, the electrons were looping around 3.5 billion cycles per second. That maximum frequency translates straight into a field strength: about 1.25 kilogauss. Earth's magnetic field at the surface is roughly half a gauss, so the exoplanet's field is on the order of two thousand times stronger. "This constitutes the first direct measurement of the magnetic field strength of an exoplanet and is consistent with dynamo scaling predictions for a young, massive giant planet," the authors write in the paper.

## A fishing expedition that paid off

The result was not born of a guarantee. "It was a bit of a fishing expedition," Berger admits. The idea grew from an observation of brown dwarfs — objects not much larger than Jupiter that do not shine very brightly in visible light but can dazzle at radio wavelengths with signals suggestive of magnetic activity. If brown dwarfs could broadcast their magnetism in radio waves, Berger's team reasoned, perhaps the largest gas giants — which can closely resemble brown dwarfs — would behave the same way. And if they did, astronomers could finally image planetary magnetic fields directly.

After poring through about a hundred candidates, the researchers settled on Beta Pictoris b. The choice was anything but random. Beta Pictoris b is huge, roughly 12 times the mass of Jupiter, and young — only about 25 million years old. Youth matters: young planets are still hot from formation, and hot, active interiors drive powerful magnetic dynamos. It was discovered back in 2008 with the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope, and it orbits its host star at roughly the distance Saturn loops around our sun, completing one orbit in about 24 years.

Just as important, Beta Pictoris b sits far enough from its parent star to avoid being drowned in stellar glare. The most massive planet in the system "reaches an angular separation of up to 0.55 arcseconds during its 24-year orbit, and the host star is magnetically quiet, making the system an ideal target for radio observations," Ceballos and his colleagues note. The Beta Pictoris system, located in the constellation Pictor some 63 light-years away, is one of the best-studied young planetary systems in the sky, with an extensive disk of gas and dust and at least two known planets. Everything about it lined up.

## An auroral show far more extreme than Earth's

The radio pattern the team detected across four observing sessions points to aurora — electrons swirling at the planet's poles, trapped and accelerated by the giant magnetic field. It is the same basic phenomenon that paints Earth's northern and southern lights, scaled up to an extreme that has no equal in our solar system.

"We have a completely new window on planets outside of our solar system for the first time," says Berger.

But independent astronomers urge a note of caution. Joe Callingham, a radio astronomer at the Netherlands Institute for Radio Astronomy who was not involved in the study, agrees that auroral activity is a "plausible model" for the signal — but adds that the true "smoking gun" will come with more data demonstrating a regular, repeating pattern. If these conclusions hold up in peer review, he says, "this would be a slam dunk."

Imagining the aurora itself is a vivid exercise in extremes. Earth's polar light shows are delicate curtains of green and red. Whatever light show Beta Pictoris b puts on would be driven by a field 2,000 times stronger, with electrons spiraling at billions of cycles per second. As Callingham puts it: "Maybe it'd be a great view for ten seconds, I don't know, before you get bombarded by radiation."

## Why a magnetic field matters: the dynamo and habitability

Planetary magnetic fields are generated by three ingredients, scientists believe: an electrically conducting fluid inside the planet, convection, and the planet's rotation. Inside Earth, that fluid is the liquid outer core of mostly iron and nickel, churning in constant motion and carrying heat from deep inside toward the surface. The convective motion carries electrical currents, which Earth's rotation twists into a corkscrew pattern — and the motion of those currents generates the magnetic bubble that envelops our planet.

Earth's magnetic bubble is often lauded as a key ingredient in our planet's habitability. It deflects the gusty solar wind to our poles, shielding the atmosphere — and everything beneath it — from a direct blast of charged particles. Without that shield, Earth's atmosphere might have been stripped away long ago, as seems to have happened on Mars after that planet's dynamo died.

But strong magnetic fields are not always benign. As stellar winds and radiation accelerate charged particles toward a planet's poles, the energy delivered can strip away atmospheric mass, according to Jackie Villadsen, an astronomer at Bucknell University. Giant magnetospheres are laboratories for extremes: they tell scientists how planets hold onto their atmospheres, how interiors evolve, and where the boundaries of habitability truly lie.

That is what makes a direct magnetic measurement so powerful. Until now, everything astronomers knew about exoplanet magnetism was indirect. By studying magnetic fields around other stars' planets, scientists hope to probe exoplanets' inner structures, paint a clearer picture of planetary evolution, and someday build a stronger measure for the probability of life on other worlds. Beta Pictoris b's 1.25-kilogauss field is consistent with what dynamo theory predicts for a young, massive giant planet — a reassuring sign that the models work beyond our own backyard.

## The long, quiet hunt — and why it was so hard

The difficulty of this measurement cannot be overstated. Astronomers have catalogued more than 6,000 exoplanets, but for most of them, only the barest facts — mass, radius, maybe a few molecules in the atmosphere — are known. Magnetic fields were one of the last holdouts. "Trying to study magnetic fields of planets in other solar systems is pretty dang difficult right now," says Villadsen, who was not involved in the study. "We have very, very little data from anyone on it."

Previous attempts had to rely on proxies and theory. Some researchers have looked for radio emission from exoplanets in star-planet interactions, but those signals were tied to the host star, not the planet itself. The Beta Pictoris b detection is different: the weather.com analysis notes this is the first radio emission unequivocally associated with an exoplanet itself rather than its host star. The planet was directly imaged, and the emission is localized to the planet's position.

That matters because the host star here cooperates. Beta Pictoris is magnetically quiet, so there is no confusion between stellar and planetary signals. The planet's wide separation from the star — it orbits at about 8 times the Earth–Sun distance — kept it cleanly separated on the sky. Everything about the system conspired to make the measurement possible.

## What comes next: peer review and the wider search

The paper is a preprint, posted to arXiv on September 15, 2026, and it has not yet been peer-reviewed. The next step is straightforward: independent scrutiny, follow-up observations, and the search for the telltale repeating pattern that would turn a plausible model into a confirmed detection.

The team is already looking ahead. After this paper, Berger's group hopes to expand the search to similar planetary systems — other young, massive gas giants orbiting quiet stars at wide separations. Telescope time is fiercely competitive, but Berger suspects the detection itself helps make the case: "Now we have really good motivation to go to these telescopes and say, 'Look, there's great payoff for these observations.'"

Others in the field see a broader turning point. "I think it's really kind of heralding the beginning of a broader kind of radio astronomy for exoplanetary science," says Sebastian Pineda, an astrophysicist at the University of Colorado Boulder who was not involved in the study.

Still, the road to weaker fields — the kind on Earth-like worlds — remains long. Earth's ionosphere effectively blocks ground-based telescopes from detecting the low-frequency radio emissions that Earth-strength magnetic fields would produce. Researchers are beginning to investigate workarounds, including placing radio telescopes in space or on the far side of the Moon, where Earth's ionosphere and radio interference cannot reach. Progress there, Pineda notes, may take several decades.

## How the ECMI fingerprint works — and why astronomers trust it

The credibility of the detection rests on a physical mechanism that space physicists know intimately. Electron cyclotron maser instability is not an exotic hypothesis: it is the everyday engine behind the auroral radio bursts of every magnetized planet in our solar system. When charged particles accelerated along magnetic field lines plunge toward a planet's poles, they spiral around those field lines at the cyclotron frequency — a frequency set directly and only by the local magnetic field strength. Coherent radio emission generated by that spiraling population escapes at or just above that frequency. Measure the frequency, and you have measured the field.

There is no other known way to produce that specific radio signature at those frequencies. That is why the team could be confident that the emission comes from the planet itself, not from its star or from background sources. The host star Beta Pictoris is magnetically quiet, and the emission is localized at the planet's position on the sky. The paper's authors note that this is the first radio emission unequivocally associated with an exoplanet itself rather than its host star — a distinction that matters, because earlier hints of exoplanetary radio signals had often turned out to be stellar activity in disguise.

The auroral interpretation also explains the signal's character. Jupiter — the strongest magnetosphere in our solar system — has been crackling with decametric radio bursts since astronomers first picked them up in 1955, modulated by the planet's rotation and by its volcanic moon Io. Beta Pictoris b appears to be doing something similar, only on a vastly grander scale: a field of 1.25 kilogauss dwarfs anything in our own solar system. Even Jupiter's mighty magnetosphere is modest by comparison.

## The Beta Pictoris system: a young laboratory next door

Part of what makes this detection so compelling is the target. The Beta Pictoris system has been a celebrity in astronomy for four decades. In 1984, observations revealed that the young star was surrounded by an enormous disk of gas and dust — the first debris disk ever imaged around another star. That discovery turned Beta Pictoris into the archetype of a young planetary system caught in the act of formation, a place where planets are still emerging from the raw material that made them.

The planet itself, Beta Pictoris b, was directly imaged in 2008 with the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope, one of the earliest exoplanets ever seen directly rather than inferred. Its mass is estimated between 9 and 13 times that of Jupiter, making it one of the heftiest directly imaged planets known. It orbits at roughly 8 times the Earth–Sun distance — similar to Saturn's place in our solar system — taking about 24 years to circle its star. The system also hosts at least one more planet, Beta Pictoris c, and the famous debris disk may eventually settle into a belt of icy bodies much like our own Kuiper Belt.

At only 23 to 25 million years old, the system is a cosmic infant. Planets this young are still glowing with the heat of their formation, their interiors churning with the energy of gravitational contraction. That youth is precisely what makes Beta Pictoris b such a powerful magnetic target: dynamo theory predicts that young, massive, rapidly rotating gas giants should generate the strongest fields of all. The measured 1.25 kilogauss is, in the authors' words, consistent with dynamo scaling predictions for a young, massive giant planet — a prediction that has now met reality for the first time.

## A new sense for distant worlds

There is a pleasing symmetry to this discovery. For thirty years, exoplanet science has been a science of shadows and light — planets found because they blocked starlight, or because their atmospheres tinted it, or because they tugged their stars in tiny gravitational dances. Now astronomers have added a new sense: hearing a planet's magnetic heartbeat in radio waves.

Berger encourages his students — one of whom, lead author Kevin Ortiz Ceballos, made this very discovery — not to limit their thinking. "Just because there is some expectation doesn't mean that you shouldn't go out and make an observation that flies in the face of accepted wisdom," he says. "Sometimes it really works, and you make a new discovery."

The paper still has to survive peer review. The auroral interpretation still needs its smoking-gun pattern. But if Beta Pictoris b's radio hum holds up, astronomy will have crossed a threshold it has been approaching for decades: for the first time, we can feel the invisible architecture of another world — not its light, not its shadow, but its magnetic soul.
