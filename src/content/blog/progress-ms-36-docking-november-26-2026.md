The International Space Station is getting a Thanksgiving delivery. Russia's unpiloted Progress MS-36 cargo freighter, which lifted off from the Baikonur Cosmodrome in Kazakhstan on Tuesday, is scheduled to dock with the station on Thursday, November 26, at 12:11 UTC — 7:11 a.m. Eastern time in the United States, where the day is Thanksgiving, and 7:11 p.m. in Jakarta. If the automated rendezvous goes as planned, the freighter will ease into the Earth-facing port of the station's Rassvet module and deliver about three tons of food, fuel, water, and science gear to the seven people of Expedition 75.

It is a routine-sounding mission with an unusual cargo manifest and an unusual plan. Tucked among the food parcels and propellant is Teledroid, a humanoid robot built to test whether a machine can do the preparatory work of a spacewalk so that astronauts spend less time outside. And once the crew has unloaded the freighter, Progress MS-36 is scheduled to do something no Progress cargo ship has done before: undock from one port, fly itself to a different one, and spend the rest of its stay pushing the station's orbit higher. The two halves of the mission — unloading at one port, reboosting from another — are a direct consequence of a leak in the Russian segment that has been getting worse for years.

NASA will carry the docking live, with coverage beginning at 6:30 a.m. Eastern, about forty minutes before contact. For a station that has now been continuously occupied for more than 26 years, the arrival is one more link in a supply chain that never stops — and this week it is a busy one, with a second, much larger cargo ship due to launch from Florida on Sunday.

## A Thanksgiving delivery, right on schedule

Progress MS-36 — known to NASA as Progress 97 — launched at 10:17:46 UTC on Tuesday, November 24, atop a Soyuz-2.1b rocket from Site 31/6 at Baikonur. Liftoff came at 5:17 a.m. Eastern time, or 3:17 p.m. local time in Kazakhstan, and the Soyuz placed the freighter into the same orbital plane as the station within minutes. By the time the rocket's job was done, the spacecraft was on a two-day, roughly 48-hour chase to catch a laboratory travelling at some 28,000 kilometres per hour, about 400 kilometres above Earth.

The flight plan is the unhurried one. Russian cargo ships have flown both fast six-hour rendezvous profiles and the classic two-day profile; Progress MS-36 is on the slower track, giving flight controllers time to check the spacecraft's systems and refine its orbit before the automated docking. The target is the nadir — Earth-facing — port of the Rassvet module on the Russian segment, and the docking is planned for 12:11 UTC on Thursday.

That timing puts the arrival squarely in the middle of the American Thanksgiving holiday, a coincidence that has not gone unnoticed in the coverage of the mission. The station's crew will not be sitting down to a turkey dinner — though holiday food is part of what cargo ships carry — but the freighter's arrival on a day when much of the United States is off work is a reminder of how the station's schedule ignores weekends and holidays alike. Supplies arrive when the orbital mechanics say they arrive.

NASA's own station blog put it plainly: the unpiloted spacecraft is safely in orbit and on its way, and it will remain docked for about eight months before departing to burn up harmlessly over the Pacific Ocean. That long stay is part of what makes this mission different from a simple delivery run. Progress MS-36 is not just a truck. For much of its docked life it will serve as part of the station itself — a fuel tank, a storage room, and eventually an engine.

The crew waiting for it is Expedition 75, led by station commander Pyotr Dubrov of Roscosmos. His fellow Russian flight engineers are Anna Kikina and Sergey Teteryatnikov. The rest of the crew is Canadian Space Agency flight engineer Joshua Kutryk and NASA flight engineers Anil Menon, Jessica Watkins, and Luke Delaney. It is Kikina and Dubrov who will have the most to do with this freighter's most unusual cargo, because they are the two cosmonauts who have been training to work with the robot it carries.

## The 48-hour chase

A rendezvous in low Earth orbit is less a matter of speed than of patience. Progress MS-36 launched into a slightly lower orbit than the station, where it circles the Earth a little faster, gaining on its target with every lap. A series of carefully timed engine burns raises its orbit in steps, and in the final phase the spacecraft's Kurs automated rendezvous system takes over, steering it to a precise approach along the station's velocity vector before the docking probe slides into the Rassvet port's drogue and the latches engage.

The two-day profile gives controllers on the ground a generous margin. If a burn underperforms or a sensor disagrees with its backup, there is time to diagnose the problem, upload a correction, and try again on the next orbit. The fast six-hour profile, by contrast, leaves almost no slack. Roscosmos has used both, but for a freighter carrying a one-of-a-kind robot and a full load of propellant, the slower road is the prudent one.

## What is inside: 2,427 kilograms

Progress MS-36 is carrying 2,427 kilograms of cargo, a figure that covers everything from drinking water to the gas the crew breathes. The breakdown shows how a station is really supplied: most of the mass is not food or experiments, but the consumables that keep seven people alive and the station in its orbit.

## Progress MS-36 cargo manifest

| Cargo | Mass |
|---|---|
| Pressurised cargo (total) | 1,206 kg |
| — Maintenance and station hardware | 327 kg |
| — Science equipment and experiments | 303 kg |
| — Food | 261 kg |
| — Clothing and hygiene items | 247 kg |
| — Medical supplies | 41 kg |
| — Crew support items | 27 kg |
| Propellant for the station | 750 kg |
| Water | 420 kg |
| Pressurised gas (air and oxygen) | 51 kg |
| **Total** | **2,427 kg** |

The spacecraft itself masses about 7,280 kilograms at launch, and the mission is the 189th Progress flight since the freighter family began flying in 1978. That long lineage is part of the point. The Progress design has been resupplying space stations since the Salyut era, and while the avionics and the docking system have been modernised, the basic job has not changed: carry what the crew needs, dock itself, stay as a spare room and a fuel tank, and then leave full of rubbish to burn up on the way home.

This particular flight was a long time coming. Progress MS-36 was originally expected to launch in August, then slipped to September 9, and then to November 24. The delay traces back to an accident at Baikonur a year ago: during the launch of the crewed Soyuz MS-28 in November 2025, a service platform at the Site 31 launch complex collapsed. The pad was repaired, and flights from Site 31 resumed with the launch of Progress MS-33 in March 2026 — but the schedule for the rest of the year never fully recovered, and this freighter's launch date slid twice before it finally flew.

## Teledroid: the robot in the cargo hold

The most interesting item in the manifest is not measured in kilograms of food or litres of water. It is Teledroid, a humanoid robot that Roscosmos launched aboard Progress MS-36 for a series of experiments on the station's exterior. The idea is simple to state and hard to do: prepare worksites outside the station so that cosmonauts spend less time on spacewalks.

According to reporting on the project, Teledroid is designed to open protective casings and install equipment before a spacewalk begins — the fiddly, time-consuming preparatory tasks that currently eat into the hours a crew member can safely spend outside. The robot combines two modes of control. In one, an operator aboard the station or at mission control wears a special suit and performs movements that the robot reproduces — a technique known as motion copying. In the other, the robot executes some tasks autonomously. The developer, NPO Android Technics, working jointly with RSC Energia, describes the project as a test of technologies that support cosmonaut spacewalks, and its control architecture allows operation from either the station's pressurised compartment or a ground control point.

Teledroid did not arrive as a complete system. Several of its control elements travelled to the station earlier, aboard Progress MS-35 in September 2026: a cosmonaut workstation — described as a copy-type master device — along with a computing module and an external camera meant to give operators an outside view of the robot during its work. An exoskeleton for controlling the robot's movements was also sent up on that earlier flight. In late October, cosmonauts Pyotr Dubrov and Anna Kikina were installing the equipment and preparing initial tasks with virtual models while awaiting the physical robot.

The next steps are practical and deliberately unglamorous. Crew training for the project has included underwater rehearsals for carrying Teledroid outside, installing it, and connecting its communications during a spacewalk. The robot is planned for installation outside the Nauka module after tests aboard the station, with Kikina and Dubrov conducting the tests. Only after the installation works will Teledroid attempt its planned exterior support tasks.

It is worth putting the experiment in context. Humanoid robots have been to the station before — NASA's Robonaut 2 arrived in 2011 and spent years aboard, though its legs never flew and its work stayed inside. What is different here is the destination: the outside of the station, where temperatures swing by hundreds of degrees, radiation is unfiltered, and a dropped tool does not fall to the floor but drifts away forever. If Teledroid works, it points toward a future in which the most dangerous and exhausting parts of station maintenance are done by machines that do not breathe, do not tire, and do not care about the cold. If it does not, the lessons will still be real: every hour of spacewalk preparation a robot can absorb is an hour a human being does not have to spend in a suit.

## One freighter, two docking ports

The other first on this mission concerns where the freighter parks. The plan, as described by space historian Anatoly Zak of RussianSpaceWeb and reported by Space.com, is for Progress MS-36 to be unloaded through one Russian module and then to relocate to a different docking port so that it can later perform orbital reboosts for the station. That makes it, in Zak's words, the first cargo mission scheduled to unload its cargo and perform orbit corrections of the station at different docking ports.

The reason is a leak. The PrK transfer tunnel, which leads to the Zvezda service module, has been sealed off because of an ongoing air leak that was first documented in 2019. The leak, in a tunnel that connects Zvezda to the rest of the station, has been a persistent worry for years: it is a slow loss of the station's air, and in 2026 the Russian side closed the tunnel off permanently in June after the problem became chronic. The aft port of that sealed tunnel, however, remains the best place on the station from which to fire a freighter's engines and raise the orbit — the thrust pushes along the station's direction of travel, and the structure there is built to take it.

So Progress MS-36 will dock first at Rassvet's Earth-facing port, where the crew can open the hatch and unload it normally. Then, once the cargo is out, the freighter will undock, fly itself around the station, and dock again at the aft port of the PrK tunnel — a port it cannot be unloaded through, because the tunnel behind it is sealed, but one from which its engines can do the most good. The manoeuvre turns a problem into a procedure: the leak took away a docking port, and the mission plan simply uses two ports instead of one.

## Rassvet vs the PrK aft port

| | Rassvet nadir port | PrK aft port |
|---|---|---|
| Role on this mission | Docking and unloading | Reboost burns |
| Location | Earth-facing side of the Russian segment | Aft end of the Zvezda service module |
| Crew access | Hatch opens into the station; cargo can be unloaded | Tunnel sealed since June 2026; no crew access |
| Why it is used | Standard cargo port, fully accessible | Best geometry for orbit-raising engine burns |
| Used before for this | Routine Progress dockings | Routine Progress reboosts |

The reboosts matter more than they might sound. The station loses altitude constantly to the faint drag of the upper atmosphere, and without regular engine burns it would eventually fall back to Earth. Progress freighters have long been the station's workhorses for this job, and a freighter that stays docked for months can raise the orbit several times. On this mission, that work will be done from a port the crew can never enter — a small, practical adaptation to a station that is ageing in public.

## Who is waiting aboard

The seven people of Expedition 75 will do the unloading, and the work will start within hours of docking. After the docking probe engages and the hooks drive home, the crew runs leak checks on the interface, equalises pressure, and opens the hatch. Then comes the unglamorous core of station life: floating 1,206 kilograms of pressurised cargo out of the freighter, bag by bag, and stowing it where it belongs.

For Dubrov and Kikina, the freighter's arrival is also the start of the Teledroid test campaign they have been preparing for since the control hardware arrived in September. For Watkins and Delaney, the week holds a second rendezvous: on Sunday, they are the two astronauts scheduled to operate the Canadarm2 robotic arm to capture the next cargo ship, Northrop Grumman's Cygnus XL, when it arrives on Tuesday, December 1. The station's manifest for the week is, by any measure, full.

It is also a moment to notice how international the station's supply chain has become. In the space of eight days, the station will have received a Russian freighter launched on a Soyuz from Kazakhstan and an American-built cargo ship launched on a SpaceX Falcon 9 from Florida — two vehicles, two rockets, two countries, one destination. That is the station working as designed: a partnership of five space agencies, with the principals being the agencies of the United States, Russia, Europe, Japan, and Canada, keeping seven people alive and working in orbit.

## The week ahead: Cygnus XL on Sunday

Progress MS-36 is not the week's only delivery. NASA's Northrop Grumman CRS-25 mission — the final cargo mission of the year — is targeted to launch no earlier than 10:47 a.m. Eastern on Sunday, November 29, from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida, with a SpaceX Falcon 9 carrying the Cygnus XL spacecraft. The freighter is loaded with about 11,000 pounds of supplies, equipment, and experiments, including holiday food for the crew: gingerbread house kits, spicy carne asada tacos, and taco and pizza kits that are favourites aboard the station.

The Cygnus XL is expected to arrive at the station by about 2:35 a.m. Eastern on Tuesday, December 1, when Watkins and Delaney will use the Canadarm2 to capture it. It will remain docked until May 2027 before undocking and burning up on re-entry. Between the two freighters, the station will have taken on roughly seven and a half tons of cargo in a single week — a measure of how much mass it takes to keep a crew of seven fed, watered, and working in orbit, and of how smoothly, when the rockets cooperate, the logistics of low Earth orbit now run.

## Why this docking is worth watching

It is tempting to file a cargo docking under routine. Progress freighters have been flying since 1978; the station has received hundreds of tons of supplies; the automated docking system has done this so many times that the drama has mostly drained out of it. But this mission carries three things worth paying attention to, and they are the reason it is more than a delivery.

The first is Teledroid. If a humanoid robot can do useful work outside a space station, the economics and the safety of station maintenance change. Spacewalks are the most dangerous routine activity in human spaceflight, and also among the most expensive in crew time: every hour outside requires hours of preparation, and every task is constrained by the suit's limits. A robot that can open a casing or install a piece of equipment before the crew arrives is not a replacement for astronauts. It is a way of spending their time on the things only people can do.

The second is the two-port plan. The station is 28 years into a life that was originally planned for far less, and it is ageing in ways that cannot be fixed — a sealed tunnel, a chronic leak, a module closed off permanently. The response, so far, has been adaptation rather than repair: use a different port, change the procedure, keep flying. That is how the station will spend its remaining years, and this freighter's relocation is the latest example of the pattern.

The third is the simplest: the supply chain works. Two freighters in eight days, from two continents, is the unglamorous machinery that makes everything else in orbit possible — the science, the spacewalks, the record-breaking missions, the headlines. None of it happens without food, water, air, and propellant arriving on time. On Thanksgiving Day, a robot and three tons of supplies are due to arrive at a laboratory travelling at 28,000 kilometres per hour, and if the automated systems do their job, the crew will open the hatch, float the cargo out, and get back to work. That is the news, and it is enough.
