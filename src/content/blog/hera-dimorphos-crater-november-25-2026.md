For four years, the most consequential collision humans have ever staged in space existed only as a number. On 26 September 2022, NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test, or DART, slammed into the little asteroid moonlet Dimorphos at more than 22,000 kilometres per hour, and telescopes on Earth measured the result: the moonlet's orbit around its parent asteroid Didymos had shortened by 33 minutes, far more than predicted. The deflection worked. But no one could say what the impact had actually done to the rock itself. Had it carved a crater? Had it shattered the moonlet's shape? Had it done nothing visible at all? The spacecraft that struck the blow was destroyed in the act, and from 11 million kilometres away, even the largest telescopes on Earth could not resolve a body only 151 metres across.

That uncertainty ended on 25 November 2026. The European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft, which arrived at the Didymos system in October after a two-year cruise, released the first close-up images of Dimorphos — and the answer, it turns out, was more dramatic than almost anyone had dared to expect. One face of the moonlet has been flattened into a crater the size of a sports stadium, a scar that stretches across the asteroid's entire width. The impact has left Dimorphos tumbling chaotically in its orbit. And the space around the binary pair is littered with hundreds of boulders, each a few metres across, still circling the asteroids more than four years after the collision that threw them there.

"Scientists debated whether Dimorphos had sustained an impact crater, changed its shape or stayed the same," said Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel, Director of Research at CNRS and the Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur, as ESA unveiled the images in Berlin. "Much to our surprise the answer turns out to be all three at once! The face struck by DART has been flattened into an impact crater the size of a sports stadium, which extends across the asteroid's entire width."

## A face flattened

The headline image, taken by Hera's black-and-white Asteroid Framing Camera, shows Dimorphos from about 30 kilometres away, and the damage is unmistakable even at a glance. Where DART struck, the moonlet's once-rounded profile has been sliced away, leaving a broad, flat face. Inside the flattened region sits the crater itself — a vast bowl that, in the words of the mission team, is almost as big as the body it sits on. Hera mission manager Ian Carnelli put it plainly: "Basically, we were not sure whether we would see a crater or a reshape of the asteroid. I think we see a bit of both. And the crater is just huge; it is almost the size of the asteroid itself!"

The comparison images tell the story with particular force. DART's own camera, DRACO, photographed Dimorphos in its final seconds, showing a rubble-covered world shaped like a squashed sphere, its surface paved with boulders of every size. Hera's new view, taken from a similar angle, shows the same world with a piece missing. Sabina Raducan, a planetary scientist at the International Space Science Institute in Bern and a member of the Hera science team, described the sight in the simplest possible terms: "It just looked like a lump. But it was a lump with a piece missing."

What makes the crater so scientifically valuable is not only its size but its strangeness. Within the flattened face, Hera's cameras picked out odd structures, including what appears to be a cigar-shaped feature at the centre of the crater. The rest of Dimorphos, meanwhile, looks remarkably untouched. ESA reports that the boulder fields covering the moonlet's other regions look just as they did in DART's 2022 images, as though the violence of the impact was concentrated almost entirely on the struck face. That asymmetry is itself a clue: it suggests Dimorphos is a loosely bound rubble pile, a body held together more by its own weak gravity than by solid rock, where an impact can excavate an enormous crater without shattering the whole structure.

The finding resolves a debate that has run since the day DART struck. Before Hera arrived, modellers could not agree on what a 580-kilogram spacecraft hitting a rubble-pile asteroid at 6.1 kilometres per second would do. Some simulations predicted a modest crater, a neat pit punched into the surface. Others predicted that the impact would deform the whole body, squeezing it like a ball of gravel. A third camp wondered whether the moonlet might barely change at all, absorbing the blow the way a sandbag absorbs a punch. Hera's images show that all three things happened at once: a crater was excavated, the shape changed, and most of the surface stayed the same. That combination is now the ground truth against which every future deflection model will be tested — and it matters, because the next time humanity needs to deflect an asteroid, the target will not be a laboratory model. It will be a real, irregular, rubble-covered rock, and the difference between a neat crater and a wholesale reshaping could be the difference between a successful deflection and a dangerous surprise.

## Tumbling in the dark

The crater was the image everyone expected to argue about. The second surprise was one almost no one had predicted. As Hera returned more images over successive days, it became clear that Dimorphos is no longer rotating the way it used to. "In addition, as Hera returned more images, it became clear that the DART impact has left Dimorphos tumbling wildly in its orbit," said Michael Kueppers, ESA's Hera mission scientist.

Before the impact, Dimorphos was tidally locked to Didymos, the way the Moon is locked to Earth: the same face always pointing at its parent, turning once per orbit in a stately, predictable rhythm. DART's blow knocked it out of that lock. The moonlet now tumbles chaotically, its orientation changing in ways that are difficult to predict from one orbit to the next. For planetary defence, that is a complication with real consequences. A tumbling target is harder to track, harder to model, and harder to hit a second time if a second deflection were ever needed. It also changes how the moonlet's orbit evolves over time, since the gravitational dance between two irregular, tumbling bodies is far more complex than the tidy two-body problem in the textbooks.

Hera's job over the coming months is to measure that tumble precisely — to pin down Dimorphos's spin state, its mass, and the exact shape of the crater — so that the chaotic rotation can be folded into the models. That measurement campaign is the heart of the mission. DART proved that an asteroid's orbit can be changed. Hera exists to explain why it changed by the amount it did, and to tell engineers how much of the effect came from the impact itself and how much came from the recoil of debris thrown off the surface. The crater's size, the tumble, and the cloud of boulders are all pieces of that single puzzle.

## Hundreds of boulders in flight

The third surprise may prove the most consequential for the mission itself. Hera's instruments have revealed that the Didymos system is surrounded by hundreds of boulders, each a few metres across, still orbiting the binary pair more than four years after DART's impact threw them off Dimorphos. A composite image assembled from 11 long exposures taken by Hera's Hyperscout H hyperspectral camera on 3 November, from about 29.9 kilometres away, shows the boulders as a swarm of small points of light drifting around the two asteroids.

"Another big surprise is that Hera is showing that the asteroids are surrounded by hundreds of orbiting boulders, just a few metres in size," Kueppers said. "The team will have to take account of these objects in the coming weeks as the mission moves in closer to acquire higher-resolution images of the asteroids."

The boulders are, in one sense, a gift. They are the ejecta of the DART impact, preserved in orbit around the system like debris frozen at a crime scene, and studying them will reveal how much material the impact threw off, how fast it was moving, and how it has evolved since. In another sense, they are a hazard. Hera plans to fly closer to the asteroids in December, to about 13 kilometres, and in January 2027 it will release two CubeSats that will venture closer still. Every one of those manoeuvres must now be planned around a cloud of unseen, slow-moving rocks, each large enough to damage a spacecraft. The mission team is tracking the boulders' motion as Hera moves, building a map of the swarm before committing to the closer approaches.

The boulders also carry a lesson for planetary defence that goes beyond this one system. When telescopes watched DART's impact in 2022, they saw a plume of debris stretch out behind Dimorphos like a comet's tail, and for weeks afterwards the system glowed with ejected dust. What Hera shows is that the story did not end when the plume faded. A substantial population of larger fragments stayed behind, bound to the system, and four years later they are still there — a reminder that deflecting an asteroid is not a clean, one-off event but the beginning of a long-lived change in the target's environment.

## How Hera sees a world 193.5 million kilometres away

Hera is currently flying about 25 to 30 kilometres from Didymos and Dimorphos, holding its position with thruster burns that maintain what the mission team calls rectangular orbits around the two ultra-low-gravity bodies at an average velocity of just 12 centimetres per second — slower than a garden snail. At that distance, in the early characterisation phase of the mission, it is observing with a trio of instruments: the monochrome Asteroid Framing Camera, which took the crater images; the multi-spectral Hyperscout H, which reads the asteroids in 25 separate colours at once to map their mineral composition; and the Thermal Infrared Imager, TIRI, supplied by Japan's space agency JAXA, which measures surface temperatures and can see into shadows that hide Dimorphos from ordinary cameras.

It is the combination of those three eyes that makes Hera's investigation more than a photo opportunity. The framing camera gives shape. The hyperspectral imager gives composition. The thermal camera gives physical structure — how porous the surface is, how quickly it heats and cools, and therefore how solid or fluffy the material beneath is. Together they are meant to answer the question DART left open: what, exactly, is Dimorphos made of, and how did its structure shape the outcome of the impact?

That investigation is being conducted from a spacecraft about the size of a small car, built by a consortium of 18 ESA member states led by OHB in Germany, at a cost of about €360 million and carrying 12 instruments in total. Hera launched on 7 October 2024, when a Falcon 9 rocket lifted the 1,150-kilogram spacecraft from Cape Canaveral, and spent two years crossing the inner Solar System before reaching its target. Its first images, released in late October 2026, showed the two asteroids as separate points of light from 1,280 kilometres away. SpaceNews covered that first glimpse in <a href="/blog/hera-first-image-didymos-dimorphos-2026/">Hera First Image of Didymos and Dimorphos 2026: One Pixel, Two Asteroids, and the Future of Planetary Defense</a>. A week later, on 5 November, Hera returned its first close-up from 60 kilometres, showing Didymos in crisp detail while Dimorphos lurked mostly in shadow — the subject of <a href="/blog/hera-didymos-close-up-60-km-november-2026/">Hera Didymos Close-Up 60 km: ESA Sees the Hidden Face of the Asteroid DART Struck</a>. By mid-November the spacecraft had closed to 25 kilometres and was returning Didymos in detail down to about 2.7 metres per pixel, as reported in <a href="/blog/hera-dimorphos-25-km-images-superhuman-vision-november-2026/">Hera Dimorphos 25 km Images: Superhuman Vision at the Asteroid DART Moved</a>. Each step closer sharpened the picture; the 25 November reveal was the step that finally showed the scar itself.

## From impact to crater: the timeline

**26 September 2022** — NASA's DART spacecraft deliberately strikes Dimorphos at 6.1 kilometres per second, in humanity's first planetary defence test.

**11 October 2022** — NASA confirms the impact shortened Dimorphos's orbit around Didymos by about 33 minutes, far more than predicted — but what happened to the asteroid itself remains unknown.

**7 October 2024** — ESA's Hera spacecraft launches aboard a Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral, beginning a two-year cruise to the Didymos system.

**October 2026** — Hera arrives at Didymos and Dimorphos. Its first images, from 1,280 kilometres away, resolve the two asteroids as separate points of light.

**5 November 2026** — Hera returns its first close-up from 60 kilometres, showing Didymos in detail while Dimorphos sits mostly in shadow.

**3 November 2026** — Hera's Hyperscout H camera takes the long-exposure images, from about 29.9 kilometres, that reveal hundreds of boulders orbiting the binary pair.

**19 November 2026** — ESA releases Hera's 25-kilometre images of Didymos, resolving features down to about 2.7 metres per pixel, and announces the first close-up images of Dimorphos will follow on 25 November.

**25 November 2026** — At the Museum für Naturkunde in Berlin, ESA unveils the first close-up images of Dimorphos: a stadium-sized crater flattening one face, a chaotically tumbling moonlet, and a swarm of orbiting boulders.

**December 2026** — Hera is scheduled to close to about 13 kilometres from the asteroid pair, returning progressively higher-resolution images.

**January 2027** — Hera plans to deploy its two shoebox-sized CubeSats, Juventas and Milani, to venture closer still; Juventas is slated for release on 16 January 2027.

## Reading the scar: what the crater records

The crater is more than a hole in a rock. It is a measurement, written in stone, of how a kinetic impactor transfers its momentum to a rubble-pile asteroid — and it is the measurement the whole DART experiment was ultimately designed to produce. When DART struck Dimorphos, the spacecraft itself delivered only part of the push. The rest came from the debris the impact threw off the surface: tonnes of rock and dust ejected in the opposite direction, each fragment carrying momentum away and pushing Dimorphos harder in return. Scientists call this the momentum enhancement factor, and before Hera arrived, its value for a real asteroid was an educated guess built from laboratory experiments, computer simulations and a single number — the 33-minute change in the orbital period. The crater's size and depth, the volume of material it displaced, and the population of boulders still in orbit are the evidence that will let researchers work backwards from that number to the physics that produced it.

That matters because Dimorphos is not a solid rock. Everything Hera has seen so far suggests it is a rubble pile: a loose agglomeration of boulders and gravel held together by its own feeble gravity, with empty space between its components. DART's final images, taken by its DRACO camera in the seconds before impact, showed a surface paved with boulders of every size and almost no smooth ground — the portrait of a body assembled from fragments rather than carved from bedrock. Hitting such a body is fundamentally different from hitting solid stone. A solid target cracks and spalls; a rubble pile absorbs, compacts and rearranges. The crater Hera found — vast, flat-floored, spanning the whole width of the struck face — looks like the signature of that rearrangement: the impact did not so much dig a pit as shove a whole face of the asteroid inward and outward at once.

The comparison with the pre-impact models is where the science gets its edge. Before DART flew, the mission team had to design an impactor and choose a target without knowing how either would behave, and the range of plausible outcomes was wide. Some models held that a rubble pile would swallow the impact with barely a mark; others that it would deform wholesale. Hera's answer — crater, reshaping and an otherwise unchanged surface, all at once — narrows that range sharply, and it does so with a real body rather than a simulation. The next deflection mission, whenever it flies, will be designed against a crater that actually exists, not a crater that was merely predicted.

There is a second record in the crater as well: its interior. The cigar-shaped structure Hera's cameras picked out at the centre of the flattened face may be a fragment of Dimorphos's interior exposed by the blast, or a piece of DART itself buried in the debris. Either way, it is a feature no telescope on Earth could ever have seen, and it will be one of the first things the mission team examines as Hera moves closer in December. If it is interior material, it offers a direct look at what lies beneath a rubble pile's skin. If it is spacecraft debris, it is the first artefact humanity has ever left embedded in an asteroid — a marker, of sorts, at the scene of the first deflection.

## What happens next

The 25 November images mark the beginning of Hera's investigation, not its conclusion. In December, the spacecraft will move in to about 13 kilometres, close enough to resolve features smaller than a metre and to begin the precise measurements of Dimorphos's mass that the mission needs. In January 2027, it will deploy its two CubeSats. The Luxembourg-led Juventas carries the smallest radar instrument ever flown in space and will use low-frequency radar to probe an asteroid's interior for the first time, eventually attempting a landing on Dimorphos itself. The Italian-led Milani will prospect asteroid materials and the dust surrounding the pair. Operating together, Hera, Juventas and Milani will form a pioneering three-spacecraft exploration system around two asteroids, acquiring complementary measurements of their physical properties and of the aftermath of DART's impact.

The venue ESA chose for the unveiling was deliberate. Hera mission manager Ian Carnelli presented the first images of Dimorphos in the dinosaur hall of Berlin's Museum für Naturkunde, beneath the skeletons of creatures wiped out by an asteroid strike. "Sixty-six million years ago, an asteroid impact changed the course of life on Earth," Carnelli said. "Today, for the first time in history, humanity is validating how to prevent such an event from happening again. NASA's DART mission demonstrated that we can change an asteroid's orbit. Hera is now revealing what happened to the asteroid itself. By combining these two missions, humanity is taking a decisive step towards making asteroid deflection a reliable tool for protecting our planet."

That international framing was echoed by ESA Director-General Josef Aschbacher, who called the mission "international collaboration at its best: NASA having demonstrated that an asteroid can be deflected, ESA is now providing the detailed scientific investigation needed to understand how that deflection worked, along with valuable contributions from international partners, including Japan's JAXA. Together, we are transforming a successful experiment into the scientific knowledge needed to develop a reliable planetary defence capability."

Hera is ESA's first planetary defence mission, but the agency is already looking past it. ESA is developing Ramses, in cooperation with JAXA, to investigate the asteroid Apophis during its exceptionally close — but entirely safe — flyby of Earth on Friday 13 April 2029, when the asteroid will pass about 32,000 kilometres above the surface. Where Hera studies the consequences of deliberately striking an asteroid, Ramses will study how an asteroid responds to the gravitational influence of a planet. "Hera is helping us understand the consequences of deliberately striking an asteroid," said Holger Krag, head of ESA's Space Safety programme. "Ramses will investigate how an asteroid responds to the gravitational influence of a planet. Together, these missions will significantly expand the scientific knowledge available to planetary defence."

## Why a crater matters

It is easy to treat the crater as a curiosity, a dramatic picture for a press conference. Its real significance is quieter and more practical. Planetary defence is the only field of spaceflight where success is measured in something not happening: an asteroid that does not hit Earth. To make deflection reliable, engineers need to know not merely that a spacecraft can nudge an asteroid, but how the nudge propagates through a real body — how much momentum comes from the impactor itself and how much from the debris it throws off, how the target's shape and spin respond, and how the environment around the target changes afterwards. Every one of those questions now has a new data point, and the data point is a stadium-sized hole in a 151-metre rock.

Before Hera, the DART experiment had a single headline result: the orbit shortened by 33 minutes. That number proved the concept. But a deflection mission designed from that number alone would be flying half-blind, because the number alone cannot say whether the next target will behave the same way. Dimorphos turned out to be a rubble pile that absorbed a blow by excavating a crater as wide as itself, by starting to tumble, and by shedding hundreds of boulders into orbit. Another asteroid might be solid rock, and might respond by cracking, or by barely changing at all. Hera's crater, its tumble and its boulder swarm are the beginning of a catalogue — a set of worked examples that future mission planners can use to predict what their impactor will actually do.

There is also a humbler lesson in the images. For four years, the most important question about humanity's first asteroid deflection went unanswered not because the data was hard to collect, but because no one had gone to look. Telescopes measured the orbit change from 11 million kilometres away with exquisite precision, and still could not see the crater. The crater was there all along, waiting for a spacecraft willing to fly 25 kilometres from a tumbling rock in a swarm of boulders and take a picture. Hera did that. In December it will fly closer, and in January it will send two smaller spacecraft closer still, and each approach will sharpen the picture a little more. The scar DART left is now visible. Understanding it is the work of the months ahead — and it is the work on which the credibility of planetary defence now rests.
