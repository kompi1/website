In a galaxy so distant that its light has travelled for more than 12.5 billion years to reach us, astronomers have found not one, but three black holes feeding at the same time. The galaxy is catalogued as J0148-4214, and the James Webb Space Telescope has shown it as it was when the universe was only about 1.2 billion years old, less than a tenth of its present age. That single snapshot, reported by an international team led by the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics and published in the journal Astronomy and Astrophysics, has become one of the most talked-about early-universe results of late October 2026, not because it rewrites cosmology overnight, but because it gives scientists a rare, concrete example of several processes they have long suspected were at work in the young cosmos: galaxies merging, black holes pairing up, and small black holes growing surprisingly fast.

The result is worth describing carefully, because it was not a photograph of three black holes. Black holes themselves emit no light. What Webb detected, through its Near-Infrared Spectrograph, was the light of hot gas whirling around them, and the way that light shifted, in position and in velocity, across the galaxy. From those fingerprints, the team inferred three separate feeding sources where a simpler reading might have seen only one. That distinction matters. It is also the reason the finding is interesting beyond a single galaxy: if close pairs of black holes routinely blur into one smudge of light, astronomers may have been undercounting them for years, and revisiting existing Webb data with the same technique could reveal more hidden pairs and triples.

## A galaxy seen when the universe was young

J0148-4214 sits at a redshift of 5.02, a measure of how much the expansion of the universe has stretched its light toward redder wavelengths on its long journey to Earth. In practical terms, redshift is a clock. The higher the number, the earlier the epoch we are seeing. At redshift 5.02, we are looking back to a time when galaxies were still assembling, when the first generations of stars were enriching the gas around them with heavier elements, and when the black holes at galactic centres were, by many accounts, growing far too quickly for comfort.

That speed of growth is the puzzle at the heart of this story. Webb has repeatedly found black holes with masses of millions or billions of times the mass of the Sun in the first billion or so years after the Big Bang. Standard models, in which a black hole starts small and feeds steadily at or below a theoretical limit, struggle to explain how some of them bulked up so fast. The usual escapes from that difficulty are episodes of unusually rapid feeding, and mergers, in which two black holes combine and gain mass in a single step rather than by slow accretion. J0148-4214 appears to offer evidence for both routes operating in the same system, at the same time.

The three black holes in J0148-4214 have estimated masses of about 80 million, 2 million, and 600,000 times the mass of the Sun. Two of them lie close together near the galaxy's centre, separated by a projected distance of only about 620 light-years. The third sits much farther out, roughly 5,500 light-years from the centre. For scale, 620 light-years is a tiny separation on galactic terms, a neighbourhood rather than a city, and it is the reason the researchers expect the central pair to merge within the next few hundred million years. That merger, if it happens as projected, would be a dramatic event in the galaxy's history, although on human timescales it remains unimaginably remote, unfolding over a span longer than the entire history of complex life on Earth many times over.

The galaxy itself is not especially massive by modern standards. Its stars together amount to roughly 1.3 billion solar masses, according to the team's analysis of the same Webb data. That means its black holes make up an unusually large share of its total stellar mass, a pattern that has also been seen in other early galaxies studied by Webb and that has prompted debate about whether black holes and their host galaxies grew in lockstep in the early universe, or whether black holes sometimes raced ahead. J0148-4214 does not settle that debate, but it adds a detailed case study to it, with three black holes rather than one to account for.

## How Webb told three black holes apart

To understand the detection, it helps to picture what an actively feeding black hole actually looks like to a telescope. Gas falling toward a black hole does not plunge straight in. It spirals, heats up, and glows, forming a disc and a broader region of fast-moving gas around the black hole. That gas emits light at particular wavelengths, including hydrogen-alpha emission, which Webb's spectrograph can measure. Because the gas is moving at high speed, some of it is moving toward us and some away from us, and that motion stretches and compresses the light in a characteristic way, broadening the emission line.

In J0148-4214, the team used NIRSpec in its integral field mode, known as NIRSpec-IFS. Instead of taking a single spectrum of the whole galaxy, this mode records a spectrum at every point across the target, building a three-dimensional map in which each pixel carries its own spectrum. It is the astronomical equivalent of moving from a single photograph to a detailed survey in which every part of the scene can be interrogated separately. The work was part of a Webb programme called BlackTHUNDER, which studies black holes in early galaxies.

With that map in hand, the researchers examined the broad hydrogen-alpha emission from the galaxy's centre. The shape and position of the emission could not be explained satisfactorily by a single black hole alone. They then applied a technique called spectro-astrometry, which measures tiny shifts in the apparent position of the emission at different velocities within the line. If the light comes from two slightly offset sources, the centre of the light will appear to wobble, almost imperceptibly, as one looks across the spectrum from gas moving toward us to gas moving away. That wobble revealed that the central signal was in fact two sources, close together on the sky but distinct.

The technique is powerful precisely because it works below the normal resolution limit of the telescope. Even Webb, with its 6.5-metre mirror, cannot resolve two black holes 620 light-years apart in a galaxy more than 12.5 billion light-years away as two separate points of light. Spectro-astrometry does not need to. It uses the spectral information to separate what the image alone blends together. The researchers said that without spatially resolved spectroscopy of this kind, only one of the three black holes would likely have been detected, and the system would have been filed away as an ordinary, single active galactic nucleus.

That has a broader implication that the team and commentators have both stressed. Surveys of the early universe may contain other multiple black hole systems hiding in plain sight, recorded as single sources because no one has yet applied this kind of analysis to them. The archive of Webb observations is already large, and it grows every month. Revisiting it with spectro-astrometry could change the census of early black holes, not by finding new galaxies, but by showing that some galaxies already in the catalogue contain more black holes than anyone realised.

The team also checked alternative explanations before settling on three black holes. Supernovae, shocks in the gas, powerful stellar winds, and very massive stars can all produce bright emission, and in a young, star-forming galaxy they are plausible contaminants. Based on the additional spectral evidence, the researchers concluded that actively accreting black holes were the most likely explanation for the observed features. That is the normal language of careful observational astronomy: not certainty, but a best explanation after alternatives have been tested and found wanting.

## The giant, its close companion, and the outsider

The most massive member of the trio, at about 80 million solar masses, anchors the centre of the galaxy. It is the kind of black hole that, in a modern galaxy, would sit quietly at the centre of a large elliptical system, but in J0148-4214 it is still actively feeding, pulling in gas and shining as an active galactic nucleus. Curiously, it is not the fastest feeder in the system. The researchers estimate that it is accreting at a lower rate than its smaller central companion, a reminder that mass alone does not determine how quickly a black hole is currently growing. Feeding depends on the supply of gas, on the geometry of that gas, and on the recent history of the galaxy, all of which can vary from one black hole to another even within the same galaxy.

The central pair's projected separation of about 620 light-years is an important detail, and it comes with a caveat. Projected separation is the distance as seen on the sky, not a full three-dimensional measurement. The true separation could be larger if the two black holes are also separated along our line of sight. Even so, 620 light-years is close enough, on galactic scales, that the pair is expected to spiral together and merge within a few hundred million years, according to the team's interpretation. That timeline is a projection based on the estimated separation and on models of how black hole pairs lose energy and shrink their orbits, not a directly measured orbit. The merger itself has not been observed, and it will not be observed by anyone alive today. Its importance is as a test case: it shows that the early universe could produce close black hole pairs, the progenitors of the massive mergers that future gravitational wave observatories hope to detect.

The third black hole, about 5,500 light-years from the centre, is the system's mystery. Its origin is uncertain, and the researchers offer three possibilities, none of which the present data can conclusively distinguish. It may be left over from an earlier galaxy merger, a relic of a smaller galaxy that was swallowed by J0148-4214 and whose central black hole survived the encounter. It may have been kicked outward by gravitational recoil, the kick a merged black hole receives when it emits gravitational waves asymmetrically during a merger, flinging it away from the centre. Or it may simply be migrating inward, on a long journey toward the centre that it has not yet completed. Each possibility tells a different story about the galaxy's past, and future observations that pin down the third black hole's motion and surroundings could help choose between them.

This kind of off-centre black hole is not merely a curiosity. If galaxies grow by merging, then off-centre and wandering black holes should be a natural by-product, the survivors of past encounters that have not yet settled. Finding one in a galaxy only 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang suggests that the merger history of galaxies was already complex at that early epoch, with multiple building blocks coming together and leaving their black holes behind as traces of the process.

## The small black hole breaking the speed limit

The most striking single result in the study concerns the smallest of the three black holes, estimated at about 600,000 solar masses. Despite its modest size compared with its 80-million-solar-mass neighbour, the researchers estimate that it is feeding faster than the Eddington limit. The Eddington limit is a theoretical benchmark, not a law of nature. In simplified models, it describes the point at which the radiation pressure from infalling, glowing gas pushes outward as strongly as gravity pulls inward, setting a maximum steady rate at which a black hole can accrete. Exceeding that limit does not mean a black hole has broken physics. It means the simplified model does not fully capture what is happening, perhaps because the gas is not spherical, because radiation escapes in some directions more easily than others, or because the feeding is episodic rather than steady.

Even with those caveats, super-Eddington feeding is important, because it offers a way out of the early-growth puzzle. A black hole that can feed above the Eddington limit, even for a time, can bulk up far faster than one constrained to feed below it. If episodes of super-Eddington growth were common in the early universe, then the massive black holes Webb keeps finding at high redshift become less paradoxical. They did not need to start implausibly large, nor to feed at the maximum steady rate for their entire lives. They needed only a few bursts of rapid growth, perhaps triggered by galaxy mergers funneling gas toward their centres, to reach the masses we observe.

The mass estimates themselves carry substantial uncertainty, and the researchers are open about that. Black hole masses at these distances are not weighed directly. They are inferred from relationships between the properties of the emission lines, such as their width, and black hole mass, calibrated on nearer systems. Applying those calibrations to distant, early galaxies can introduce large errors. The finding, therefore, is best read as evidence of unusually rapid feeding, not as a precise measurement of exactly how far beyond the Eddington limit the small black hole is operating. That honesty about uncertainty is a strength of the study, not a weakness, and it points to the work that remains: better calibrations, more observations, and a larger sample of systems to see whether super-Eddington feeding is common or exceptional.

Co-author Roberto Maiolino of the University of Cambridge has stressed the complementary role of mergers in this picture. A coming merger of the central pair would combine their mass in one step, alongside steady feeding, offering a second fast route to growth. The two mechanisms are not rivals. In a merging galaxy, they may work together: the merger drives gas inward, triggering rapid feeding, and eventually the black holes themselves merge, adding their masses together. J0148-4214, with its close pair and its rapidly feeding small member, is a system in which both mechanisms appear to be in play.

## Why three black holes in one galaxy matter

It is tempting to ask why a single galaxy, however unusual, should matter to anyone outside a small community of astronomers. The answer lies in what early galaxies can teach us about the rules that governed the universe's first billion years, and about the origins of the structures we see around us today, including the Milky Way and its own central black hole, Sagittarius A*, with a mass of about four million Suns.

Every massive galaxy in the modern universe appears to host a central black hole, and the masses of those black holes correlate, roughly, with the masses of their host galaxies' central bulges. That correlation suggests that black holes and galaxies grew together, influencing each other through feedback, in which energy from a feeding black hole heats or expels gas and regulates star formation, and through mergers, in which both galaxies and their black holes combine. But the correlation we see today is the end product of billions of years of evolution. To understand how it was established, astronomers need to catch galaxies and black holes in the act of growing together, early on, before the relationship settled into its modern form. J0148-4214 is such a catch: a young galaxy, still assembling, with three black holes at different stages and in different locations, each telling part of the story.

The system also matters for gravitational wave astronomy. When two massive black holes merge, they emit gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime that travel across the universe. The planned Laser Interferometer Space Antenna, or LISA, a space-based observatory led by the European Space Agency with NASA participation, is designed to detect mergers of massive black holes, among other sources. A system like J0148-4214, with a central pair expected to merge, is the kind of progenitor that LISA's science case is built around, even though this particular pair's merger will unfold over hundreds of millions of years and will not be detected as a single event by any observatory operating today. By studying how common such pairs were in the early universe, astronomers can predict how often LISA should hear massive mergers, and what those signals will tell us about galaxy evolution.

There is also a lesson here about how science progresses through archives. The discovery did not require a new telescope or a new instrument. It required a new way of looking at data that Webb had already collected, through the BlackTHUNDER programme, and a technique, spectro-astrometry, that squeezes positional information out of spectra. That is a pattern that recurs throughout astronomy: a major survey produces a treasure trove, and later analyses, sometimes years later, extract results the original observers did not anticipate. The paper on J0148-4214 was first released as a preprint in September 2025 and announced by the Max Planck Society in August 2026, and it drew renewed attention in late October 2026 after a summary appeared. The timeline is a useful reminder that science news is not always breaking news. Sometimes the most interesting story is a result that has been sitting in the literature, waiting for the right moment, and the right explanation, to reach a wider audience.

For readers following Webb's work more broadly, J0148-4214 sits alongside a run of early-universe results that have kept the telescope at the centre of astronomy since its first images in 2022. Earlier SpaceNews coverage has tracked <a href="/blog/webb-telescope-2026-discoveries/">Webb telescope 2026 discoveries</a> and the telescope's look at the <a href="/blog/webb-earliest-supernova-grb-250314a-730-million-years/">earliest supernova and gamma-ray burst</a> seen only hundreds of millions of years after the Big Bang. Those stories, like this one, share a theme: Webb's infrared vision lets us see epochs that were previously out of reach, and each new epoch brings surprises about how quickly structure formed. J0148-4214 adds a new kind of surprise, not a single object that is too big or too early, but a system that is too crowded, with three giants where one was expected.

## What this result does not prove

Good science reporting should be as clear about limits as about breakthroughs, and J0148-4214 has several limits worth stating plainly. First, this is a study of one galaxy. One galaxy cannot show how common triple black hole systems are, nor whether they are a typical stage in galaxy evolution or a rare accident. Astronomers will need to study other distant galaxies, with the same technique, to establish whether systems like this are routine or exceptional. Until that larger sample exists, any claim that triple systems explain early black hole growth in general would be an overreach.

Second, the central pair was inferred from spectral structure, not resolved directly as two separate points of light. The authors argue that two black holes best explain the data, and they tested alternatives, but inference is not the same as a direct image. Future observations, perhaps with higher spectral resolution or with different instruments, could refine or revise the picture. That is normal in astronomy, and it is why the team describes the result as the first evidence of three active black holes in a single distant galaxy, in the words of lead author Hannah Übler of the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics, rather than as a closed case.

Third, the masses and feeding rates are estimates, with substantial uncertainties, particularly when calibrations developed for nearer black holes are applied to galaxies seen at redshift 5. The super-Eddington claim for the smallest black hole is therefore evidence of unusually rapid feeding, not a precise measurement of the excess. Fourth, the merger timeline for the central pair is a projection, based on the estimated separation and on models of orbital decay, not a measured orbit. The pair may merge sooner, later, or, if the true three-dimensional separation is larger than the projected separation, on a longer timescale than the headline figure suggests.

None of those caveats diminishes the result. They define it. A single, well-studied system with a clear detection method, tested alternatives, and honestly stated uncertainties is exactly the kind of evidence on which bigger theories are built, one case at a time. The next steps are also clear: more Webb spectroscopy of other early galaxies, revisiting existing data with spectro-astrometry, and, in the longer term, gravitational wave observations that can test whether massive mergers were as common in the early universe as systems like J0148-4214 suggest.

## The wider context: black holes, galaxies, and the early universe

J0148-4214 also connects to a second thread in recent Webb research: the relationship between black holes and their host galaxies. In the nearby universe, the most massive black holes tend to live in the most massive galaxies, but the ratio is not fixed, and in the early universe Webb has found systems in which the black hole seems oversized relative to its host. With a stellar mass of about 1.3 billion Suns and black holes totalling roughly 82 million Suns, J0148-4214 leans in that oversized direction, although the comparison is complicated by the fact that there are three black holes to count, not one, and by the uncertainties in both the stellar mass and the black hole masses.

One way to read that pattern is that black holes grew first, and galaxies caught up later, perhaps because early black holes fed efficiently on abundant gas while star formation was still ramping up. Another reading is that the early galaxies Webb sees are a biased sample: the brightest, most active systems are the easiest to detect at great distances, so they may not be representative of typical galaxies at that epoch. A third possibility is that the mass calibrations themselves are biased at high redshift, making black holes appear more massive than they are. Distinguishing those possibilities requires larger, less biased samples, and careful work on calibrations, which is precisely the work that programmes like BlackTHUNDER are designed to support.

The story also resonates with research on black holes closer to home, including the way astronomers study feeding and feedback in active galaxies today. SpaceNews recently covered a very different kind of black hole system, in which <a href="/blog/black-hole-solar-storm-ngc-3783-neupert-effect/">a black hole's outburst behaved like a solar storm in NGC 3783</a>, showing how the same physics of accretion, radiation, and outflows operates across vastly different scales and epochs. The contrast is instructive. NGC 3783 is a nearby, well-studied active galaxy whose behaviour can be tracked in detail. J0148-4214 is a distant, young system whose behaviour must be inferred from faint, redshifted light. Together, they illustrate the range of the field: from precise studies of individual nearby objects to statistical and case-study work at the edge of the observable universe, all aimed at the same questions about how black holes grow and how they shape their surroundings.

Looking ahead, the most immediate follow-up is observational rather than theoretical. Astronomers will want deeper Webb spectra of J0148-4214 itself, to refine the masses and to test whether the third black hole shows signs of motion or of a distinct stellar population around it. They will want to apply spectro-astrometry to other Webb targets, to see whether hidden pairs and triples are lurking in data already collected. And they will want to compare the system with simulations of galaxy formation, which predict how often triple black hole systems should occur, how long they should last, and what their observable signatures should be. If simulations predict far fewer triples than observations find, or far more, that mismatch will itself be informative, pointing to missing physics in the models or to misinterpretation of the data.

In the longer term, the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna, planned for launch in the 2030s, will open a new window on massive black hole mergers. LISA will not hear the specific merger of J0148-4214's central pair, which lies hundreds of millions of years in the pair's future, but it will hear mergers of similar systems at similar epochs, if they are common enough. The rate and masses of those mergers will provide an independent check on the picture emerging from Webb: that the early universe was a crowded, dynamic place, where galaxies merged frequently and black holes grew by feeding and by combining, sometimes in the same system at the same time.

## A snapshot, not a finish line

It is easy, in the rush of space news, to treat each new result as a revolution. J0148-4214 is better understood as a snapshot, a single frame from a film that is still being shot. In that frame, we see a galaxy barely a billion years after the Big Bang, already hosting three feeding black holes, already shaped by mergers, already pushing against the limits of simple theory. The frame does not tell us the whole story, and it does not need to. It tells us that the story is richer than a single black hole growing quietly at the centre of a single galaxy, and it gives astronomers a concrete system to test their ideas against.

For the rest of us, the wonder is in the scale. The light we are analysing left J0148-4214 when the universe was young, travelled for more than 12.5 billion years, and arrived at a telescope parked a million miles from Earth, where a spectrograph split it into its component colours and revealed, in tiny shifts of position across a hydrogen emission line, that what looked like one monster was in fact three. That is the quiet power of modern astronomy: not just seeing farther, but seeing more cleverly, extracting from faint light the evidence of objects that cannot be seen directly, and using that evidence to reconstruct a history that began long before our Sun, our planet, or our species existed.

Three black holes, one young galaxy, and a merger still hundreds of millions of years away. The universe, it turns out, was already complicated, already crowded, and already full of surprises, when it was barely a billion years old. J0148-4214 is a reminder to keep looking, and to keep looking closely, because the next surprise may already be in the data, waiting for someone to notice that a single blur is, in fact, more than one thing.
