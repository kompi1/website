At 14:29 Central European Summer Time on Tuesday, August 18, two astronauts switched their spacesuits to battery power inside the International Space Station's Quest airlock. It is a small, technical moment — the flipping of a switch that marks the official start of a spacewalk — but for Sophie Adenot, it was the threshold of history. Minutes later, she pushed herself out into the vacuum, and France had its first female spacewalker.

Adenot, a 44-year-old European Space Agency astronaut and former helicopter test pilot, spent the next six hours and twenty-three minutes working outside the station alongside NASA astronaut Anil Menon. By the time the pair repressurized the airlock at 20:52 CEST, Adenot had become the first French woman to conduct an extravehicular activity, and only the second European woman ever to do so — following Italy's Samantha Cristoforetti, who spacewalked in July 2022. It was US Spacewalk 97, the 282nd spacewalk in support of station assembly, maintenance, and upgrades. And like so many spacewalks before it, almost nothing went according to plan.

## The job: a stubborn antenna on the Z1 truss

The task for the day sounded straightforward on paper: swap out a failed Space-to-Ground antenna — SGANT-2 in station shorthand — mounted on the station's Z1 truss segment. The antenna had been dead weight for months. Since November 2025, it had been unable to track the Tracking and Data Relay Satellite (TDRS) constellation, the network of communications satellites that relays data between the station and mission control in Houston. A Space-to-Ground antenna exists for exactly one purpose — high-speed communications — and a station the size of a football field cannot afford to lose its links to the ground.

The plan was a classic remove-and-replace: detach the failed unit, secure it, and bolt a fresh spare onto the truss in its place. Planners had budgeted about six and a half hours for the full job. The crew would not be slow — the EVA came in at six hours and twenty-three minutes, right on the planned duration. The work, as it turned out, was simply more resistant than the schedule assumed.

Adenot and Menon began by organizing their tools inside the Quest airlock in the days before the excursion, while station commander Jessica Meir and flight engineer Jack Hathaway reviewed the Canadarm2 robotic-arm maneuvers that would support the spacewalkers. Once outside, Menon retrieved a foot restraint and installed it on the end of Canadarm2, which Hathaway operated from inside the station. The robotic arm then transported Menon to the worksite on the Z1 truss, while Adenot translated there independently along the station's handrails — and began removing a heat shield from the failed antenna to expose the hardware beneath.

Working together, the pair installed gimbal locks to prevent the antenna from rotating during transport, then set about the unglamorous core of the job: removing bolts and disconnecting a series of electrical connections to free the failed antenna from its boom assembly. This is where the day began to slip. Several bolts were stuck. Connectors refused to release. In the vacuum, with extreme temperature swings and bulky pressurized gloves, every fastener becomes an adversary. What should have taken minutes took far longer, as the two astronauts applied careful, coordinated force to hardware that had been baking and freezing in orbit for years.

Mission Control watched the timeline erode and made the call that spacewalk planners dread but train for: revise the objectives. Rather than transporting the failed antenna to a stowage platform and installing the spare, the crew would perform a long-duration tie-down of the removed antenna directly on the station's truss structure — securing what they had accomplished — and come home. The installation of the replacement antenna was deferred to a future spacewalk. As the station's official account put it, the crew "needed more time than expected to disconnect electrical cables and loosen bolts, delaying work to install a replacement antenna."

It is worth emphasizing what did not happen: at no point was the station out of contact with the ground. A second, fully working antenna carried the high-speed communications link throughout the EVA, so SGANT-2 was a maintenance item, not an emergency. The conservatism of the flight controllers' decision — secure the worksite, live to fight another day — is exactly how you keep a twenty-five-year-old orbital outpost healthy.

Menon was candid afterward about the gap between the plan and the reality. "We got into this with the mindset that we wanted everything to go perfectly," he said. "Things were a lot different than expected." It is about as honest a summary of maintenance work as you will ever hear — on the ground or off it.

## Who is Sophie Adenot

To understand why August 18 mattered beyond the antenna, you need to know who was in the suit. Sophie Adenot, born in Cosne-Cours-sur-Loire in central France, is an engineer by training who became France's first female helicopter test pilot — a career built on precisely the kind of methodical problem-solving that a stubborn antenna demands. She studied aerospace engineering at ISAE-SUPAERO, France's storied aeronautics institute, then earned a master's degree in Human Factors Engineering at MIT in the United States. She has logged more than 3,000 flight hours across more than 22 helicopter types and holds the rank of colonel in the French Air and Space Force.

The European Space Agency selected her as an astronaut in November 2022, part of a class chosen to carry Europe's human spaceflight program into its next decades. She launched to the International Space Station in February 2026 on her epsilon mission, becoming only the second Frenchwoman in history to fly in space — three decades after pioneer Claudie Haigneré, who first flew in 1996. Weeks before the spacewalk, on July 14, she had been appointed a chevalier of the National Order of the Legion of Honour, France's highest order of merit.

Aboard the station, Adenot threw herself into the science program with characteristic intensity. Her experiments included testing EchoFinder, a device for autonomous medical ultrasounds to study the effects of weightlessness on the human body; PhysioTool, measuring physiological factors; and ChlorISS, an experiment developed with French schools to observe how light and gravity affect the growth of plants from the mustard family. She even brought a taste of home to orbit: lobster bisque and foie gras created by Michelin-starred chef Anne-Sophie Pic, supplementing the standard onboard menu. She also tested a prototype suit called EuroSuit and continued exercise research, running on the station's COLBERT treadmill wearing the "EasyMotion-2" system, which uses electrical impulses to trigger muscle contractions during workouts.

## A milestone decades in the making

The historical weight of the day is best measured against the long, slow history of women spacewalking. When Soviet cosmonaut Svetlana Savitskaya became the first woman to conduct a spacewalk in 1984, it would be years before another followed. For France — a nation with a proud human spaceflight tradition stretching back to Jean-Loup Chrétien's flight in 1982 — the wait for a first female spacewalker lasted until 2026.

Adenot is the second European woman to spacewalk, after Samantha Cristoforetti's excursion in July 2022. But there is a notable technical distinction between the two milestones: Cristoforetti conducted her spacewalk from the station's Russian Poisk module wearing a Russian Orlan suit, while Adenot went out from the Quest airlock in an American Extravehicular Mobility Unit — the classic NASA EMU with its white fabric shell and backpack life-support system. She is the first European woman to spacewalk in the EMU, the suit in which the overwhelming majority of ISS spacewalks have been conducted.

The distinction matters because the EMU and Orlan represent two different philosophies of spacewalking, with different airlocks, different procedures, and different training flows. Qualifying in the EMU puts Adenot in the mainstream of ISS extravehicular operations — eligible for the full range of US-segment spacewalks that make up the station's maintenance program. It is one thing to visit space; it is another to be certified to work outside the vehicle, and Adenot's qualification expands Europe's operational independence aboard the station.

The numbers around women and spacewalking remain stark. Of the hundreds of spacewalks conducted in history, only a small fraction have included women. Each new name on the list — and Adenot's is now etched there permanently — chips away at a demographic imbalance that has persisted for six decades. France, which appointed Adenot a chevalier of the Legion of Honour just weeks earlier, clearly understands the symbolic power of the moment. But symbolism only goes so far in a vacuum; what earned Adenot her place was six and a half hours of difficult, skilled manual labor in the most hostile work environment humans have ever entered.

## The crew around her

No spacewalk is a solo endeavor, and August 18 showcased the choreography that makes EVAs possible. Anil Menon, the NASA astronaut alongside Adenot, brought his own steady competence to the worksite — riding the Canadarm2 to the truss, wrestling the same stubborn hardware, and sharing the disappointment of the deferred installation with characteristic grace. His post-EVA candor about the day's difficulties reflected a culture where honesty about what went wrong is valued over bravado.

Inside the station, the support team was just as critical. Commander Jessica Meir and flight engineer Jack Hathaway had reviewed the Canadarm2 maneuvers in advance; during the EVA, Hathaway operated the robotic arm that positioned Menon at the worksite, translating centimeters of joystick input into meters of precise motion outside. Every spacewalker depends on an entire team — the capcoms in Houston talking them through procedures, the flight controllers watching telemetry, the crewmates managing the airlock — and the smooth execution of the revised plan, the tie-down and safe return, was their achievement as much as the spacewalkers'.

It is a fitting detail that Meir, herself one of the most experienced spacewalkers in the NASA corps, was commanding the station that day. The lineage is visible: women who walked in space before making it possible — logistically, culturally, institutionally — for the women who follow. Adenot has spoken about that responsibility directly. "I recognise and acknowledge that a lot of women and girls are looking for inspiration," she said in an interview with Radio France Internationale, "and I am here for them."

## The job isn't finished

The story of SGANT-2 does not end with the tie-down. NASA moved quickly to schedule a follow-up spacewalk to complete what August 18 started — installing the spare antenna that the stuck bolts denied the crew. The failed unit, now secured to the truss, awaits its ride to a stowage platform, and the replacement awaits its turn on the Z1 truss.

This is the unglamorous reality of operating an orbital outpost in its third decade: maintenance is a campaign, not an event. Antennas degrade. Bolts seize. Timelines slip. The station keeps flying because crews keep going outside, year after year, to do the work. Adenot's first spacewalk — historic as it was — was also, in the most literal sense, just a day at the office. An office 400 kilometers up, moving at 28,000 kilometers per hour, where the commute involves a vacuum.

For Adenot personally, the milestone opens a new chapter. Having proven herself outside the vehicle, she joins the small cadre of astronauts qualified for the station's most demanding maintenance work. Her epsilon mission still has months to run, with more science, more operations, and — given the unfinished antenna — quite possibly more time outside.

## The suit: a spacecraft you wear

It is easy to forget, watching the graceful footage, what an Extravehicular Mobility Unit actually is: a one-person spacecraft. The EMU's hard upper torso, pressurized to about a third of an atmosphere of pure oxygen, resists every movement the astronaut makes — bending an elbow means fighting the suit's determination to stay straight. The gloves, the most notorious component, turn delicate work into an endurance trial; astronauts train for years to build the hand strength needed to operate tools through pressurized gauntlets, and still return with bruised fingernails and aching forearms.

The backpack — the Primary Life Support System — carries oxygen, removes carbon dioxide, circulates cooling water through the liquid cooling garment worn against the skin, and powers the radios. Lose any of it and the spacewalk ends. The helmet's gold-tinted visor shields against unfiltered solar radiation. Every layer is a compromise between protection and mobility, and every spacewalker learns the same lesson: the suit is both what keeps you alive and what makes the work so hard.

That Adenot was the first European woman to work in the EMU is significant for another reason: suit fit. For years, spacesuit sizing was a quiet barrier — the famous 2019 incident in which NASA had to cancel a planned all-female spacewalk for lack of a second medium-sized torso assembly made headlines worldwide and forced a reckoning. The EMU fleet has since been better provisioned, and Adenot's excursion is evidence of the progress: the system worked, the suit fit, and the work got done. The frontier of inclusion in spaceflight is measured not only in firsts, but in logistics.

## France's long road to this moment

France has been a spacefaring nation for more than four decades, but its human spaceflight story has always run through partnerships — first with the Soviet Union and Russia, later through the European Space Agency. Jean-Loup Chrétien became the first Frenchman in space in 1982 aboard a Soviet mission. Claudie Haigneré became the first Frenchwoman in space in 1996, and later the first European woman to visit the International Space Station. Thomas Pesquet became one of ESA's most visible astronauts, commanding the station in 2021.

Adenot's milestone slots into that lineage and extends it. She is the product of a French aerospace ecosystem — ISAE-SUPAERO, the test pilot schools, the Air and Space Force — that has produced world-class aviators for generations, now finally producing a woman qualified to work outside a spacecraft. Her appointment to the Legion of Honour on Bastille Day 2026, weeks before the spacewalk, signaled how seriously France takes the moment: this is not just a personal achievement but a national one, the kind that gets taught in schools.

There is also a distinctly European dimension. ESA's astronaut corps remains small compared to NASA's, and every European spacewalk is a significant operational commitment for the agency. Adenot's qualification in the EMU gives Europe another fully certified spacewalker at a time when the ISS partnership is managing an aging station and planning its eventual transition to commercial successors. European astronauts who can do US-segment EVAs are strategic assets — and Adenot, at 44 with years of station operations ahead of her, is now firmly among them.

## A kicker written in starlight

History will remember August 18, 2026 for the first: the first French woman to walk in space. But the day deserves to be remembered for something else, too — for what it revealed about the nature of the work. The stuck bolts. The revised plan. The honest debrief. Spaceflight, for all its romance, is mostly this: skilled people solving uncooperative problems in impossible places, and coming home safely to try again.

Somewhere over the next weeks, a spare antenna will be bolted to the Z1 truss, and the station's communications will be whole again. When it happens, it will be because two astronauts spent six and a half hours on a Tuesday in August refusing to let stubborn hardware win — and because one of them, in doing so, carried the hopes of everyone who ever looked up and wondered whether someone like them could go outside. Sophie Adenot went outside. France will not forget it.
