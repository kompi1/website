On the Florida coast, on the same stretch of ground where John Glenn left Earth to become the first American in orbit, a rocket company that has never flown to space is already planning its second launch pad. Stoke Space announced on November 6, 2026, that the U.S. Space Force's Space Launch Delta 45 has granted it a Right of Entry to property north of its existing launch site, Space Launch Complex 14, at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station. The authorisation clears the way for engineering studies, environmental assessments and site surveys — the first formal step toward building a second launch site for the company's Nova rocket, a vehicle designed from the outset to be fully and rapidly reusable.

The timing is striking. Stoke's first rocket, Nova Pathfinder, has not yet flown. Its first flight booster only left the company's Washington factory on November 4, bound for the Cape. Yet the company is already laying the groundwork for the larger rocket that follows it, Nova Block 2, and for the launch tempo that rocket is meant to serve. Behind the expansion sits serious money: a $1 billion funding round closed in September and a conditional $150 million loan commitment from the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital, announced on October 26, specifically to help build the second pad. The message from both the Space Force and the Pentagon is the same — the United States wants more launch capacity, and it wants it from rockets that can fly again and again.

For Stoke, the second pad is not a vanity project. It is the physical expression of a bet that the next decade of spaceflight will be defined not by who can build the biggest rocket, but by who can fly the same rocket most often, at the lowest cost, with the shortest time between flights. A single pad can only turn around so fast. Two pads, built for rapid reuse from the start, can keep a fleet flying while the other is reset, inspected and loaded again. That is the logic behind the expansion — and the reason the Space Force is opening more of the Eastern Range to a company whose rocket has yet to reach orbit.

## What the Space Force actually approved

A Right of Entry is not a construction permit, and Stoke has been careful not to present it as one. The notice from Space Launch Delta 45, known as SLD 45, authorises the company to enter the property north of SLC-14 to carry out the studies that must precede any building: engineering surveys, environmental assessments and on-site investigations. The parcel sits in the location of the old LC-15 pad, extending Stoke's existing complex northward along the Cape's historic "Missile Row."

The company describes the authorisation as the first step toward opening additional Eastern Range property for fully and rapidly reusable launch. That phrasing matters, because the Eastern Range — the network of tracking stations, airspace and safety corridors that supports launches from Florida — is the bottleneck for how many rockets can fly from the Cape in a given year. Every new pad must be fitted into that system, and every operator must prove its vehicles can be recovered and reflown without disrupting its neighbours. A Right of Entry is the formal beginning of that process, not its end, and Stoke has given no construction timetable for the second site.

What is already clear is what the second site is for. It will support high-cadence launch and re-entry operations for both of Stoke's Nova vehicles: the Pathfinder now being prepared for its first flight, and the larger Block 2 that follows it. The existing SLC-14 complex was rebuilt for exactly that kind of fast-turn operation, and the company says the northern property will extend that capability as both vehicles come online. The near-term focus, the company stresses, remains completing Nova Pathfinder and preparing SLC-14 to receive the flight hardware now travelling across the country.

The Space Force's own words framed the decision as part of a broader opening of the Cape. "Every launch provider strengthens the nation's access to space," said Col. Brian Chatman, commander of Space Launch Delta 45 and director of the Eastern Range. "Our team here at the Eastern Range is proud to support partners bringing more launch capability to the United States, and we look forward to enabling more launches from the Space Coast." Brian Wemple, Stoke's vice president of launch, called the milestone a reflection of the Delta's commitment to developing the range infrastructure that fully and rapidly reusable launch requires, adding: "It's an honor to build the future of launch on the historic ground of SLC-14, and we are excited to carry that momentum forward with this expansion."

## From Mercury pad to Nova

SLC-14 holds a prominent place in American space history, and Stoke has leaned into that heritage rather than paving over it. The pad was the site of John Glenn's Friendship 7 mission on February 20, 1962, the first orbital flight by an American astronaut. It later supported the crewed Mercury-Atlas launches of Scott Carpenter, Wally Schirra and Gordon Cooper, before being deactivated in February 1967. The complex forms part of the Cape Canaveral Air Force Station National Historic Landmark district, designated in 1984, and the pad then sat largely dormant for decades.

Stoke was allocated the complex in 2023 following a competitive process, and received the property licence in 2024. The company says it restored and reactivated the site in just over a year after completing environmental assessments — a fast turnaround for a pad that had been silent since the 1960s. The rebuild included a Horizontal Integration Facility, a launch mount, umbilical support structure, a flame trench and diverter, and propellant systems, all designed around fast-turn, high-cadence launch and recovery operations. Concrete from the original Mercury-era pad was crushed and reused in some areas during reconstruction, and the prominent launch tower is painted red in tribute to the early Space Coast launch towers.

Flyovers through 2025 and 2026 showed the pad progressing to a largely complete state, with the site appearing ready to receive hardware once it arrived from Stoke's facilities in Washington state. That hardware is now on its way. The picture that emerges is of a company that treated the pad itself as part of the rocket's design: not a place to launch from, but a machine for launching, built to be reset and used again as quickly as the vehicle it serves.

## The rocket the second pad is for

Nova Pathfinder is Stoke's first orbital configuration, and it is currently targeted for its first orbital launch in the first half of 2027. The vehicle is powered by seven Zenith full-flow staged-combustion engines on the first stage and a single Andromeda hydrolox engine on the upper stage, which incorporates an actively cooled metallic heat shield — the technology that is meant to let the second stage survive re-entry and fly again, rather than burning up as a disposable upper stage does on almost every rocket flying today.

Pathfinder is designed to demonstrate full and rapid reuse technologies while carrying customer payloads, and reports put its reusable payload at about three tonnes to low Earth orbit. That is a modest lift by the standards of the big rockets now flying, but the point of Pathfinder is not raw capacity. It is to prove that both stages of an orbital rocket can come home, be turned around quickly, and fly again — the capability on which the entire business case for Stoke's larger rocket depends. The company has said Pathfinder flights in 2027 and 2028 will generate operational experience while Nova Block 2 development continues in parallel.

The hardware is produced and tested at Stoke's Washington facilities, and the first flight booster shipped from Washington on November 4, bound for Cape Canaveral. Once the stages arrive at SLC-14, final mating will take place in the Horizontal Integration Facility before rollout to the pad. Ahead of the first flight, a large amount of testing is expected for both the rocket and the pad systems. The second stage has already been through its own journey: it left the rocket factory for Moses Lake, Washington, where it was due to undergo cryo proof testing and a hotfire campaign with its Andromeda engine before shipment to the launch site.

That sequence — a booster shipped, a stage in hotfire testing, a pad declared ready to receive hardware — is why the second-pad announcement lands when it does. Stoke is not planning a pad for a distant future. It is building the ground infrastructure for a flight programme that is supposed to begin within months, and for the larger vehicle that follows it within a few years.

## Nova Block 2: the bigger rocket behind the bigger pad

If Pathfinder is the proof of concept, Nova Block 2 is the business. Unveiled in September 2026, Block 2 is a larger configuration targeting 15 tonnes to low Earth orbit in a fully reusable mode — roughly six times Pathfinder's published payload figure. It will use 14 Zenith engines on the first stage, double Pathfinder's seven, and an upper stage powered by the more powerful Andromeda Block 2 propulsion system. The first flight of this variant is currently planned for 2029.

The proposed second pad is intended to support high-cadence operations for both vehicles once Block 2 enters service. That is the key to understanding why a pad for a 2029 rocket is being planned in 2026: a high-cadence launch business cannot wait until the rocket exists to start building the ground systems it needs. Environmental assessments, range safety approvals and concrete take years. Stoke is starting the clock now so that the pad is ready when the rocket is — and so that Pathfinder can use it in the meantime.

The company has positioned Block 2 to meet projected demand for constellation deployment and replenishment, the market that now dominates the launch industry. Thousands of satellites are being launched to build broadband and Earth-observation constellations, and those constellations need regular replenishment launches for years after they are first deployed. A 15-tonne fully reusable rocket, flying often from two pads at the Cape, is Stoke's answer to that demand. It is also, pointedly, a vehicle sized for the national-security launch market: the Pentagon's loan commitment is explicitly framed as support for expanding U.S. launch capacity to meet rising demand for commercial and national-security space launch.

## Nova Pathfinder vs Nova Block 2

| Feature | Nova Pathfinder | Nova Block 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Role | First orbital configuration; prove full, rapid reuse while carrying payloads | Larger follow-on; high-cadence constellation and national-security launch |
| First-stage engines | 7 Zenith full-flow staged-combustion engines | 14 Zenith engines |
| Upper-stage engine | Single Andromeda hydrolox engine | Andromeda Block 2 propulsion system |
| Upper-stage heat shield | Actively cooled metallic heat shield | Actively cooled metallic heat shield (scaled up) |
| Payload to low Earth orbit, fully reusable | About 3 tonnes (as reported) | 15 tonnes |
| First flight target | First half of 2027 | 2029 |
| Launch site | SLC-14, Cape Canaveral | SLC-14 plus second site north of SLC-14 (LC-15 area) |
| Flight programme | Flights in 2027–2028 to build operational experience | Enters service as Pathfinder experience accumulates |

The table makes the strategy legible. Pathfinder is the rehearsal; Block 2 is the performance. Every technology on the smaller rocket — the Zenith engines, the Andromeda upper stage, the actively cooled heat shield, the fast-turn pad — exists to be scaled up. And the second pad is the ground-side half of that scaling: a bigger rocket flying more often needs more than one place to launch from and land at.

## The money behind the concrete

Few rocket startups have ever been as well funded before their first orbital flight as Stoke is now. In September 2026 the company announced a $1 billion Series E funding round, with an initial closing on September 8, bringing its total capital raised to $2.3 billion. The round came alongside the unveiling of Nova Block 2, and the two announcements were clearly meant to be read together: the money pays for the bigger rocket, and the bigger rocket justifies the money.

Then, on October 26, 2026, the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital announced a $150 million conditional loan commitment for Stoke, to help build the second launch pad at Cape Canaveral. The loan is not final: Stoke must still satisfy a list of financial, legal, technical and due-diligence requirements before any money flows, and the federal funds would be combined with private capital to cover the pad's cost. The commitment amounts to about 15 percent of the Series E round, and the Pentagon's release did not disclose an interest rate, a maturity date or the total construction cost of the pad.

The Office of Strategic Capital was set up in 2022 to draw private investment into technologies seen as critical to national security, and its backing is a signal as much as it is financing. "The proposed loan from OSC, alongside private capital, would support the buildout of the company's second launchpad at its existing launch complex at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station," the Pentagon said in its statement. "The OSC commitment to Stoke Space more broadly supports the expansion of U.S. launch capacity, which is required to meet the rising demand for commercial and national security space launch by the United States Space Force." Jordan Sebold, the office's senior managing director for investments, oversees the programme that made the commitment.

The scale of the bet is worth pausing over. Stoke has raised $2.3 billion and secured a $150 million conditional federal loan, all before a single orbital flight. That is the kind of funding that, a decade ago, would have been unimaginable for a rocket startup. It reflects how far the launch market has moved: investors and the Pentagon alike now treat fully reusable rockets not as a speculative idea but as the baseline for the next generation of launch vehicles, and they are willing to pay for the ground infrastructure before the rockets that will use it have proven themselves.

## Why a second pad, and why now

The logic of a second pad is the logic of the whole reusable-rocket era. A disposable rocket needs a pad only for the hours around launch. A reusable rocket needs a pad that can receive a returning booster, inspect it, refuel it, mate it with a new upper stage and payload, and send it back up — and it needs to do that while the next mission is already being prepared. SpaceX learned this lesson with Falcon 9, whose high flight rate depends on multiple pads and droneships working in rotation, and it is now pushing the idea further with Starship, which is targeting the first tower catch of an orbital Starship on its fifteenth test flight. <a href="/blog/starship-flight-15-first-ship-catch-attempt/">Starship Flight 15: SpaceX Gears Up for the First Tower Catch of an Orbital Starship</a>

Stoke is not alone in expanding at the Cape, either. Blue Origin has leased SLC-46 to build two additional launch pads for the various versions of its New Glenn rocket, and the company is targeting a return to flight in late December after the pad explosion that destroyed a rocket and a launch mount earlier in the year. <a href="/blog/blue-origin-new-glenn-comeback/">Explosion, Rebuild, Return: Blue Origin's New Glenn Aims for a December Comeback</a> United Launch Alliance, meanwhile, is bringing its Vulcan rocket back to flight with a mission carrying six booster engines' worth of thrust, a reminder that even established operators are scaling up for the same surge in demand. <a href="/blog/vulcan-leo-1-return-to-flight-november-13/">Vulcan Leo 1 Return to Flight November 13: ULA Bets Six Boosters on Its Comeback</a> The Cape is in the middle of a construction boom, and Stoke's second pad is one more crane on a crowded skyline.

There is also a competitive clock running. The constellation market Stoke wants to serve is being filled now, not in 2029. Every year of delay is a year in which satellite operators sign launch contracts with rockets that already fly. Stoke's answer is to build the ground infrastructure in parallel with the rocket, so that when Block 2 is ready, the pad, the range approvals and the recovery operations are ready too. The Right of Entry is the first, unglamorous step in that parallel build: surveys and environmental assessments, the paperwork that has to be done before the first concrete is poured.

The timeline tells the story in miniature: a historic pad rescued from decades of silence, a startup funded to the tune of billions before its first flight, a federal loan for a second pad, and a flight programme that is supposed to begin within months. Each step depends on the one before it, and the second pad depends on all of them.

## What to watch next

The next milestone is unglamorous but decisive: the engineering studies and environmental assessments the Right of Entry authorises. Those will determine whether the northern parcel can support a second launch site, what it will cost, and how long it will take to build. The Pentagon's loan, meanwhile, still has to reach financial close — a process with no published date, and one that requires Stoke to clear the financial, legal and technical conditions attached to the commitment. Until that close, the $150 million is a promise, not cash.

Closer at hand is the hardware. The first Pathfinder booster is travelling to the Cape, its second stage is in hotfire testing, and SLC-14 is waiting to receive them both. The first half of 2027 will show whether a rocket designed to be fully reusable can get off the ground at all — and whether the pad built for rapid turnaround can handle its first real campaign. If Pathfinder flies and flies again, the second pad stops being a plan and becomes a necessity. If it does not, the second pad will be the most expensive lesson in the history of a company that has already raised $2.3 billion to learn it.

Either way, the expansion is a statement of intent that the industry will be watching. Stoke is betting that the future of launch belongs to rockets that come home, to pads that turn around in days rather than months, and to companies willing to build the infrastructure for that future before the rockets exist to use it. On a strip of Florida coastline where John Glenn once left the Earth, the company is now building the ground on which that future is supposed to land — twice over.
