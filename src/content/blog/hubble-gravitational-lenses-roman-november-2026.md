Nine galaxies, bent out of shape by gravity itself, are now the most important pictures in astronomy. On November 12, 2026, NASA released a gallery of nine gravitational lenses photographed by the Hubble Space Telescope, and the images are far more than a cosmic art show. They are the calibration set, the practice targets, and the opening evidence for the biggest dark matter experiment ever attempted — a survey by the newly launched Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope that could multiply the known population of strong lenses from a few hundred to more than one hundred thousand.

The timing is deliberate. Roman launched in August 2026 and is now on its way to its observing post, with science operations due to begin in 2027. When it starts work, it will sweep the sky in infrared light across a field of view at least two hundred times wider than Hubble's infrared view, and it will find gravitational lenses in bulk. Before that flood arrives, astronomers need to know exactly what a good lens looks like, how to model one, and how to spot the tiny imperfections in a bent arc of light that betray the presence of something invisible. That is what Hubble has just given them: nine lenses, imaged in exquisite detail, each one different, each one a lesson.

A gravitational lens forms when a massive foreground galaxy sits almost exactly in front of a more distant galaxy. The foreground galaxy's gravity bends the light of the background galaxy as it passes, warping it into arcs, stretching it into rings, and sometimes copying it several times over. Albert Einstein predicted the effect more than a century ago, in his general theory of relativity, and the first gravitational lens was only confirmed in 1979. Since then, lenses have become one of astronomy's most powerful tools, because the amount of bending reveals the amount of mass doing the bending — including mass that emits no light at all.

That is the point of the new gallery. The nine systems were not chosen at random. They were selected to show the full range of what a strong lens can look like, so that the software and the scientists preparing for Roman can learn the variety of the phenomenon before the new telescope starts delivering lenses by the thousand. Some of the panels show graceful arcs. One shows a near-complete ring of light. Another shows a single galaxy copied four times. Together, they are a sampler, a field guide, and a dare: find the dark matter hiding in these pictures, and then find it a hundred thousand times over.

## What the nine lenses show

The gallery is arranged as nine panels, labelled A through I, and no two are alike. Panel A is an arc-counterarc configuration, in which the lensed background galaxy appears as a duplicated pair of arcs. What makes it unusual is that the background galaxy is being magnified not by one foreground galaxy but by a pair of them, whose combined mass bends the light around both. It is a reminder that lenses are not always tidy single-galaxy systems; sometimes the lens itself is a small group, and the model has to account for every mass in the way.

Panel F is the showpiece. It is an Einstein ring, the configuration that occurs when the alignment between the background galaxy and the approximately spherical mass of the foreground lens is so exact that the lensed galaxy almost fully encircles the foreground galaxy. A complete ring is one of the rarest and most beautiful sights in astronomy, and a near-complete one is nearly as good: the symmetry of the ring gives modellers a powerful grip on the mass distribution of the lens, because any lump or gap in the ring is a clue about where mass sits.

Panels C and I show the opposite extreme of the same phenomenon. In each, the background galaxy is replicated four times, appearing as four smeared dots or arcs around the foreground galaxy. Panel I shows the four images as smeared dots; panel C shows a quadruply lensed galaxy with double knots that are most likely strong star-forming regions in the background galaxy. A quadruply imaged galaxy is a gift to modellers, because four images of the same object provide four independent views of the lensing mass, and the differences between them — their brightness, their positions, their shapes — constrain the mass map far more tightly than a single arc ever could.

Panel D is the oddity of the set, and in some ways the most scientifically interesting. Its foreground galaxy carries a visible dust lane, which is rare in a lensing galaxy. The lensed background galaxy could itself be an Einstein ring, with the dust lane breaking our view of the ring. That is exactly the kind of detail that Hubble's sharp, visible-light vision excels at revealing — and it is also a case where Roman's infrared eyes will add something Hubble cannot. Infrared light is less obscured by dust, so future Roman observations could help determine whether the arcs around panel D's foreground galaxy form a more complete Einstein ring than Hubble can see.

The other panels fill out the range. In panel G, the foreground galaxy appears elongated, which may be a genuine shape or a trick of perspective. In panels B and F, the foreground galaxies appear to have spiral arms — a surprise, because most of the lensing galaxies in the gallery are ellipticals, older, denser, and more compact galaxies whose mass density reaches the critical point at which lensed images of background galaxies can form. Not all of them are ellipticals, though, and researchers can use those differences to explore the evolutionary paths taken by different kinds of galaxies. Even the colours carry information: some of the magnified and warped background galaxies appear blue and some appear red. The bluish arcs indicate young stars, while the reddish arcs indicate older stars, and Roman and Hubble will explore those differences to better understand how galaxies evolved earlier in the universe.

## Found by machine, confirmed by Hubble

None of these nine lenses was found by a human being staring at the sky. They were found by software. A neural network combed the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys — the deep sky maps produced by the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona — and flagged about 3,500 lens candidates. Hubble then took short snapshot exposures of 51 of them, and confirmed every one as a genuine lens. That perfect hit rate, 51 out of 51, is itself a result: it shows that the automated search works, and it shows that the sky is full of lenses waiting to be found, if only a telescope looks in the right places with the right tools.

The nine in the gallery are a showcase drawn from that confirmed set. They are the most diverse and most instructive of the 51, chosen to demonstrate the range of configurations Roman will encounter. But the number 51 matters as much as the number nine. It is proof of concept for the whole Roman programme. If a neural network plus a few dozen Hubble snapshots can confirm 51 lenses, then a telescope with a field of view hundreds of times wider, sweeping the sky systematically for years, can plausibly find lenses by the tens of thousands.

There is still work to do on the nine themselves. Each measurement depends on a precise model of the lensing mass and on the distances to both galaxies in each system. For roughly a third of the systems, the background galaxy is too distant for DESI spectra to measure its distance, so its distance will need infrared spectroscopy from the Keck Observatory instead. That is the unglamorous, essential groundwork of the project: without accurate distances, a lens model is a shape without a scale, and the mass it implies cannot be trusted. The distances are being chased now, while Roman is still in transit.

## The nine lenses at a glance

The table below collects what NASA's release tells us about each of the nine panels, and what each one is for. Two panels, E and H, were not singled out in the release text, which is itself a reminder that a gallery is a starting point: the detailed modelling of every system, including the ones that look ordinary at first glance, is still to come.

| Panel | Configuration | What makes it special |
|---|---|---|
| A | Arc-counterarc pair | Background galaxy magnified by a pair of foreground galaxies, not one |
| B | Arc | Foreground galaxy appears to have spiral arms |
| C | Quadruply lensed | Background galaxy copied four times, with double knots likely to be star-forming regions |
| D | Possible Einstein ring | Rare dust lane in the foreground galaxy breaks the view of the ring |
| E | Lens (not detailed in release) | Part of the confirmed 51-lens sample; detailed modelling to come |
| F | Near-complete Einstein ring | Near-perfect alignment; foreground galaxy appears to have spiral arms |
| G | Arc | Foreground galaxy appears elongated, possibly a trick of perspective |
| H | Lens (not detailed in release) | Part of the confirmed 51-lens sample; detailed modelling to come |
| I | Quadruply lensed | Background galaxy replicated four times as smeared dots around the foreground |

The diversity is the point. A lens survey that only ever found clean, symmetric Einstein rings would be easy to model and easy to misinterpret. A survey that finds rings, arcs, quadruples, dust-lane lenses, and spiral lenses forces the models to be honest. If a model can explain all nine of these, it can probably explain the hundred thousand Roman will find. If it cannot explain all nine, the failure will show up early, while there is still time to fix the model.

## Why bent light can weigh dark matter

The scientific prize behind the gallery is a test of what dark matter is made of — or, more precisely, a test of whether the standard theory of dark matter survives contact with the smallest scales in the universe. Cold dark matter, the standard model, predicts that galaxies should be peppered with small dark clumps of between a million and a billion solar masses. For scale, the dark matter halo of the Milky Way is around 1.3 trillion solar masses. Clumps that small hold no stars, so they show up only through gravity, as tiny kinks in a lensed arc.

That is why the arcs matter. A smooth, undisturbed lens produces smooth, predictable arcs. A lens whose halo is studded with small dark clumps produces arcs with small imperfections — faint kinks, slight brightness changes, tiny distortions that betray a clump sitting in the way. With Hubble's high-resolution imaging and precise models, researchers should be able to detect those imperfections, and the clumps they imply. Find the clumps in the expected numbers, and the cold dark matter model passes another test. Fail to find them, and the model has a problem — one that might point toward warmer dark matter, or self-interacting dark matter, or something else entirely.

It is important to be clear about what has and has not happened. No dark matter clump has been detected in these images yet. The gallery is not an announcement of a discovery; it is the announcement of a programme. The lenses are the laboratory, the models are the instruments, and the measurements are still to come. Anyone who describes the nine panels as a detection of dark matter has misunderstood the story. What NASA has released is the starting gun.

That honesty is part of what makes the programme credible. Dark matter has a long history of claimed detections that did not survive scrutiny, and the community has learned to separate the instrument from the result. The instrument here is a population of lenses, imaged sharply, modelled carefully, and counted honestly. The result will be a number: how many clumps of a given mass exist per galaxy halo. If that number matches the cold dark matter prediction, the theory survives its hardest test yet. If it does not, astronomy will have to rethink one of its most successful ideas. Either way, the answer will be worth having.

The stakes are high because the same question echoes through the rest of modern astronomy. The James Webb Space Telescope has spent the past year mapping dark matter in three dimensions across the COSMOS-Web field, and its <a href="/blog/jwst-3d-dark-matter-map-cosmos-web-november-2026/">JWST 3D dark matter map</a> shows the cosmic web's invisible scaffolding in unprecedented detail. Webb has also been finding the seeds of the first supermassive black holes, as our report on the <a href="/blog/jwst-little-red-dots-november-2026-direct-collapse-black-holes/">JWST Little Red Dots</a> explained, and those early black holes grew inside the very dark matter haloes that lensing measures. On the largest scales, clusters like El Gordo are already testing whether dark matter interacts with itself at all, as described in our coverage of the <a href="/blog/el-gordo-two-state-self-interacting-dark-matter/">El Gordo two-state dark matter result</a>. The Hubble lens gallery is the small-scale counterpart to all of that work: same question, different ruler. Together, the large-scale maps and the small-scale clump counts will pin down dark matter from both ends.

## Hubble and Roman: a partnership, not a handover

It would be easy to read this story as a changing of the guard — the old telescope handing over to the new one — but NASA's account stresses the opposite. Hubble and Roman are complementary, and the programme is designed around their differences. Hubble views the cosmos primarily in visible and ultraviolet light, with some infrared coverage, providing a comprehensive, high-resolution view of individual objects. Roman sees primarily in infrared light and some visible, over a much larger field of view, to create panoramas of the universe. While Hubble gets a better view of individual lenses, Roman will be able to capture far more of them with its wider field of view.

The two telescopes even share the same size of primary mirror, 2.4 metres, which makes the comparison clean: the difference is not aperture, it is field of view and wavelength. Roman's wide-field instrument is designed to gather large samples; Hubble supplies the complementary ultraviolet and visible-light detail, alongside some infrared coverage, for the objects that deserve a closer look. If Roman finds a particularly interesting lens, researchers may use Hubble to take follow-up observations, using its visible and ultraviolet prowess for individual objects. And Hubble's pre-Roman observations, including the gallery published on November 12, establish the reference frame against which Roman's flood of new lenses will be judged.

The table below sets out the partnership as NASA describes it, with the numbers that define the scale of the change.

| | Hubble Space Telescope | Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope |
|---|---|---|
| Primary mirror | 2.4 metres | 2.4 metres |
| Main wavelengths | Visible and ultraviolet, some infrared | Infrared, some visible |
| Strength | High-resolution views of individual objects | Wide panoramas; huge survey speed |
| Field of view | Narrow | At least 200 times wider than Hubble's infrared view |
| Strong lenses known today | A few hundred worth studying closely | Expected to discover more than 100,000 |
| Role in this programme | Sharp imaging, follow-up, and the training set | Bulk discovery and population statistics |
| Status | In service since 1990 | Launched August 2026; science operations begin 2027 |

The simulated yields make the scale concrete. A 2025 study by Bryce Wedig and colleagues simulated galaxy lenses and Roman detector effects, and in one stated exposure scenario predicted roughly 160,000 detectable lens systems, with about 500 suitable for detailed dark-matter substructure work. Those figures are simulated yields, not objects Roman has already counted, and NASA's accompanying account notes that the calculation used an earlier survey design, so the numbers should not be treated as a guaranteed final harvest. But they show the shape of the change: from studying remarkable lenses one at a time, as Tansu Daylan of Washington University in St. Louis put it, to studying them as a population. That shift, from anecdote to statistics, is what turns a clever technique into a decisive test.

## From Einstein's prediction to Roman's harvest

The gallery sits at the end of a long chain of ideas and instruments, and the chain is worth tracing, because it shows how slowly and how deliberately a decisive experiment is built.

## A century of bent light

| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1915 | Einstein publishes general relativity, predicting that mass bends light |
| 1979 | First gravitational lens confirmed: the Twin Quasar, one object seen twice |
| 1990 | Hubble Space Telescope launches, bringing sharp visible-light imaging above the atmosphere |
| 2020s | DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys map the sky; a neural network flags about 3,500 lens candidates |
| 2026 | Hubble snapshot exposures confirm 51 of the candidates as genuine lenses |
| 12 Nov 2026 | NASA releases the nine-lens gallery as Roman's training set and reference sample |
| Aug 2026 | Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope launches aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy |
| 2027 | Roman science operations begin; the lens harvest starts in earnest |

Every step in that timeline was necessary for the next. Without Einstein's prediction, there would have been no reason to look. Without the 1979 discovery, lensing would have remained a theoretical curiosity. Without Hubble's three and a half decades of sharp imaging, there would be no reference set worth training software on. Without DESI's sky maps and the neural network that searched them, the 51 confirmed lenses would still be hiding in the data. And without Roman, the 51 would remain a curiosity rather than the seed of a population of a hundred thousand.

The timeline also explains why the gallery matters now, and not five years ago or five years from now. Roman is in transit. Its science operations begin in 2027. The months between launch and first light are the last chance to prepare: to train the search software on real Hubble arcs before turning it loose on Roman images, to build the lens models, to measure the missing distances, and to settle the analysis methods while the data are still small enough to argue about. Once Roman starts delivering lenses by the thousand, there will be no time to build the tools from scratch. The gallery is the dress rehearsal, published while the rehearsal can still change the performance.

## What happens next

Two parallel programmes in Roman's Cycle 1 science operations are building the software infrastructure to handle what is coming. The first, led by Simon Birrer of Stony Brook University, with co-investigators Tansu Daylan, Xinyu Huang of the University of San Francisco, and Justin Pierel of the Space Telescope Science Institute, is building what the team describes as the definitive strong-lens catalog from Roman's High-Latitude Wide-Area Survey. The programme uses the mejiro pipeline, a simulation tool that generates synthetic Roman observations, so that the astronomy community can test and refine its lens-finding algorithms before Roman's science operations begin in 2027.

The second programme, called LensQuery and led by Patrick Kelly of the University of Minnesota, will use machine learning to identify gravitational lenses in real time, characterise each lens's properties with uncertainties, and map the precise pixel footprint of every lensing arc in Roman's images. Where Birrer's programme builds the catalog, Kelly's builds the machine that fills it. Together, they are the reason the Hubble gallery is described as a training set: the nine lenses, and the 51 behind them, are the labelled examples the algorithms will learn from.

Behind both programmes sits the modelling work of Huang's team, whose GIGA-Lens forward-modelling pipeline is designed to fit lens models fast enough to keep up with Roman's data rate. Forward modelling means building a simulated lens — a foreground mass, a background galaxy, a set of predicted arcs — and adjusting it until it matches the observation. Done well, it extracts the mass map, the distances, and the substructure limits from each system. Done at Roman's scale, it has to be automated, and the automation has to be trustworthy. The nine Hubble lenses are the test cases the pipeline must pass before it is trusted with a hundred thousand.

There is also the follow-up work that no space telescope can do alone. For roughly a third of the gallery systems, the background galaxy is too distant for DESI spectra, and its distance will need infrared spectroscopy from the Keck Observatory. Those observations are the slow, ground-based complement to the space-based survey, and they will continue for years as Roman's catalog grows. The partnership is not only between Hubble and Roman; it is between space and ground, between survey and follow-up, between machine and human.

The gallery is also a reminder of how the Great Observatories work together. As NASA put it in the release, images like these are an example of the collaborative efforts that routinely take place between NASA's Great Observatories to build a comprehensive picture of the cosmos. As Roman joins the family of large space observatories that includes Hubble, the Chandra X-ray Observatory, and the James Webb Space Telescope, a new window opens onto the universe, revealing sights previously hidden while expanding our cosmic knowledge for all of humanity. That is grand language, but in this case it is also literal: Roman's window is wider than any that has come before it, and the nine lenses are the first thing seen through it, in preview.

## Why nine pictures can change a field

It is worth stepping back and asking why nine pictures, of galaxies most people will never have heard of, should matter to anyone who is not a professional astronomer. The answer is that they are the visible edge of an invisible question, and the question is one of the biggest in science: what is most of the universe made of?

Ordinary matter — stars, planets, gas, people — makes up only a small fraction of the mass of the universe. The rest is dark matter and dark energy, and dark matter is the part we can, in principle, map. We cannot see it, we cannot touch it, and we do not know what particle, if any, it is made of. But we can weigh it, because gravity does not care whether mass shines. A gravitational lens is a scale, and a galaxy is the thing being weighed. Every arc in the new gallery is a weighing, and every kink in an arc is a weighing of something smaller still.

The nine lenses are also a demonstration of a method. Astronomy is moving, decisively, from an era in which a telescope pointed at one interesting object at a time to an era in which telescopes survey the whole sky and the discoveries are made by software. DESI's maps, the neural network that searched them, the mejiro simulations, the LensQuery machine-learning pipeline — none of these existed when Hubble launched in 1990. The gallery is a product of that shift, and it is also a test of it: if the software can find 51 lenses and confirm all of them, then the software can be trusted with the next hundred thousand. If it cannot, better to learn that now, from nine pictures, than in 2027, from a flood.

There is a quieter lesson in the gallery, too. The nine lenses are beautiful, and beauty is not incidental to the science. The reason an Einstein ring is scientifically valuable is the same reason it is visually arresting: its symmetry. The reason a quadruply lensed galaxy is valuable is the same reason it is striking: the same object, four times, arranged around a point. The human eye is good at spotting symmetry and its violations, which is why a gallery like this one works as a training set for machines and as an invitation to people. The pictures look like jewellery, as one report put it. To the people modelling them, each one is a scale with something invisible sitting on it. Both descriptions are true, and both are necessary. A field that cannot show the public what it is doing will not keep the public's trust, and a field that cannot measure what it shows will not keep its own.

## The kicker

The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is named for the astronomer who made Hubble possible, the woman known as the mother of Hubble, who spent her career arguing that a great space telescope was worth building. It is fitting that Hubble, the telescope she fought for, is now preparing the way for the telescope that bears her name — and fitting, too, that the preparation takes the form of nine pictures of bent light. Roman's own survey will decide whether the cold dark matter model survives, and the answer will come not from a single dramatic image but from the patient accumulation of a hundred thousand lenses, each one weighed, each one modelled, each one counted.

The nine lenses are the first instalment of that count. They are already the most carefully chosen lenses ever published, and they will soon be the least remarkable lenses anyone has — because Roman will find more of them before breakfast than Hubble has found in a decade. That is not a diminishment of the gallery. It is the point of it. A training set is meant to be surpassed by the thing it trains. When Roman's first lenses arrive in 2027, astronomers will know what they are looking at, because they will have seen it before, nine times, in Hubble's gallery. And somewhere in those hundred thousand arcs, in a kink too small to see with the naked eye, may sit the answer to what most of the universe is made of. The hunt begins with nine warped galaxies and a telescope on its way. The rest is counting.
