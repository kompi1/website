United Launch Alliance has put a date on its comeback. The company's Vulcan rocket is targeted to return to flight on Friday, November 13, 2026, carrying 40 satellites for Amazon's Leo internet constellation from Space Launch Complex 41 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. It will be the fifth flight of a rocket that debuted in January 2024, the first flight in nine months, and the first time Vulcan has ever flown with six strap-on solid rocket boosters. The date is still pending range approval, and no launch time has been announced, but the announcement itself, made on October 28, ends months of uncertainty about when one of America's newest heavy-lift rockets would fly again.

That combination — a new date, a bigger configuration, and a payload that matters to two very different customers — is what makes this flight more than a routine satellite deployment. For Amazon, Vulcan Leo 1, also designated LV-01, is the opening mission of a 38-launch Vulcan campaign to build out a broadband constellation that already has nearly 400 satellites in orbit. For the United States Space Force, which certified Vulcan for national security launches in March 2025 and then watched it ground itself after a booster anomaly in February 2026, the flight is an unofficial test: the data from a commercial mission carrying a redesigned booster nozzle will weigh heavily in any decision to clear Vulcan for military payloads again. One flight, in other words, has to prove a fix, open a constellation campaign, and restore confidence in a rocket program all at once.

## What ULA announced on October 28

United Launch Alliance said its Vulcan rocket would return to flight on Friday, November 13, after a nine-month grounding caused by problems with its solid rocket boosters. The mission, Vulcan Leo 1, will carry 40 satellites for Amazon's Leo internet service, and it will be the first time Vulcan has been fitted with six strap-on solid rocket boosters. Amazon, in its own update on October 28, confirmed the same target: liftoff is targeted for November 13, pending range approval, from Space Launch Complex 41, with the exact launch time to be confirmed closer to the mission.

The qualifiers matter, and they are worth stating plainly. A target date pending range approval is not a guarantee. Range approval is the sign-off that clears a launch window on the Florida coast, and it sits outside ULA's direct control. No backup date has been publicly announced for this mission. Readers who see November 13 presented as a certainty should treat that as an overstatement of what ULA and Amazon actually said. What is certain is the plan: a Friday in mid-November, a specific rocket configuration, a specific payload, and a specific pad. Everything else — the hour, the final clearance, the weather — will be settled in the days immediately before launch.

The mission will be Vulcan's fifth flight since its debut in 2024, and it introduces several firsts at once. It is the first Vulcan flight with six Northrop Grumman GEM 63XL solid rocket boosters, the first to use the longer payload fairing at this scale for Amazon's satellites, and the first Vulcan mission for Amazon after eight Leo deployments on ULA's Atlas V. Amazon says the 40 satellites are encapsulated inside a 70-foot-tall fairing, the largest Leo payload the company has flown to date, and that they will be deployed from an adapter on the Centaur upper stage over the course of about half an hour. The rocket will fly in its most powerful configuration, designated VC6L, with two Blue Origin BE-4 engines on the first stage and two RL10 engines on the Centaur V upper stage completing the journey to orbit.

Assembly of the vehicle has been under way for months, and the timeline ULA and Amazon have described shows a campaign that was already deep into preparation before the date was announced. Assembly began on May 14, when the first-stage booster was hoisted onto its launch platform inside ULA's new Vertical Integration Facility, dubbed VIF-A, with the "A" standing for Amazon. Four days later, the first LEO-optimized Centaur upper stage was stacked onto the booster. In August, teams rolled the integrated stack out to the pad at Space Launch Complex 41 to conduct fueling tests, then returned it to VIF-A, where its six solid rocket boosters were recently installed. The payload fairing containing the 40 Leo satellites departed Amazon's satellite processing facility near the former shuttle landing strip for attachment to the rocket on October 21. Amazon has also said a second vehicle is already assembled for the Vulcan Leo 2 mission in ULA's original Vertical Integration Facility, a sign that the company expects this campaign to move quickly if the first flight goes well.

## Nine months on the ground: two nozzle failures

To understand why November 13 carries so much weight, it helps to retrace how Vulcan ended up grounded. The trouble centers on the GEM 63XL solid rocket boosters supplied by Northrop Grumman. These are graphite-epoxy motors strapped to Vulcan's core stage to provide additional thrust at liftoff; each one delivers about 459,600 pounds of thrust, according to reporting on the configuration. Vulcan has flown with two boosters and with four boosters. It has never flown with six, which means Leo 1 will test the redesigned nozzle across six units simultaneously, with six potential failure points at liftoff instead of two or four.

The first warning came on Vulcan's second flight, the Cert-2 certification mission in October 2024, when a nozzle on one of the two GEM 63XL boosters disintegrated during ascent. The rocket still reached its intended orbit, because the methane-fueled first stage and hydrogen-fueled upper stage were able to compensate for the reduced and asymmetric thrust. ULA thought it had put the problem behind it. Then, on February 12, 2026, a similar nozzle failure afflicted one of the four boosters less than 30 seconds into Vulcan's fourth flight, the USSF-87 mission for the Space Force. Once again, the core and upper stages compensated and the mission reached its intended orbit, carrying two satellites for the Space Force's Geosynchronous Space Situational Awareness Program and a training and demonstration payload. But a second failure of the same kind of hardware, on a national security mission, could not be treated as a one-off. Vulcan has not flown since.

An industry-government team comprising ULA, Northrop Grumman, and the U.S. Space Force was convened to investigate the most recent incident. ULA has not provided specific details about the findings of that investigation, but in its October announcement the company said the integrated team had made great strides in improving its understanding of the solid rocket booster anomaly and had identified several potential contributors, which have been mitigated through design and process improvements to the nozzle. The upcoming launch, ULA said, will utilize the improved nozzle design, and additional testing and analysis will continue in parallel with the mission. By flying the improved design, the company added, ULA gains valuable flight data that validates these improvements and is the most beneficial path in support of all its customers. Earlier in 2026, Northrop Grumman performed a test firing of a 63XL motor as part of the effort to resolve the nozzle problem.

That framing — fly the fix, gather the data, keep testing in parallel — is honest about the limits of ground testing. A static test firing can validate a nozzle design under controlled conditions, but it cannot reproduce every condition of a real ascent: the vibration, the aerodynamic loads, the interaction between six boosters firing together, and the asymmetric thrust that follows if one of them underperforms. Flight data is the evidence that matters, and Leo 1 is the flight that will provide it. That is also why the stakes of a third anomaly would be so high. Another GEM 63XL failure would not merely delay a satellite deployment; it would call the redesign itself into question, put Amazon's 38-launch Vulcan campaign under severe renegotiation pressure, and push any return of Vulcan to national security missions further into the future.

## Amazon Leo: a constellation waiting on Vulcan

For Amazon, the timing of Vulcan's return is inseparable from the state of its Leo constellation. Amazon Leo, renamed from Project Kuiper on November 13, 2025, is the company's low Earth orbit broadband network, intended to compete with SpaceX's Starlink. The company has booked a total of 38 Vulcan launches to assemble the constellation, by far the largest single commitment in its launch manifest. Previous Leo launches used Atlas V, Ariane 6, and Falcon 9 vehicles, and Amazon had hoped to use Vulcan earlier precisely because it can launch more Leo satellites than any rocket other than Blue Origin's New Glenn. The loss of the first New Glenn rocket slated to carry Leo satellites in a launch pad explosion in May 2026 was another setback to the rollout, although the satellites were not attached for that ill-fated test firing.

Against that background, the arithmetic of Leo 1 is straightforward. The mission will add 40 satellites to the nearly 400 Amazon Leo spacecraft already in orbit, the largest single Leo payload the company has flown. The satellites will join a constellation that is still in its build-out phase, where every launch adds capacity and coverage, and where the pace of launches determines how quickly the service can expand. The Atlas V has continued deploying Leo satellites during Vulcan's hiatus — another 29 were added on a July 2 launch from Space Launch Complex 41 — but Atlas V is a retiring rocket with a finite number of flights remaining, and it cannot carry as many Leo satellites per launch as Vulcan in its six-booster configuration. Vulcan, in its VC6L form, is rated for roughly 27,200 kilograms to low Earth orbit, enough for a large stack of broadband satellites in a single flight.

The commercial logic is therefore clear, and so is the risk. Amazon has tied the bulk of its constellation deployment to a rocket that has not flown in nine months and that is about to fly its most complex configuration for the first time, with a redesigned component whose previous version failed twice. That is not a criticism of the decision; it is a description of the position Amazon is in. The company has parallel capacity on other rockets, a second Vulcan already assembled for Leo 2, and a dedicated processing and integration infrastructure at Cape Canaveral built around the Vulcan campaign. But the fastest path to a full constellation runs through Vulcan, and Vulcan's fastest path back to regular flight runs through a successful Leo 1.

## The Space Force is watching, too

The other customer watching November 13 is not buying broadband. The U.S. Space Force certified Vulcan for National Security Space Launch missions in March 2025, after two certification flights, and then moved some military missions to SpaceX while the anomaly investigation continued. Officials have said they want data from commercial Vulcan flights before approving the rocket for national security payloads again. Brigadier General Eric Zarybnisky, the Space Force's portfolio acquisition executive for space access, said plainly on October 21 that the decision would depend a lot on what officials see, not only in the engineering and tests being done on the ground, but also in the flight data from the commercial missions ULA flies. Spaceflight Now has asked the Space Force what work, if any, remains before Vulcan is cleared to fly national security payloads again, and had not received a response at the time of its October 28 report.

That linkage — commercial flight data as an informal national security validation test — is the defining fact about November 13 that ULA's announcement did not spell out. Vulcan Centaur is carrying 40 Amazon broadband satellites and, in effect, the informal blessing of the national security space program, with neither guaranteed and both riding on the same flight. A clean flight with nominal booster performance would give the Space Force the kind of evidence that ground tests alone cannot provide: six redesigned nozzles performing together through the most stressful seconds of ascent, with no anomalous plume, no asymmetric thrust event, and a clean separation and Centaur deployment sequence. A flight with another anomaly, even one the core stage compensates for, would give the opposite evidence, and would likely keep Vulcan sidelined from national security missions while a deeper redesign is pursued.

This is a familiar pattern in the history of launch vehicles, and it is worth naming it. Rockets earn their reliability record one flight at a time, and a rocket that has suffered the same failure twice carries a burden of proof that a new rocket does not. Vulcan's first two flights, including the Cert-2 anomaly, were enough to earn certification in a period when the Space Force was eager to add a second certified heavy-lift provider alongside SpaceX. The USSF-87 anomaly changed the calculation, not because the mission failed — it did not — but because it showed that the first anomaly had not been fully understood or fully fixed. Certification, in that light, is not a permanent status but a judgment that must be re-earned after a failure, and flight data is the currency in which it is re-earned.

The broader launch market adds another layer. With Vulcan grounded, SpaceX has absorbed missions that might otherwise have flown on Vulcan, and the Space Force's desire for assured access to space — the principle that the United States should never depend on a single launch provider — is directly served by getting Vulcan back to regular flight.

A healthy Vulcan program means competition, redundancy, and resilience in the national security launch enterprise. A Vulcan program stuck on the ground means a single point of failure, however reliable that single provider may be.

## What the VC6L configuration actually means

It is easy to treat "six boosters" as a marketing detail. In engineering terms, it is a step change. Vulcan's core stage is powered by two BE-4 engines burning liquefied natural gas and liquid oxygen, a combination chosen for performance and reusability of the design, if not yet of the hardware. The Centaur V upper stage, powered by two RL10 engines burning liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, is the stage that actually delivers satellites to their precise orbits and, on Leo 1, will deploy the 40 satellites over about half an hour. The solid rocket boosters are the muscle at liftoff: they ignite at launch, burn for roughly the first two minutes of flight, and then separate, having provided the extra thrust needed to lift a heavy payload off the pad.

Adding boosters increases payload capacity, but it also increases complexity in ways that are not linear. Six boosters must ignite together, burn symmetrically, and separate cleanly. If one underperforms, the thrust becomes asymmetric, and the core stage's guidance system must compensate by gimballing its engines and adjusting its trajectory — exactly what happened on Cert-2 and USSF-87, when the core and upper stages compensated successfully. That compensation capability is a strength of the Vulcan design, and it is why both anomalous flights still reached their intended orbits. But compensation is a safety net, not a plan. A rocket that routinely needs its safety net is a rocket whose margins are being consumed, and a redesigned nozzle is supposed to restore those margins so the net is not needed.

The long payload fairing on Leo 1 is another first worth noting. Amazon's 40 satellites are encapsulated inside a 70-foot-tall fairing, and the fairing must protect them through the aerodynamic stress of ascent, then separate cleanly at the right moment to expose the satellites and the Centaur adapter for deployment. Fairing separation is a routine event on modern rockets, but it is never trivial, and on a mission carrying the largest Leo payload to date, the deployment sequence — 40 satellites released from an adapter over about half an hour — is itself a carefully choreographed operation. Each satellite must separate without colliding with its neighbors, orient itself, and begin the process of raising itself to its operational orbit. The launch is only the first step; the deployment is the second, and both must go well for the mission to count as a full success.

## Why Friday the 13th is a fitting date

There is an unavoidable piece of color in this story, and it would be dishonest to ignore it: the return to flight is targeted for Friday, November 13. Rocket engineers are, by reputation and by necessity, a superstitious-adjacent profession — not in the sense of believing in luck, but in the sense of respecting how much can go wrong and how little margin separates success from failure. A Friday the 13th launch, after nine months grounded by two failures of the same component, is the kind of coincidence that writes its own headlines. ULA has not commented on the date's folklore, and there is no reason to think the date was chosen for its symbolism; launch dates are set by range availability, vehicle readiness, and payload processing, not by calendar superstition. But the symbolism will be present in every newsroom and every watch party on the day, and it will sharpen the narrative either way: a clean flight on Friday the 13th becomes a story about a program that faced its bad luck down, and an anomaly on Friday the 13th becomes a story nobody will be able to resist telling.

The more serious version of that point is about risk communication. ULA's October statement is careful: it describes potential contributors that have been mitigated, an improved nozzle design, and additional testing and analysis continuing in parallel with the mission. It does not claim the problem is solved. That restraint is appropriate, and it is also strategically wise. A company that overclaims a fix and then suffers a third failure loses more credibility than a company that describes its fix as a step validated by flight data. The public, the Space Force, and Amazon will all judge Leo 1 by what actually happens on the pad and in the first two minutes of flight, not by what was promised in advance. The most useful thing ULA can do between now and November 13 is what it is already doing: finish the vehicle, complete the testing, secure range approval, and let the flight speak.

## What to watch between now and launch

Several things will happen, or fail to happen, in the two weeks between the announcement and the target date, and each is worth watching. First, range approval: until the Eastern Range clears the November 13 window, the date remains a target. Second, the launch time: Amazon has said it will be confirmed closer to the mission, and the time will determine viewing conditions, the trajectory over the Atlantic, and the timing of satellite deployment. Third, any further statement from the Space Force about what remains before Vulcan can resume national security launches; Spaceflight Now's question is still open, and an answer would clarify how much weight Leo 1 carries in that decision. Fourth, weather: mid-November on the Florida coast can bring fronts, winds, and clouds that scrub launches for reasons entirely unrelated to the rocket, and a weather scrub would be the least dramatic but most common reason for a slip.

Beyond the launch itself, the deployment sequence deserves attention. Forty satellites deployed over about half an hour from a Centaur adapter is a busy sequence, and confirmation that all 40 are healthy, communicating, and raising their orbits will come over hours and days, not minutes. Amazon's constellation build-out will then be measured not by a single launch but by the cadence that follows: Leo 2 is already assembled, and the pace at which Vulcan can fly Leo 2, Leo 3, and beyond will determine whether the 38-launch campaign stays on schedule or slips further. A single successful flight reopens the campaign; a regular cadence sustains it.

There is also a longer horizon to keep in mind. Vulcan was designed as ULA's next-generation rocket, replacing both Atlas V and Delta IV Heavy, and its success or failure will shape the American launch market for a decade. Atlas V is retiring, Delta IV Heavy is retired, and the heavy-lift market is increasingly dominated by SpaceX's Falcon Heavy and, eventually, Starship, alongside Blue Origin's New Glenn. Vulcan's niche — a reliable, certified, medium-to-heavy lift vehicle with a long heritage of ULA mission assurance — depends on it flying regularly and flying cleanly. Nine months on the ground has not erased that niche, but it has made the next flight the most important in the program's short history.

## A comeback measured in minutes

In the end, the story of Vulcan Leo 1 will be decided in about two minutes. That is roughly how long the six GEM 63XL boosters will burn, and it is the window in which the redesigned nozzles must perform nominally, symmetrically, and without the anomalous plume that marked the two previous failures. If they do, the rest of the mission — Centaur burns, fairing separation, satellite deployment over half an hour — will proceed as a well-rehearsed sequence, and the headlines will be about a comeback, a constellation, and a rocket back in business. If they do not, the core stage will once again be asked to compensate, the mission may still reach orbit, and the conversation will immediately turn to whether a third failure means a deeper redesign, a longer grounding, and a renegotiation of the campaign that depends on this rocket.

That is the honest shape of the risk, and it is why November 13 is worth watching even for readers who do not follow launch vehicles closely. Spaceflight is often presented as a series of triumphs — launches, landings, landings on other worlds — but its real texture is closer to this: a problem identified, a fix designed, a test fired on the ground, and then a flight that either validates the fix or sends engineers back to their data. Vulcan has done the first three steps. On November 13, pending range approval and weather and all the other variables that govern a launch day, it will attempt the fourth. Forty Amazon satellites, a 38-launch campaign, a national security certification, and the reputation of a rocket program are all riding on the same two minutes of fire. For a rocket returning from nine months on the ground, that is exactly as it should be: the comeback is not announced, it is flown.
