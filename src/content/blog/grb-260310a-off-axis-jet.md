For fifty-three seconds on March 10, 2026, a flash of gamma rays swept past Earth and barely raised an eyebrow. It was the kind of signal astronomers see often: a long gamma-ray burst, apparently unremarkable, carrying far less energy than the most powerful explosions of its kind. Telescopes logged it, catalogued it as GRB 260310A, and moved on. Then the afterglow began, and it refused to behave like anything modest at all.

Over the following hours, the fading point of light in visible wavelengths did something afterglows are not supposed to do. It grew brighter, steadily, until it outshone the entire galaxy that hosted it. Weeks later, just as the X-ray glow seemed to be fading for good, it brightened again, and kept shining for months. When astronomers finally assembled the full picture, the verdict was startling: this supposedly ordinary explosion had produced one of the brightest afterglows recorded in the past twenty years, ranking among the top one percent in X-rays and visible light and the top five percent in radio waves. The explosion had not been weak. Earth had simply been watching it from the wrong angle.

That is the conclusion of an international team led by researchers at the University of Rome Tor Vergata, whose study appears in The Astrophysical Journal Letters under the title "An Extremely Bright Slow-rising Afterglow from an Off-axis Jet in GRB 260310A." The lead author is Yu-Han Yang, a postdoctoral researcher in the university's BHianca group. The paper's argument is elegant in its simplicity and far-reaching in its implications: the jet that powered GRB 260310A was pointed away from Earth, so our telescopes caught only its outer edges at first. As the jet slowed and its light spread over a wider range of angles, the more energetic heart of the explosion gradually came into view, producing the slow-rising, exceptionally bright afterglow that puzzled observers for months.

## A flash that did not add up

Gamma-ray bursts are the most luminous explosions known in the universe, brief flashes of the most energetic form of light, produced when massive stars collapse or when compact objects collide. Long bursts, lasting more than a couple of seconds, are associated with the deaths of massive stars: a star many times the mass of the Sun exhausts its fuel, its core collapses, and the collapse launches jets of material outward at nearly the speed of light. When one of those jets points at Earth, we see a brilliant gamma-ray flash, followed by an afterglow that fades across the spectrum as the jet slams into the gas around the dying star.

GRB 260310A followed the first half of that script and then departed from it. NASA's Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope detected the burst on March 10, 2026, and measured a flash lasting about 53 seconds, a typical duration for a long burst. But the energy it appeared to release was modest: roughly 3.5 × 10^50 ergs in gamma rays, far below the output of the most powerful long bursts. On that evidence alone, astronomers had little reason to expect anything extraordinary to follow.

What followed was extraordinary. The optical afterglow brightened gradually over several hours, rather than fading from the start, until it temporarily outshone its host galaxy. The X-ray afterglow, tracked for about 68 days, faded steeply and then brightened again between roughly days 18 and 30, with no change in the character of the X-ray light itself. Observations continued for months across X-rays, visible light and radio waves, drawing on NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory, the European Space Agency's XMM-Newton, the China-ESA Einstein Probe, the NuSTAR X-ray telescope, the Large Binocular Telescope, the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array, and ground-based observatories including Calar Alto in Spain. It was the kind of campaign that only a genuinely puzzling object attracts, and the puzzle only deepened as the data accumulated.

The comparison samples make the scale of the surprise concrete. The team compared GRB 260310A with hundreds of other bursts whose distances are known: 485 in X-rays, 467 in visible light and 64 in radio. From Earth, its X-ray brightness ranked in the top one percent, its visible-light peak outshone 99 percent of the sample, and its radio signal beat 95 percent. Yet its gamma-ray output had been unremarkable. Something about the geometry of the explosion, not its intrinsic power, had to explain the mismatch.

## The flashlight seen from the side

The explanation the team proposes rests on one of the most basic facts about relativistic jets: they are beamed. Material moving at close to the speed of light does not radiate equally in all directions. Its light is concentrated forward, along the direction of motion, like the beam of a flashlight. An observer sitting inside that beam sees a blazing source. An observer sitting outside it sees something far dimmer, because most of the light is flying elsewhere. The faster the material moves, the narrower the beam and the sharper the difference between the two views.

GRB 260310A, the team argues, is a case of the second view. Earth lay outside the jet's most intensely beamed light. The initial gamma-ray flash we recorded came from the jet's outer wings, the broader, slower-moving material surrounding its core, rather than from the core itself. That is why the flash looked modest: we were never in the path of the explosion's fiercest radiation.

But jets do not stay fast forever. As the outflow ploughs into the gas surrounding the dying star, it decelerates, and as it decelerates, its beam widens. Light that was once confined to a narrow cone spreads over a broader range of angles, and regions of the jet that were previously invisible to a side-on observer gradually come into view. Yang described the process in plain terms: "We looked at it from a side angle. As the jet slowed down, its light became visible over a wider range of angles, allowing us to probe the jet's shape, from its outer edges toward its inner core."

That widening beam explains the afterglow's strange behaviour. The slow rise in visible light over the first hours reflects the outer jet material coming into view as it decelerated. The later X-ray rebrightening, between days 18 and 30, marks the moment when the narrow, energetic core itself slowed enough for its beam to sweep across Earth's line of sight. In the team's model, the wide outer component spreads about 16 degrees from the jet's centre line, while the core is confined to only about 0.8 degrees. The early glow traces the wings; the late flare traces the core. The two components, seen in sequence, reveal the jet's structure the way a lighthouse beam sweeping past reveals the shape of the lamp.

## A supernova behind the burst

Before the geometry could be modelled, the team needed to confirm what kind of explosion GRB 260310A actually was. That confirmation came from Calar Alto Observatory in Spain, where observations revealed the telltale spectral signature of a supernova: a broad-lined Type Ic supernova, the kind produced when a massive star sheds its outer layers and then collapses. The paper describes GRB 260310A as "a nearby long-duration gamma-ray burst at z ≃ 0.153 associated with a broad-lined Type Ic supernova."

The redshift of about 0.153 places the explosion roughly two billion light-years away. On cosmic scales that is close: the burst sits among the closest three percent of gamma-ray bursts, which is part of why it could be followed in such detail for so long. A more distant burst would have faded beyond the reach of most telescopes within days. GRB 260310A stayed bright enough to track for months, and that long baseline is what made the off-axis interpretation possible. Without the late X-ray flare, the jet's core might never have been recognised at all.

The supernova connection also anchors the story in a familiar narrative. Long gamma-ray bursts are the death cries of massive stars, and the Type Ic classification tells astronomers that the progenitor had already lost its outer hydrogen and helium envelopes before it collapsed, leaving a bare, compact core. When such a core collapses, it can form a black hole or a rapidly spinning neutron star, and the infalling material can launch the jets that produce the burst. GRB 260310A fits that picture, with one crucial twist: the jets were not aimed at us, and so the explosion's true power was hidden behind a viewing angle.

That twist matters beyond this single event. If a burst as powerful as GRB 260310A can masquerade as a modest one, then the population of gamma-ray bursts we have catalogued almost certainly contains other misjudged explosions. Some bursts dismissed as weak may in fact be powerful jets seen from the side. Others may be even more extreme than they appear. The paper's authors note that future sky surveys could uncover more of these overlooked explosions, revealing how often dying stars launch powerful jets that miss Earth entirely.

## The hidden core and its staggering energy

The most eye-catching number in the study is also the most speculative, and the team is careful to present it as such. In the most extreme scenario the authors consider, had the jet's core pointed straight at Earth, the energy an observer would have inferred could top 10^55 ergs. That figure, if taken at face value, would place the explosion among the most energetic events ever recorded. But it is an extrapolation, not a measurement: it depends on the assumed structure of the jet, the angle at which we viewed it, and the model used to reconstruct the core's properties from the light that eventually reached us.

The honest way to read that number is as a measure of how much viewing angle can distort our sense of an explosion's power. A jet's apparent brightness depends enormously on whether we sit inside its beam. Move a few degrees off-axis and the same explosion can look orders of magnitude weaker. GRB 260310A demonstrates that distortion in action, and the 10^55 erg figure shows how large the correction could be in the most extreme case. It is not a claim that this burst was, in fact, the most powerful ever seen. It is a warning that our catalogue of burst energies is filtered through geometry, and that some of the entries in that catalogue may be badly wrong.

There is a second, quieter lesson in the study, and it concerns how rarely astronomers get to watch a burst for long enough to catch a late flare. The team examined more than 1,300 long-duration bursts in the Swift satellite's X-ray afterglow archive and found that only 13 had observations extending beyond 15 days. Most bursts are followed intensively for a few days and then abandoned as they fade, which means that late rebrightenings like GRB 260310A's would usually be missed entirely. The implication is that off-axis jets may be more common than the record suggests, simply because the observations needed to recognise them are rarely made.

That is a practical problem with a practical remedy. Longer monitoring campaigns, and survey instruments that revisit the sky on timescales of weeks and months, could catch more of these slow-rising afterglows. The Einstein Probe, which contributed to the GRB 260310A campaign, is designed in part for exactly that kind of wide-field, long-duration monitoring. As more such instruments come online, the population of misjudged bursts should come into sharper focus.

## How the campaign came together

The GRB 260310A study is also a portrait of modern time-domain astronomy, in which no single telescope tells the whole story. Fermi's detection triggered alerts that sent telescopes around the world and in orbit slewing toward the burst's position within hours. Chandra's sharp X-ray vision confirmed that the late flare came from the burst itself and not from a nearby, unrelated source, a crucial check given how crowded the sky can be at X-ray wavelengths. XMM-Newton and NuSTAR added X-ray coverage at different energies. The Very Large Array tracked the radio afterglow as the jet's shock wave expanded into the surrounding gas. The Large Binocular Telescope imaged the optical afterglow beside its host galaxy, and Calar Alto supplied the supernova spectrum that tied the burst to a collapsing massive star.

Each instrument contributed a piece of the puzzle, and the pieces only made sense together. The gamma-ray data alone suggested a modest burst. The optical data alone suggested a slow-rising afterglow. The X-ray data alone showed a late flare. The radio data alone showed an unusually bright shock. It was the combination, interpreted through a structured-jet model with a narrow core and a wide outer layer, that turned a collection of anomalies into a coherent physical picture. That is often how progress happens in transient astronomy: not with a single decisive observation, but with a long, multi-wavelength campaign that slowly narrows the range of possible explanations.

The campaign also illustrates why nearby bursts are so valuable. At a redshift of about 0.153, GRB 260310A was close enough that its host galaxy could be studied, its supernova spectrum could be measured, and its afterglow could be followed for months. The most distant bursts, seen when the universe was young, are scientifically precious for other reasons, but they fade too fast and are too faint for this kind of detailed dissection. The closest three percent of bursts, of which GRB 260310A is one, are the ones that teach us how the engines work.

## What this means for the burst population

The broader significance of GRB 260310A lies in what it implies about the bursts we have already seen and the ones we have yet to find. Gamma-ray bursts are used as probes of the distant universe, as tracers of star formation, and as laboratories for extreme physics. All of those uses depend on knowing how much energy a burst actually released, and that knowledge is only as good as our understanding of the jet's geometry. If off-axis viewing can hide a powerful jet behind a modest flash, then some of the statistical conclusions drawn from burst catalogues may need revisiting.

Consider the distribution of burst energies. Astronomers have long known that the apparent energies of gamma-ray bursts span an enormous range, and they have debated how much of that range reflects real differences between explosions and how much reflects viewing angle. GRB 260310A is a concrete example of the second effect operating at full strength: an explosion whose true power was disguised by geometry, revealed only because it was close enough and bright enough to follow for months. If such cases are common, then the true energy distribution of bursts may be narrower, or differently shaped, than the observed distribution suggests.

There is also a connection to the search for the sources of gravitational waves and other cosmic messengers. The jets launched by collapsing stars and merging neutron stars are among the most promising sites for accelerating cosmic rays, the high-energy particles that constantly rain down on Earth. Understanding how those jets are structured, how wide their cores and wings are, and how their energy is distributed, is essential for working out how much they contribute to the cosmic-ray flux. GRB 260310A, with its unusually well-mapped jet structure, offers a rare test case for those models.

And there is a simple, human lesson in the result. Astronomy is often described as the science of looking, but GRB 260310A is a reminder that looking is not the same as seeing. For the first minute, our telescopes saw a modest flash. For the first hours, they saw a puzzling afterglow. It took months of patient observation, across the electromagnetic spectrum, to realise that the explosion had been powerful all along, and that we had simply been standing in the wrong place. The universe does not arrange its most spectacular events for our convenience. Sometimes the most interesting thing about an explosion is not how bright it is, but the angle from which we happen to watch it.

## Jets across the universe

GRB 260310A is not the only recent result to show how much jets can hide. The <a href="/blog/webb-earliest-supernova-grb-250314a-730-million-years/">earliest supernova linked to a gamma-ray burst, GRB 250314A</a>, observed by the James Webb Space Telescope just 730 million years after the Big Bang, showed that these explosions were already occurring in the universe's infancy, and that their supernova signatures could be detected across almost the whole of cosmic history. Together, the two bursts bracket the story of stellar death: one from the dawn of time, one from our own cosmic neighbourhood, both revealing how much a single explosion can teach us when it is watched carefully.

The physics of jets also connects GRB 260310A to objects on vastly different scales. The <a href="/blog/3c-120-black-hole-jet-2026-xrism-hot-inner-flow/">3C 120 black hole jet studied by XRISM</a> shows how a supermassive black hole can launch a powerful relativistic jet from a hot inner flow, while gamma-ray bursts show the same basic machinery operating in a single dying star. The scales differ by factors of millions, but the underlying processes, accretion, magnetic fields, shocks and particle acceleration, rhyme across them. A jet's wake in a cold cloud, a flare in a distant galaxy's core, and a slow-rising afterglow from a burst two billion light-years away are different chapters of the same story: how gravity and magnetism together turn infalling matter into some of the most energetic phenomena in the universe.

The Swift connection is worth noting too. The <a href="/blog/nasa-swift-observatory-ends-22-years-gamma-ray-bursts/">Swift observatory's 22-year gamma-ray burst legacy</a> includes the archive of more than 1,300 long bursts that the GRB 260310A team searched, and it is that archive that reveals how rarely bursts are followed beyond 15 days. Swift's long service made this study possible in a way that goes beyond any single observation: it provided the statistical context against which GRB 260310A's brightness could be judged, and it showed how unusual the long monitoring campaign really was.

## The case for longer watches

If there is one practical recommendation to take from the GRB 260310A study, it is that astronomers should watch bursts for longer. The late X-ray flare that revealed the jet's core appeared between days 18 and 30, well after most follow-up campaigns would have ended. The radio afterglow remained measurable for months. The optical afterglow's slow rise took hours to become apparent. At every stage, the interesting behaviour arrived later than expected, and a shorter campaign would have missed it.

This is not an argument for watching every burst for months. Telescope time is scarce, and most bursts fade below detectability within days. But it is an argument for a tiered approach: intensive early monitoring for all bursts, plus extended monitoring for the subset that shows unusual behaviour, such as a slow-rising afterglow or a nearby distance that keeps the source bright. GRB 260310A showed both of those warning signs early, and the telescopes that stayed with it were rewarded with one of the most informative burst datasets of the past two decades.

It is also an argument for the value of survey instruments that monitor the sky without needing a trigger. A burst whose jet points away from Earth may produce a gamma-ray flash too weak to trigger a dedicated follow-up campaign, yet still produce a bright afterglow that a wide-field survey could catch. The more such surveys operate, in optical, X-ray and radio wavelengths, the more off-axis bursts will be found, and the better astronomers will understand how common they are.

The authors themselves frame the result as a beginning rather than an ending. Their structured-jet model explains the observations well, but it is a model, not a photograph, and it will benefit from comparison with other bursts. Each new off-axis event will test whether the two-component structure, a narrow core wrapped in a broader, slower outflow, is a general feature of gamma-ray burst jets or a peculiarity of GRB 260310A. If it proves general, it will reshape how burst energies are estimated and how the burst population is understood.

## A monster, misjudged

Strip away the technical language, and GRB 260310A is a story about misjudgement. A dying star, two billion light-years away, collapsed and fired a jet of matter at nearly the speed of light into the surrounding gas. The jet was structured, with a ferociously energetic core wrapped in a broader, gentler outflow. Earth happened to sit outside the core's narrow beam, so the first flash we saw was a pale shadow of the explosion's true power. Only as the jet slowed and its light spread did the real scale of the event emerge: an afterglow that outshone its host galaxy, ranked among the brightest in two decades, and kept shining for months.

That is a humbling result, and also an exciting one. It means the sky may be full of powerful explosions we have been underestimating, waiting for telescopes patient enough to watch them for weeks instead of days, and for surveys wide enough to catch the ones whose gamma-ray flashes never triggered an alert. GRB 260310A was not a dud that turned out to be interesting. It was a monster that we happened to see from the side, and it took a global fleet of telescopes, four months of observation, and a careful piece of modelling to recognise it for what it was.

The next time a gamma-ray burst looks modest, astronomers will have a new question to ask before they move on: modest from where? The answer, as GRB 260310A shows, can change everything.
