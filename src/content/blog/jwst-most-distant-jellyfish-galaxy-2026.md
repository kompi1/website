Eight and a half billion years ago, when the universe was barely more than a third of its present age, a galaxy was already being torn into the shape of a jellyfish. It was plunging through a crowded neighbourhood of galaxies, and the hot, thin gas filling that neighbourhood was acting like a hurricane wind, peeling gas from the galaxy's disc and streaming it behind it in long, glowing tentacles. Inside those tentacles, new stars were already being born, far from the galaxy they had come from.

That galaxy has now been found. Astrophysicists at the University of Waterloo, sifting through deep-sky data from the James Webb Space Telescope, identified it in the COSMOS field, a patch of sky that telescopes have stared at for decades precisely because it offers such a clean view of the distant universe. The object, catalogued as COSMOS2020-635829, is the most distant jellyfish galaxy ever captured, seen at a redshift of 1.156 — the astronomers' way of saying that its light has been stretched by the expansion of the universe on a journey lasting 8.5 billion years. The discovery was reported on 17 February 2026, in a paper titled "JWST Reveals a Candidate Jellyfish Galaxy at z = 1.156," published in The Astrophysical Journal.

It matters for a reason that goes beyond the pleasing image of a cosmic jellyfish. Until this find, astronomers had generally assumed that the violent environment needed to strip a galaxy in this way — a mature, dense galaxy cluster, swimming in hot gas — was still assembling at that early epoch, and that ram-pressure stripping, the process that makes jellyfish galaxies, would have been uncommon. COSMOS2020-635829 suggests otherwise. It suggests that cluster environments were already harsh enough, early enough, to rip gas from a passing galaxy and remake it, and that the transformation of galaxies inside clusters may have begun far sooner than textbooks have assumed.

"We were looking through a large amount of data from this well-studied region in the sky with the hopes of spotting jellyfish galaxies that haven't been studied before," said Dr. Ian Roberts, a Banting Postdoctoral Fellow at the Waterloo Centre for Astrophysics, who led the work. "Early on in our search of the JWST data, we spotted a distant, undocumented jellyfish galaxy that sparked immediate interest."

That sentence, modest as it sounds, captures something important about how modern astronomy works. Nobody pointed Webb at this galaxy because it was known to be special. The team was mining an archive — combing through observations of a famous field, looking for shapes that had not been catalogued — and the jellyfish swam out of the data.

## A galaxy caught mid-plunge

To understand what the Waterloo team found, it helps to picture what a jellyfish galaxy actually is. It is not a separate species of galaxy, and it is not born with tentacles. It is an ordinary disc galaxy caught in an extraordinary situation: falling at high speed into a galaxy cluster, a gravitationally bound swarm of hundreds or even thousands of galaxies, all embedded in a cloud of gas heated to millions of degrees.

That intracluster gas is thin by earthly standards — far thinner than the best laboratory vacuum — but at the speeds involved, hundreds of kilometres per second, it exerts real pressure. Astronomers call it ram pressure, the same kind of pressure you feel as wind on your face when you stick your hand out of a moving car window, scaled up to cosmic proportions. The pressure cannot easily move the galaxy's stars, which are dense and widely spaced, but it can push on the galaxy's gas, which is spread out and comparatively fragile. The gas is blown backwards, out of the disc, into tails that can stretch for tens of thousands of light-years.

Those tails are the jellyfish's tentacles. And they are not dead debris. Compressed and turbulent, the stripped gas can collapse and ignite into new stars, so the tentacles glow with bright, blue knots of young starlight. That is exactly what the Webb images of COSMOS2020-635829 show: a normal-looking galaxy disc, and behind it, extraplanar sources in its tail — four of them marked in the team's figures — that shine with the blue light of stars far younger than the galaxy itself.

The age of those stars is a clue in itself. If the knots were old stars pulled out of the disc, they would look redder and older. Their youth suggests they formed where they now sit, outside the main galaxy, in gas that had already been stripped. In other words, this galaxy is not merely losing material. It is giving birth in its wake, leaving a trail of stellar nurseries behind it as it falls.

The COSMOS field made this detection possible in a particular way. The Cosmic Evolution Survey field is a region of sky deliberately chosen because it lies far from the plane of our own Milky Way, so there is little contamination from foreground stars and dust, and because it is visible from both hemispheres and free of bright foreground objects that would dazzle a sensitive telescope. Generations of telescopes have mapped it, which means that when Webb looked, the team could compare its images against a deep library of earlier observations and be confident that what they were seeing was genuinely new, not an artefact or a familiar object seen in a new filter.

Webb's own contribution was its infrared vision. At a redshift of 1.156, the light that left this galaxy as visible and ultraviolet radiation has been stretched into the infrared by the time it reaches us. Webb's filters — the team used combinations including F115W, F150W, F277W and F444W to build their colour images — are designed precisely for this regime, letting astronomers see structure in galaxies whose light would be invisible, or nearly so, to a purely optical telescope. It is one more example of the observatory's power to dissect the early universe rather than merely detect it, a theme that has run through <a href="/blog/webb-telescope-2026-discoveries/">Webb's Breakthrough Year: Baby Galaxies, Impossible Planets, and the Clearest Alien World Ever Seen</a>.

## Why an early jellyfish changes the story

The surprise in COSMOS2020-635829 is not that jellyfish galaxies exist. Astronomers have catalogued just over 400 candidate jellyfish galaxies in the nearby universe, and their physics is well established. The surprise is the date.

For years, the working picture went something like this. In the early universe, galaxies were still gathering into clusters. The clusters were looser, their hot gas less dense, their gravitational wells shallower. A galaxy falling into such a young structure would feel a gentler wind, and dramatic stripping would be rare. Only later, as clusters matured and their intracluster medium thickened, would ram-pressure stripping become a common way to transform galaxies — starving them of the gas they need to make new stars and gradually turning blue, star-forming spirals into red, quiescent ellipticals and lenticulars.

COSMOS2020-635829 does not fit that tidy timeline. Here is a galaxy, 8.5 billion years in the past, already showing the full jellyfish anatomy: a disc, a stripped tail, and young stars forming in that tail. For that to happen, its environment must already have been dense and hot enough to exert strong ram pressure. Roberts and his team drew three lessons from the find, and each one pushes against the older picture.

"The first is that cluster environments were already harsh enough to strip galaxies, and the second is that galaxy clusters may strongly alter galaxy properties earlier than expected," Roberts said. "Another is that all the challenges listed might have played a part in building the large population of dead galaxies we see in galaxy clusters today."

That third point is worth pausing on. Look at a mature galaxy cluster today and you will find it disproportionately full of "dead" galaxies — systems that long ago stopped forming stars and now shine with the steady, reddish light of old stellar populations. Astronomers have long debated how those galaxies died: whether they used up their gas, had it stripped away, had it heated by their central black holes, or suffered some combination of all three. If stripping was already operating 8.5 billion years ago, then it had far longer to do its work, and it may account for a larger share of those dead galaxies than previously credited. The early universe, in this telling, was not a gentle nursery. It was already a rough neighbourhood.

There is, of course, a caveat, and the paper's own title carries it: this is a candidate jellyfish galaxy. Classification at such distances is hard. The tail could, in principle, be a tidal feature — debris pulled out by gravity during a close encounter with another galaxy, rather than gas pushed out by wind. The two mechanisms can produce similar-looking streams, and distinguishing them requires detailed spectroscopy: measurements of the gas's motion, temperature and chemical composition that can tell a wind-driven tail from a gravity-driven one. The Waterloo team has requested additional time on Webb to dig deeper into this galaxy's mysteries, and those follow-up observations will be the test. A candidate is not yet a certainty, and honest astronomy keeps that distinction in view.

But even as a candidate, the object is valuable. Candidates are how a field moves forward: they mark the places where the old picture strains, and they tell telescope-time committees exactly where to point next. If follow-up confirms the stripping interpretation, the timeline for cluster transformation moves earlier. If it does not, astronomers will still have learned something about how easily tidal tails can mimic jellyfish at high redshift — itself a useful lesson for the next survey.

## The wind that strips — and sometimes feeds

Ram-pressure stripping sounds purely destructive: a galaxy loses its gas, star formation sputters, the galaxy fades. That is often the end of the story. But one of the most intriguing twists in jellyfish research is that the same wind can, for a time, do the opposite at the galaxy's centre. It can feed the monster lurking there.

Almost every large galaxy hosts a supermassive black hole at its centre, millions to billions of times the mass of the Sun. Yet only a small fraction of those black holes are active — only a few are currently swallowing gas fast enough to shine brilliantly as active galactic nuclei, or AGN. For decades, astronomers have asked why. If every galaxy has a black hole, and most galaxies have gas, why do so few black holes light up?

A landmark study from the GASP programme — GAs Stripping Phenomena in galaxies with MUSE — offered an unexpected part of the answer. Using the MUSE spectrograph on the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope in Chile, an Italian-led team led by Bianca Poggianti of the INAF Astronomical Observatory of Padova studied extreme jellyfish galaxies in nearby clusters, including JO204 and JO194, whose tendrils of gas and newborn stars stretch far beyond their discs. They found that six of the seven jellyfish galaxies in their sample hosted an actively feeding supermassive black hole at the centre. Among galaxies in general, the active fraction is less than one in ten.

"This strong link between ram pressure stripping and active black holes was not predicted and has never been reported before," Poggianti said at the time. "It seems that the central black hole is being fed because some of the gas, rather than being removed, reaches the galaxy centre."

The mechanism, as the team described it, is a kind of cosmic irony. The same pressure that blows most of a galaxy's gas outwards also compresses and disturbs the gas that remains, and some of that disturbed gas loses angular momentum — the rotational motion that normally keeps it orbiting safely far from the centre — and spirals inward. The black hole, previously starved and dormant, suddenly finds itself at the centre of a feast. It brightens into an AGN, and its own outpouring of energy may then help to heat or expel the galaxy's remaining gas, accelerating the very quenching that stripping began. Stripping, feeding and feedback become three acts of a single drama.

That picture has since been tested in computer universes as well as real ones. A 2025 study using the IllustrisTNG cosmological simulations, published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, modelled jellyfish galaxies with stellar masses between about ten to the power of 9.5 and 10.8 solar masses and found that they were more likely to host luminous AGN than central galaxies of the same mass, and more likely than other satellite galaxies too — largely because many ordinary satellites have already lost their gas and have little left to feed a black hole. Compared with non-jellyfish satellites holding similar amounts of gas, the simulated jellyfish experienced stronger ram pressure, piled gas more densely towards their centres, and had lower central gas sound speeds, all of which favour accretion. The simulations, in other words, reproduced the observational hint and supplied a physical reason for it: compression.

Not every sample agrees perfectly, and that disagreement is itself informative. Other researchers studying roughly two dozen jellyfish galaxies have found active black holes in only a minority of them, a reminder that the original GASP sample deliberately targeted the most extreme examples — the galaxies with the longest, most spectacular tentacles — and that extreme examples may not represent the whole population. Selection effects matter. A galaxy chosen because its tentacles are dramatic may also be a galaxy in the most violent phase of its fall, when central feeding is at its peak. Quieter jellyfish, mid-plunge or late in the process, may show no AGN at all. The honest conclusion is not that every jellyfish feeds its black hole, but that stripping can feed black holes, sometimes dramatically, and that the frequency of that feeding is still being measured. The GASP programme was designed to obtain deep MUSE data for 114 galaxies in a range of environments, precisely to turn a striking result in seven galaxies into a statistical statement about hundreds.

That context makes COSMOS2020-635829 doubly interesting. If stripping was already common 8.5 billion years ago, then black-hole feeding by stripping may also have been operating that early, contributing to the growth of supermassive black holes and to the feedback that shaped young clusters. The new galaxy's own central black hole has not yet been characterised in the detail that the GASP galaxies were — that will require the follow-up spectroscopy the team is seeking — but the question is now squarely on the table. Did this ancient jellyfish also light up its centre as it fell? The answer will help decide whether AGN feeding by ram pressure is a late-universe curiosity or a long-running engine of galaxy evolution.

The broader black-hole story has been moving quickly in parallel. Recent work on active galaxies has shown magnetic eruptions around supermassive black holes behaving with surprising familiarity, as described in <a href="/blog/black-hole-solar-storm-ngc-3783-neupert-effect/">A Solar Storm Erupted Around a Monster Black Hole — and Left the Sun's Fingerprint</a>, where X-ray telescopes caught a flare in NGC 3783 following the same pattern as a solar storm. The two stories are different in detail — one is about a single flare, the other about a galaxy-wide wind — but they share a theme: the physics that governs black holes is not as exotic, or as isolated, as it once seemed. It is connected to the gas around them, to magnetic fields, to the weather of the galaxy itself.


## Star birth in the wake

One of the most visually striking aspects of any jellyfish galaxy is also one of the most scientifically puzzling: stars forming outside a galaxy. Textbook star formation happens inside galaxies, in cold, dense molecular clouds shielded from harsh radiation. The tail of a jellyfish galaxy seems like a terrible nursery — exposed to the hot intracluster medium, turbulent, low in density compared with a galactic disc. Yet the blue knots are there, and their colours and brightnesses leave little doubt that they are young.

The explanation lies in compression and cooling. As stripped gas is pushed outwards, parts of it are squeezed — by the ram pressure itself, by turbulence, and by the pressure of the surrounding hot medium — until pockets of it become dense enough to collapse under their own gravity. Once collapse begins, the same physics takes over as anywhere else: a cloud fragments, protostars ignite, and a cluster of hot, blue, massive stars burns briefly and brilliantly before dying young. Because massive blue stars live only millions of years, their presence is a timestamp. The knots in COSMOS2020-635829's tail cannot be ancient stars that drifted out long ago. They must have formed recently, in the tail itself, during the galaxy's fall.

This has a curious consequence for how we count a galaxy's stars. A jellyfish galaxy's stellar mass is not confined to its disc. Some of its newest stars will never return to the galaxy at all. Stripped gas that forms stars in the tail may leave those stars behind as a faint, diffuse trail — or they may eventually fall into the cluster itself, contributing to the intracluster light, the ghostly glow of orphan stars that drifts between the galaxies of a mature cluster. In that sense, a jellyfish galaxy is not just transforming itself. It is seeding its environment, donating stars to the cluster as it passes through.

The process also complicates the simple story of quenching. Stripping removes gas from the disc, which should suppress star formation there. But it triggers star formation in the tail, which partly compensates, at least temporarily. The galaxy's total star-formation rate may stay roughly steady, or even spike, even as its disc is being emptied. Astronomers call this a starburst phase, and it is one reason why jellyfish galaxies are so eye-catching: they are galaxies in transition, caught between a star-forming past and a quiescent future, and the transition itself is luminous.

In the nearby universe, the GASP galaxies showed this pattern in detail, with star-forming knots strung along their tails like lights on a wire. In COSMOS2020-635829, Webb sees the same pattern at a far greater distance, in a younger universe. The physics, it seems, has not changed. What has changed is our ability to see it, and our understanding of when it began.

It is worth remembering, too, how much of astronomy's progress comes from looking again at familiar fields with better eyes. The COSMOS field has been observed by Hubble, by Spitzer, by ground-based telescopes, and now by Webb. Each generation of instruments has found things its predecessors missed — not because the earlier astronomers were careless, but because each new telescope opens a new window: deeper, sharper, or in a new wavelength. The jellyfish found by the Waterloo team was hiding in plain sight, in one of the most studied patches of sky in existence, waiting for an instrument sensitive enough in the infrared, and a team patient enough to search for an uncatalogued shape.

## Clusters as engines of change

Galaxy clusters are the largest gravitationally bound structures in the universe, and they are also among the most effective at remaking the galaxies inside them. A galaxy that lives alone in the field can evolve slowly, forming stars at its own pace, merging occasionally, ageing gracefully. A galaxy that falls into a cluster faces a gauntlet: the hot intracluster medium strips its gas, the cluster's gravity tides distort its shape, close encounters with other galaxies — sometimes called harassment — stir its stars, and mergers can rebuild it entirely. By the time it settles, it may be unrecognisable.

The recent history of cluster science has been a story of decoding these mechanisms one by one, and of discovering that they often act together. Radio observations have revealed vast, ghostly structures in merging clusters — revived remnants of old black-hole outbursts, stretched and re-energised by shock waves rippling through the intracluster gas — as explored in <a href="/blog/treble-clef-radio-phoenix-chandra-galaxy-clusters/">The Treble Clef in the Deep Sky: Chandra and LOFAR Decode a Black Hole's Echo Behind the Milky Way</a>. X-ray telescopes map the hot gas itself, showing where it is densest, where it has been disturbed by a merger, and where a falling galaxy is most likely to feel the wind. Optical and infrared telescopes, Webb foremost among them, trace the stars and the cold gas, showing what the wind actually does.

COSMOS2020-635829 adds a time dimension to that picture. If clusters were already capable of stripping galaxies 8.5 billion years ago, then the cluster environment has been an engine of galaxy transformation for most of cosmic history, not merely for the last few billion years. That pushes back the era in which astronomers must look for the origins of today's cluster populations. The red, dead galaxies that dominate mature clusters did not necessarily die late. Some of them may have begun dying — losing gas, lighting up their black holes, forming their last generations of stars in their tails — when the universe was still young.

It also raises a question about the clusters themselves. A cluster capable of strong stripping needs a substantial reservoir of hot gas, which in turn requires a deep gravitational well and time for gas to fall in and heat up. Finding such an environment at redshift 1.156 suggests that at least some clusters, or protoclusters on their way to becoming clusters, assembled their hot atmospheres earlier than some models predicted. That is a constraint that simulators will want to reproduce. Cosmological simulations like IllustrisTNG already produce jellyfish galaxies and AGN feeding by ram pressure at the present day; the new challenge is to produce them at the right rate, at the right epoch, in the early universe, and to check whether the simulated clusters of that era are as harsh as the real one that stripped COSMOS2020-635829 appears to have been.

There is a broader lesson here about how astronomy revises itself. The old picture — clusters forming late, stripping starting late — was not foolish. It was based on the best data available, which was dominated by nearby clusters, where stripping is obvious, and by distant clusters that were hard to observe in detail. As Webb and other instruments push detailed galaxy studies to higher redshifts, the boundary of "early" keeps moving. Phenomena once thought to belong to the mature universe keep turning up in the young one: massive galaxies earlier than expected, black holes larger than expected, clusters harsher than expected. Each such find does not overthrow the standard model of cosmology, but it tightens the screws on the models of galaxy formation inside it, forcing them to assemble structure faster, or more efficiently, than they did before.

## What comes next for this jellyfish

The immediate future for COSMOS2020-635829 is more telescope time. The Waterloo team has requested additional Webb observations, and the wish list for such a target is easy to imagine. Spectroscopy would measure the velocities of gas in the disc and the tail, confirming whether the tail is truly moving away from the galaxy as a wind-driven stream should, and whether its motion matches the direction of the galaxy's plunge. It would measure the chemical composition of the tail gas, which should resemble the galaxy's own interstellar medium if it was stripped, rather than pristine intracluster gas. It would search for emission lines from an active nucleus at the centre, testing whether this ancient jellyfish, like six of the seven GASP galaxies, is also feeding its black hole. And it would date the young stars in the tail more precisely, pinning down when stripping began.

Other telescopes will have their own roles. Radio observatories can trace cold gas and search for the faint synchrotron glow of charged particles in the tail. X-ray telescopes can map the hot gas around the galaxy, showing the pressure it faces. Ground-based spectrographs like MUSE, which made the GASP discoveries possible, can dissect nearby analogues in exquisite detail, building the statistical samples that a single distant object cannot provide. The distant jellyfish points the way; the nearby ones fill in the physics.

There is also the survey dimension. Webb is not the only instrument hunting for jellyfish at high redshift, and COSMOS2020-635829 is unlikely to remain the record holder for long. As deep surveys accumulate, and as machine-learning searches comb through them for the tell-tale combination of a disc plus a one-sided tail, more early jellyfish should emerge. Each one will add a data point to the question that this discovery raises: was COSMOS2020-635829 an unusually precocious cluster, an outlier, or was it typical of its era? A single object can challenge a picture. Only a population can replace it.

For now, though, the single object is enough to admire. A galaxy, falling through hot gas 8.5 billion years ago, trailing tentacles of newborn stars behind it, was caught by a telescope that did not exist when its light set out, in a field of sky that humans have mapped for a generation, by a team that was simply looking for shapes nobody had catalogued yet. It is a reminder that the universe still keeps its most interesting creatures in the places we thought we had already explored — and that sometimes, to find them, you only have to look again, a little deeper, in a little more light.

## Why it matters beyond one galaxy

It is fair to ask, at the end of a story about a single faint smudge 8.5 billion light-years away, why it should matter to anyone who is not an astronomer. Part of the answer is the usual one for basic science: understanding how galaxies live and die is understanding how the universe built the structures we inhabit, including the Milky Way, which has itself felt the wind of its environment in the past and will feel it again in the future as it falls towards Andromeda and the Virgo cluster's outskirts on cosmic timescales.

But there is a more immediate answer, too. Jellyfish galaxies are laboratories for physics that cannot be reproduced on Earth: gas dynamics at millions of degrees, star formation in turbulent, low-density environments, black-hole feeding driven by a galaxy-wide wind. The rules worked out in those laboratories — how pressure strips gas, how compression triggers collapse, how feedback regulates growth — are the same rules that go into every simulation of galaxy formation, and those simulations are how astronomers interpret almost everything else they see, from the first galaxies to the large-scale structure of the cosmos. Get jellyfish wrong, and you get a piece of all of it wrong.

And there is the simplest answer of all. Eight and a half billion years ago, a galaxy fell into a crowd, and the crowd tore it into the shape of a jellyfish, and in its wake, new stars lit up like sparks from a firework. We can now see that. A species that evolved on a small rocky planet, looking up with mirrors and detectors chilled to near absolute zero, can reconstruct a drama that played out before its own Sun existed. That is not a small thing. It is, in its quiet way, extraordinary — and it is why astronomers keep looking, field by field, galaxy by galaxy, for the next jellyfish swimming out of the dark.
