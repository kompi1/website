Some eleven million light-years from Earth, in a small and ragged galaxy that has barely any of the heavy elements we take for granted, a black hole is doing something extraordinary. It is not merely swallowing gas. It is lighting up an entire cloud of gas around it, stripping electrons from atoms so thoroughly that the glow carries the fingerprints of ions that should not exist in such a quiet place. For more than two decades astronomers have suspected that this object, known as Holmberg II X-1, was responsible for the strange glow. Now the James Webb Space Telescope has caught it in the act.

The evidence arrived in the form of a paper published on November 4, 2026, in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*, led by F. A. Fogantini and an international team of collaborators. Using Webb's infrared spectrographs, the researchers mapped the nebula around Holmberg II X-1 in finer detail than ever before, resolving its structure on scales of just 15 to 25 parsecs — roughly 50 to 80 light-years — and detecting, for the first time in this source, the spectral lines of neon ions that require some of the most energetic radiation in the universe to create. The result is the clearest direct link yet between an individual ultraluminous X-ray source and the highly ionized nebula that surrounds it, and it turns a small, metal-poor dwarf galaxy into a laboratory for understanding how black holes shaped the first galaxies.

It is a result that reaches far beyond one obscure object. The early universe was filled with galaxies much like Holmberg II: small, poor in metals, and lit by fierce radiation. If a single accreting black hole can ionize gas across a hundred parsecs in such a galaxy, then black holes like this one may have played a much larger role in the cosmic dawn than astronomers once assumed.

## A galaxy with almost nothing in it

Holmberg II is not a galaxy that makes headlines. It is a dwarf irregular galaxy about 3.4 megaparsecs away — roughly 11 million light-years — in the constellation Ursa Major, part of the nearby M81 group of galaxies. Its stars contain only about a tenth of the heavy elements found in the Sun, a metallicity astronomers write as roughly 0.1 times solar. It forms new stars at a leisurely pace of about 0.03 solar masses per year, a trickle compared with the Milky Way's one or two solar masses per year.

That poverty is precisely what makes it valuable. In the early universe, the first generations of stars formed from gas that contained almost no elements heavier than hydrogen and helium. Galaxies like Holmberg II are the closest surviving analogues of those primordial systems, and the physics that operates inside them — how radiation travels, how gas cools, how black holes feed — is the same physics that governed the universe's first billion years. When astronomers want to understand the cosmic dawn, they often look not at the most distant objects, but at the nearest poor ones.

Inside Holmberg II sits the object of this study. Holmberg II X-1, often shortened to Ho II X-1, lies at the heel of a sprawling, characteristically shaped region of glowing hydrogen gas known as the Foot Nebula. Around the X-ray source itself sits a more compact region, about 100 parsecs across, that glows in the light of doubly ionized helium — a He III region discovered more than two decades ago by astronomers Manfred Pakull and Laurent Mirioni. That glow was the first clue that something extremely energetic was at work.

## What is an ultraluminous X-ray source?

Ho II X-1 belongs to a class of objects called ultraluminous X-ray sources, or ULXs. These are X-ray binaries — a compact object, usually a black hole or neutron star, pulling gas from a companion star — whose X-ray luminosity exceeds 10^39 erg per second. That number matters because it is above the Eddington limit for a black hole of ten solar masses: the theoretical ceiling at which the outward push of radiation should balance the inward pull of gravity and choke off further feeding.

ULXs break that ceiling, and for years astronomers argued about how. One possibility is that they are powered by black holes more massive than ten suns, accreting at or near the Eddington limit. Another is that they are ordinary stellar-mass black holes feeding far above the limit, in the strongly super-Eddington regime, driving powerful winds and outflows from their accretion discs. A third possibility, confirmed for a handful of ULXs, is that the compact object is a neutron star, its magnetic field channelling matter onto its poles so that the radiation escapes in beams rather than in all directions.

Ho II X-1 is one of the brightest and best-studied ULXs in the nearby universe. In X-rays it is a persistent source, shining with a luminosity between about 10^39 and 3 × 10^40 erg per second in the 0.3 to 10 keV band, and varying substantially in brightness and spectral hardness on many timescales. No coherent pulsations have ever been detected from it, which argues against a strongly magnetized neutron star, though it does not rule one out entirely. The best current estimates put the mass of its black hole — if it is a black hole — between about 16 and 70 solar masses.

Crucially, Ho II X-1 is one of the few ULXs with a securely identified donor star. Its companion is a B-type star, hot and blue, but too cool to contribute significantly to ionizing the surrounding nebula. That makes the system a clean laboratory: whatever is ionizing the gas around it must be the accreting compact object itself, not its companion.

## The mystery of the glowing nebula

The puzzle that has drawn astronomers to Ho II X-1 for more than twenty years is the ionization state of its nebula. Webb's recent observations of other low-metallicity galaxies have revealed fine-structure lines from ions with ionization potentials above 50 electron volts, such as four-times-ionized neon, [Ne V], and three-times-ionized oxygen, [O IV]. Creating those ions requires photons with energies far beyond what ordinary hot stars produce. Something in these galaxies is generating extremely hard radiation, and ULXs have long been considered prime suspects.

The problem was proof. ULXs usually sit in crowded fields in distant galaxies, and connecting a particular ionized nebula to a particular X-ray source has been difficult. Earlier observations with the Hubble Space Telescope showed that the shapes of the helium, hydrogen, and oxygen emission regions around Ho II X-1 were consistent with photoionization by the ULX, and optical and ultraviolet spectra were dominated by narrow, high-excitation lines with no sign of the broadening that fast winds would produce. The Spitzer Space Telescope had even detected the [O IV] line at 25.9 microns — but Spitzer could not resolve the nebula spatially, so the link remained circumstantial.

What was needed was a telescope that could map the nebula line by line, pixel by pixel, in the infrared, where these high-ionization lines shine brightest. That is exactly what Webb was built to do.

## Webb's infrared eyes open on the Foot Nebula

The new observations were taken as part of Webb program 5846, led by principal investigator Lidia Oskinova. The team used two of Webb's spectrographs in integral field unit mode, a technique that records a spectrum at every point in the field of view, building a three-dimensional map of the gas — two dimensions of sky position plus one of wavelength.

The Mid-Infrared Instrument's Medium Resolution Spectrometer, MIRI MRS, observed Ho II X-1 on January 11, 2025, covering wavelengths from 4.9 to 27.9 microns with a total on-source exposure of nearly 14,716 seconds per sub-band and channel. The Near-Infrared Spectrograph's integral field unit, NIRSpec IFU, followed on February 10, 2025, covering 2.87 to 5.10 microns with a total exposure of 11,789 seconds. Together, the two instruments produced the first spatially resolved infrared spectroscopy of a ULX nebula ever obtained.

The data were processed through all three stages of the Webb calibration pipeline, version 1.19.1, with careful attention to the instrument's quirks — stray-light contamination in the MIRI channels, residual fringing in the spectra, and the cosmic-ray "snowballs" that pepper NIRSpec exposures. The result is a set of data cubes in which every pixel, or spaxel, carries its own infrared spectrum.

What the team found in those spectra was striking. The nebula's spectrum is crowded with emission lines, including hydrogen recombination lines and numerous fine-structure lines from ions spanning ionization potentials from 14 to 126 electron volts. And for the first time in this source, the spectra contain [Ne V] and [Ne VI] — neon stripped of four and five of its electrons, ions that require photons of roughly 97 and 126 electron volts to create. Those are energies far beyond the reach of any normal star.

## The neon fingerprint points along a jet

The most telling discovery was not merely that these extreme ions exist, but where they live. The team measured spectra at three positions: the ULX itself and two compact radio knots, one to the northeast and one to the southwest, that had been resolved by earlier radio observations. The three knots are aligned along a northeast-southwest axis, with the central radio source sitting exactly at the X-ray position of the ULX. The northeast and southwest knots lie about 16.5 and 24 parsecs from the center.

The emission from the highest-ionization species — the [Ne V] and [Ne VI] lines — comes from compact regions offset from the ULX toward those radio knots. Lower-ionization species, by contrast, trace the wider nebula. The pattern is anisotropic: the hardest radiation is not escaping equally in all directions. Instead, it appears to leak preferentially along the same axis defined by the radio knots.

That alignment is the paper's central argument. The radio knots are thought to be intermittent, optically thin ejecta — blobs of material thrown off in past or recurring jet-related outbursts. Their steep radio spectra and the fading of the central source argue against a steady, confined jet; instead, the knots look like the fading remnants of episodic ejections. The fact that the most extreme ionization lines up with this axis suggests that the ionizing radiation escapes preferentially along the jet direction, and that the black hole's jet activity and the nebula's ionization are physically connected.

There is a second, equally important clue. The spectral lines across the nebula are narrow — consistent with the instrumental resolution — which means the gas is kinematically quiet. If shock waves from a jet or a wind were doing the ionizing, the gas would be churned up and the lines would be broadened by the motion. Their narrowness disfavours shocks as the dominant ionization mechanism. The gas is being lit up by photons, not smashed by shock waves.

Together, the two findings paint a coherent picture: an accreting black hole whose hardest radiation escapes along a preferred axis, photoionizing the gas around it out to scales of about 100 parsecs, while the gas itself remains calm. It is, the authors write, direct evidence linking an individual ULX to a highly ionized nebula in a low-metallicity galaxy.

## Why a quiet nebula matters so much

The distinction between photoionization and shock ionization may sound technical, but it is the key to the whole result. ULX nebulae come in many varieties. Some display shock-excited expanding shells with velocities of several hundred kilometres per second, the scars of powerful jets and winds ploughing into the surrounding gas. Others are quiescent, their spectra dominated by narrow lines that betray photoionization. Telling the two apart is notoriously difficult, because photoionization is always present to some degree, and shocks can hide inside photoionized gas.

Spatially resolved spectroscopy is what breaks the deadlock. By mapping where each ion lives and how fast the gas is moving at each point, astronomers can connect local ionization conditions with the kinematics of the gas — and in Ho II X-1, the connection points squarely at photons from the black hole, escaping anisotropically along the jet axis.

This matters for a long-standing debate about the early universe. In the first billion years after the Big Bang, something had to produce enough high-energy photons to strip electrons from helium and heavier elements in young galaxies, and to help reionize the intergalactic medium. Hot massive stars are the usual suspects, but they struggle to produce enough photons above 54 electron volts, the energy needed to doubly ionize helium. ULXs powered by black holes have been proposed as a supplementary source, capable of supplying the missing hard photons. But the contribution of ULXs to the helium-ionizing photon budget of metal-poor galaxies has remained debated, precisely because the direct evidence was missing. Holmberg II X-1 now provides it.

The result also sharpens the picture of how ULXs feed back into their surroundings. A black hole that can ionize gas across a hundred parsecs is not a passive object. It is reshaping the interstellar medium of its host galaxy, heating gas, changing its chemistry, and potentially regulating where future stars can form. In a dwarf galaxy with only a trickle of star formation, a single object like Ho II X-1 can be a significant source of energy.

## The black hole at the centre of it all

What kind of black hole is doing this? The honest answer is that astronomers still do not know exactly. The mass estimates of 16 to 70 solar masses place it above the typical range for black holes formed from single collapsing stars in the Milky Way, but not so far above that exotic explanations are required. In a low-metallicity environment, massive stars lose less mass to stellar winds during their lives, and can collapse into heavier black holes. Ho II X-1 may simply be the product of that physics: a black hole born heavy because its parent star kept most of its mass.

Whether it is feeding above the Eddington limit or near it is also unresolved. Most ULXs appear to be accreting in the strongly super-Eddington regime, driving strong and ultrafast outflows from their discs. But a second population could be powered by black holes heavier than ten solar masses accreting at the Eddington level, and such systems are expected to be particularly common in low-metallicity environments. The narrow, quiet lines in the Ho II X-1 nebula do not settle the question — a predominantly photoionized nebula does not imply the absence of mechanical feedback, as the authors are careful to note. Jets or disc winds may still be at work, even if photons are doing most of the ionizing.

What the Webb data do establish is the geometry of the system: a black hole, a donor star too cool to matter for ionization, radio knots marking episodic jet activity along a northeast-southwest axis, and hard radiation escaping preferentially along that same axis to light up the nebula. It is a remarkably complete picture for an object eleven million light-years away.

## A growing Webb census of black hole extremes

Holmberg II X-1 is not the only black hole Webb has been illuminating lately. The telescope's infrared vision has been rewriting the story of black holes across the universe, from the runaway black hole <a href="/blog/rbh-1-runaway-black-hole-webb-954-kms-november-2026/">RBH-1, spotted racing through space at 954 kilometres per second</a>, to the dense young star clusters in the <a href="/blog/jwst-firefly-sparkle-black-hole-seeds-2026/">Firefly Sparkle and Cosmic Gems galaxies that may have forged the seeds of the first supermassive black holes</a>. Each of these results uses Webb's ability to see through dust and to dissect light into its component wavelengths, revealing physics that was invisible to earlier telescopes.

The Ho II X-1 result belongs in that company, but it is different in an important way. Where other Webb black hole stories reach across billions of years to the early universe, this one looks at a nearby, accessible system in detail — resolving structures just tens of parsecs across and mapping the ionization state of the gas point by point. It is the difference between a census and a case study, and the case study is what turns a suspicion into a mechanism.

The comparison with other black hole systems also sharpens the questions. The <a href="/blog/black-hole-solar-storm-ngc-3783-neupert-effect/">supermassive black hole in NGC 3783, for example, drives flares and particle acceleration on vastly larger scales</a>, but the underlying physics — accretion, jets, radiation, and the response of the surrounding gas — rhymes across a range of masses spanning millions. ULXs like Ho II X-1 sit in the middle of that range, and they may be the missing link between stellar-mass black holes and their supermassive cousins.

## What comes next for Ho II X-1

The paper is careful about its own limitations, and those limitations point to the next steps. No dedicated background exposures were taken for either instrument, and the nebula fills the NIRSpec field of view entirely, so the measured fluxes are source-plus-background quantities. Absolute line fluxes and ratios should be interpreted with caution, especially for diffuse, low-surface-brightness emission — though the relative morphology and kinematics of the compact high-ionization lines, which carry the paper's main conclusions, are much less affected.

The authors also note that their spectral energy distribution for the ULX remains uncertain in the extreme ultraviolet, the band that governs the production of ions above 54 electron volts. Pinning down that part of the spectrum will require combining the Webb data with ultraviolet and X-ray observations, and with models of super-Eddington accretion flows.

Longer term, the obvious next step is to look at more ULXs the same way. Ho II X-1 is a template — a clean, nearby, well-studied system — but it is one object. Webb's integral field spectrographs can now be pointed at other ULX nebulae, in other metal-poor galaxies, to test whether anisotropic photoionization along a jet axis is the rule or the exception. If it is the rule, then ULXs deserve a much larger place in models of how the first galaxies were lit up, and how the universe was reionized.

There is also the question of the radio knots themselves. The northeast and southwest knots are fading remnants of past ejections, but are they one-off events or part of a recurring cycle? Monitoring the radio emission over years, and watching for new knots to appear, would reveal the duty cycle of the jet — how often the black hole fires, and how much energy each outburst carries into the galaxy.

## A small galaxy's outsized lesson

In the end, the story of Holmberg II X-1 is a story about scale. A galaxy with a tenth of the Sun's metals, forming stars at a trickle, hosts a black hole of a few dozen solar masses. That black hole, feeding on a companion star, produces radiation so hard that it strips five electrons from neon atoms and lights up gas a hundred parsecs away — three hundred light-years — in a pattern that traces the axis of its jets. The gas itself barely moves. It simply glows.

It is a reminder that in astronomy, the most revealing objects are not always the biggest or the brightest. Sometimes they are the small, poor, quiet ones, where a single source of energy stands out against the darkness and the physics can be read clearly. Holmberg II gave astronomers that clarity more than twenty years ago, when its glowing helium first hinted at something extraordinary. Webb has now confirmed the suspicion, line by line, pixel by pixel, in the infrared light of neon ions that should not be there — and are, because a black hole put them there.

For a universe that was once filled with galaxies just like Holmberg II, that is a lesson with very long reach.
