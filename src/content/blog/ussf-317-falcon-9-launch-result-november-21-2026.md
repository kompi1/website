The sixth attempt was the one that flew. After five scrubs, a Friday of slipping target times, and a countdown that had been reset more often than any mission in recent memory, SpaceX's Falcon 9 finally carried the classified USSF-317 mission off the pad at Space Launch Complex 4 East at Vandenberg Space Force Base in California on Saturday, November 21, 2026. Liftoff came at 04:20:35 UTC — 11:20 in the morning in Jakarta, 8:20 on Friday evening in California — and a little more than eight minutes later the mission's veteran first stage, booster B1100, touched down on the droneship Of Course I Still Love You in the Pacific Ocean, closing out a launch campaign that had tested the patience of everyone involved in it.

The rocket climbed away on a south-easterly trajectory, the same heading its planners had filed for every attempt before it, and the flight's early milestones unfolded exactly as the launch profile promised. Stage separation came on schedule. The first stage turned, boosted back toward the recovery zone, and made its landing burn over the Pacific before settling onto the droneship's deck at about T+8 minutes and 11 seconds. It was the 235th landing on that vessel and the 679th Falcon booster landing to date — two numbers that would have been unremarkable milestones for any other flight, but that carried a particular weight on this one, because they were the numbers the mission had been chasing through five failed attempts to reach.

What the flight carried, and where exactly it was going, remains what it has always been: classified. SpaceX ended its launch webcast shortly after the booster touched down, so the Falcon 9's upper stage was not shown powering its way to orbit, and neither the company nor the Space Force has said what the rocket is carrying or confirmed that the payload has been deployed. That silence is not new. It is the defining feature of this series of missions, and it is the reason the result of USSF-317 has to be reported in two parts — the part that happened in public, which was flawless, and the part that happened out of sight, which nobody outside the programme can yet confirm.

## Liftoff at last: the sixth attempt flies

The morning of the launch had begun the way the previous days had begun — with a status board that said GO and a countdown that was actually running. At 00:15 UTC on Saturday, about four hours before liftoff, the launch status was formally set to GO, and the countdown proceeded through its final holds without the interruptions that had defined the week. The targeted time, 04:20 UTC, had been set late on Friday evening in the United States, when Launch Library 2 recorded the time moving from 02:32 UTC to 04:20 UTC — a refinement of about one hour and fifty minutes that turned out to be the last adjustment the mission would need.

The four-hour window, running from 02:32 UTC to 06:32 UTC — 09:32 to 13:32 WIB — gave the team room to absorb a delay if one had appeared. None did. When the clock reached zero at 04:20:35 UTC, the nine Merlin engines of the Falcon 9's first stage lit and the rocket left the pad at Vandenberg, climbing into the dark sky over the California coast on a south-easterly track that would carry it out over the Pacific. Spaceflight Now's live coverage, which had followed the mission through its scrub on Thursday night and its slipping times on Friday, recorded the liftoff in a brief update and then, in the early hours of Saturday morning UTC, added the confirmation that mattered most after the launch itself: SpaceX had landed its booster on the drone ship.

For the teams at Vandenberg, the significance of the moment was not only that the rocket had flown. It was that it had flown on a day when the base was supposed to host two launches. USSF-317 was the first half of a planned double-header, with a second Falcon 9 — carrying the classified NROL-136 mission for the National Reconnaissance Office — scheduled to lift off from the same pad, Space Launch Complex 4E, on Saturday evening. That second launch did not happen on Saturday. It slipped by about a day, to Sunday evening, and the reasons for that delay, and the story of the pad's turnaround, are told later in this article. But the morning belonged to USSF-317, and the morning went exactly as planned.

The launch was the 121st Falcon 9 launch of 2026, a number that says as much about the state of orbital access as anything else in this story. A vehicle that flies 121 times in a year is not a novelty; it is infrastructure. And it was the third mission that SpaceX has launched for the Space Force so far in November, after USSF-432 on November 6 and USSF-627 on November 12. The cadence is the context in which USSF-317's five scrubs should be read. In a month when the same pad, the same rocket, and the same recovery ship are flying classified missions every few days, a single mission that takes six attempts to leave the ground is not a sign of a programme in trouble. It is a sign of a programme that can afford to wait for the right moment — and that did, five times, until the sixth attempt was the one that counted.

## Eight minutes to the droneship

If the launch itself was the headline, the landing was the proof. Booster B1100, the Falcon 9 first stage assigned to USSF-317, was flying for the eleventh time. Its previous flights tell the story of a workhorse: it had flown the NROL-105 mission, it had flown USSF-385 — the third mission in this same R-series — and it had flown eight batches of Starlink satellites. Its most recent flight before USSF-317 had been on September 26, 2026, when it carried USSF-385 through dense fog at Vandenberg. On Saturday it added one more flight to that record, and one more landing.

A little more than eight minutes after liftoff, B1100 landed on the droneship Of Course I Still Love You, stationed in the Pacific Ocean off the California coast. The landing came at about T+8 minutes and 11 seconds, following the stage's landing burn, which had begun at about T+7 minutes and 52 seconds. SpaceX's webcast showed the touchdown, and then — as it has done for the previous missions in this series — the coverage ended. The upper stage, and whatever it was carrying, continued on without a public audience.

The numbers attached to the landing are worth pausing over, because they measure how routine the extraordinary has become. This was the 235th landing on Of Course I Still Love You, a ship that has been catching Falcon 9 boosters in the Pacific since the CRS-8 mission in 2016, when it hosted the first ever landing of a Falcon 9 first stage on a drone ship. It was the 679th Falcon booster landing to date — a figure that spans the Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy fleets and that has been climbing by several landings a week for most of this year. And it was the eleventh landing for B1100 itself, a booster that has now flown and landed more times than most rockets ever fly at all.

There is a temptation, when a landing has become this routine, to treat it as a footnote to the launch. That would be a mistake. The landing is the reason the launch can be flown the way it is flown. A first stage that comes home is a first stage that can fly again, and a booster on its eleventh flight is the visible evidence of the economics that let SpaceX fly 121 Falcon 9 missions in a year. USSF-317's five scrubs cost the programme days; the fact that the booster that finally flew was a veteran on its eleventh flight, rather than a new vehicle that had to be built from scratch, is part of why those days were affordable. The rocket that waited on the pad through five scrubs was the same rocket that flew on the sixth attempt, and the same rocket that came home eight minutes later.

USSF-317 will be watched the same way its predecessors were watched — by the people who track what appears in orbit in the days after the launch, and who count what they find.

## The road to Saturday: a timeline of delays

The launch that finally flew on Saturday morning was, in one sense, the end of a story that began months ago, when the first mission in this series lifted off from the same pad. In another sense, it was the end of a much shorter and more frustrating story — the week in which USSF-317 itself tried, five times, to leave the ground. Both stories belong in the record, and both are told here.

- **Aug 16, 2026** — USSF-366 (R-1) launches, the first mission in the R-series. Twenty-three satellites are catalogued afterwards.
- **Sep 10, 2026** — USSF-153 (R-2) launches. Twenty-three more satellites are catalogued.
- **Sep 26, 2026** — USSF-385 (R-3) launches through dense fog at Vandenberg, flown by booster B1100. Twenty-three satellites are catalogued.
- **Nov 6–7, 2026** — USSF-432 (R-4) launches, flown by booster B1095 on its eighth flight. Twenty-three satellites are catalogued.
- **Nov 12, 2026, 11:36 WIB** — USSF-627 (R-5) launches after three slips in four days; booster B1103 lands on Of Course I Still Love You. Twenty-three satellites are catalogued.
- **Thu, Nov 19, 10:16 p.m. EDT (Fri, Nov 20, 10:16 WIB)** — The USSF-317 attempt is scrubbed for the fifth time. SpaceX gives no reason and retargets Friday.
- **Fri, Nov 20, morning** — The Friday target slips from 11:32 WIB to 13:43 WIB, then the mission moves to Saturday, with the window opening at 09:32 WIB.
- **Fri, Nov 20, 17:15 UTC** — Launch Library 2 records the targeted time moving from 02:32 UTC to 04:20 UTC, a delay of about one hour and fifty minutes.
- **Sat, Nov 21, 00:15 UTC** — The launch status is set to GO for launch.
- **Sat, Nov 21, 04:20:35 UTC (11:20:35 WIB)** — USSF-317 lifts off from SLC-4E at Vandenberg on its sixth attempt.
- **Sat, Nov 21, about T+8:11** — Booster B1100 lands on the droneship Of Course I Still Love You, the 235th landing on that vessel and the 679th Falcon booster landing.
- **Sun, Nov 22, 19:16 WIB** — The day's second launch, NROL-136, is now scheduled — about 24 hours later than planned, after a delay logged on Friday, November 20.

The fifth scrub deserves a closer look, because it is the moment the week's story turned. USSF-317 had first been aimed at Thursday, November 19. That attempt ended in a scrub: SpaceX called off the Thursday night launch at 10:16 p.m. Eastern time — 03:16 UTC on Friday, or 10:16 WIB on Friday morning — and did not state a reason. Spaceflight Now's live coverage recorded the scrub in a brief update and noted that SpaceX was retargeting Friday. It was the fifth time the mission had been scrubbed, and by then the delays had become the story as much as the launch. SpaceNews covered that fifth scrub in its [final preview of the mission, published on Saturday morning](/blog/ussf-317-falcon-9-launch-november-21-2026-final-preview/), which set out the full history of the campaign and the stakes of the sixth attempt.

The pattern is not new for this series. The previous mission in the same programme, USSF-627, also slipped three times in four days before finally lifting off on Thursday, November 12, at 11:36 WIB, when booster B1103 — flying for the seventh time — climbed away from the same pad and landed on the same droneship about eight and a half minutes later. SpaceNews reported that result in full in its [USSF-627 launch result article](/blog/ussf-627-falcon-9-launch-result-november-12-2026/). USSF-317 has now exceeded that record of patience. It has been scrubbed five times, rescheduled repeatedly, and has spent more days on the pad than any of its sister missions managed before flying. On Saturday morning, the sixth attempt was finally the one that stuck.

And the delays matter, in their own way. A mission scrubbed five times is a mission that has been tested by its own ground campaign, and a booster that has waited on the pad through five scrubs is a booster whose teams have rehearsed the countdown until it is muscle memory. The history of spaceflight is full of launches that failed because the ground teams were rushed; it has very few examples of launches that failed because the ground teams waited too long. USSF-317 waited. On Saturday morning, at 11:20 WIB, the waiting ended — and the rocket that lifted off carried with it not just a classified payload, but the accumulated patience of a week that refused to hurry.

## What was on board — and what we still do not know

The honest answer to the most obvious question about USSF-317 is that nobody outside the programme knows, and the programme is not telling. The mission is classified. SpaceX has not said how many satellites the Falcon 9 carried, what they do, or what orbit they were aimed at. The Space Force has not confirmed the deployment, and it has not confirmed the details of any of the five missions that came before this one in the same series. That is not an oversight or a delay in the reporting. It is the policy, and it has been the policy for every R-series launch since the first one in August.

What can be said, and what the reporting supports, is narrower — and it is still substantial. USSF-317 is the sixth mission in a series of launches that the Space Force has assigned to SpaceX under the designation R-1 through R-6. The first five — USSF-366 on August 16, USSF-153 on September 10, USSF-385 on September 26, USSF-432 on November 6, and USSF-627 on November 12 — were all followed by the same observation: 23 satellites were catalogued in orbit after each launch. That figure comes from the independent satellite-tracking community, which watches for new objects appearing in orbit and records them, and it has now been repeated five times with the same count. It is the single most consistent fact in the public record of this programme, and it is the reason analysts treat these missions as a single, deliberate constellation-building effort rather than six unrelated classified launches.

The leading public assessment of what that constellation is for comes from the same open-source analysis that first connected the R-series to SpaceX's Starshield satellite bus. The working hypothesis, reported by independent trackers and repeated in launch coverage, is that the satellites are a batch of classified low Earth orbit spacecraft built on the Starshield bus — which is itself derived from SpaceX's Starlink v2 Mini design — for a mission described as Space Based Sensing and Targeting, with the satellites forming part of a data-network backbone. The evidence for that reading is circumstantial but specific: the rocket's drop zones match the pattern of previous Starlink Group 15 launches, and open-source trackers have estimated the target orbit at roughly 535 kilometres at a 70-degree inclination. None of that has been confirmed by the Space Force or by SpaceX, and it should not be reported as if it had been. But it is the best public account of the mission's purpose, and it is consistent with everything that is known about the five flights that preceded it.

The earlier missions in the series give the hypothesis its weight. USSF-385, the third R-series mission, was flown by this same booster, B1100, through dense fog at Vandenberg on September 26, and was followed by the same count of 23 catalogued satellites. SpaceNews reported that flight in its [USSF-385 Starshield launch article](/blog/ussf-385-starshield-launch-vandenberg/), which set out the Starshield assessment and the evidence behind it. USSF-317, flown by the same booster two months later, fits the same pattern in every respect that can be observed from outside: the same pad, the same south-easterly trajectory, the same recovery ship, the same silence afterwards.

There is a second, simpler way to read the silence, and it is worth stating plainly. The Space Force does not confirm these deployments because the deployments are the mission. A constellation built for sensing and targeting is useful in proportion to how little an adversary knows about where its satellites are, what they can see, and when they were put there. The 23 satellites catalogued after each previous launch are visible to anyone with a telescope and a tracking programme — the secrecy is not about whether the satellites exist, but about what the programme says about them. The refusal to confirm is itself a signal, and it has been consistent across all six missions. USSF-317's silence is not a gap in the story. It is the story, told the way the Space Force tells it.

What happens next, in public at least, is a waiting game measured in days. Independent trackers will watch for new objects in the expected orbit, and the catalogues will be updated as objects are found and confirmed. If the pattern of the previous five missions holds, 23 new satellites will appear in the records over the coming days, and the R-series will have added a sixth identical layer to whatever it is building. If the count is different, that difference will itself be news — the first break in a pattern that has held since August. Either way, the confirmation, when it comes, will come from the trackers, not from the programme. SpaceNews will report it when it arrives.

## A record year, and a crowded November

USSF-317 was the 121st Falcon 9 launch of 2026, and that number deserves a moment of its own. A single rocket family flying 121 times in less than eleven months is a tempo that no launch vehicle in history has sustained, and it is a tempo that changes what a launch means. When a rocket flies this often, an individual mission stops being an event and becomes a schedule — and a schedule is exactly what the Space Force is buying. Three classified missions in a single month from a single pad, each on a few days' notice, each recovered on the same droneship, is not a demonstration. It is an operation.

November has been the sharpest illustration of that operation. USSF-432 flew on November 6. USSF-627 flew on November 12, after three slips in four days. USSF-317 flew on November 21, after five scrubs. Three missions, three boosters, one pad, one recovery ship, and a combined scrub count that would have grounded a slower programme for a month. Vandenberg absorbed all of it and kept flying, and the pad that launched USSF-317 on Saturday morning was scheduled to launch again on Saturday evening — a turnaround measured in hours, not days.

The pace is not slowing. Spaceflight Now's coverage notes that SpaceX is scheduled to launch at least two more missions for the Space Force before the end of the month, and the wider manifest for the coming days is crowded in a way that would have been unthinkable a few years ago. The next launches on the board include the delayed NROL-136 on Sunday evening, the Amazon Leo mission LE-04 on an Ariane 64 from Kourou on Monday, and USSF-266 — another classified Space Force mission on a Falcon 9 — late on Monday night. Each of those missions has its own story, and each will be covered in its own article. But together they describe a launch industry that no longer measures its year in launches, but in launches per week — and a November in which the week's count keeps climbing.

For the Space Force, the significance of that tempo is strategic rather than statistical. A programme that can put 23 satellites into orbit every few weeks, on a rocket that lands and flies again, is a programme that can build a constellation the way a factory builds a product — in batches, on a schedule, with the losses from any single failure absorbed by the next flight. The R-series has now flown six times in three months, and if the pattern holds, it has put something like 138 satellites into orbit in that time, without a single public statement about any of them. That is the quiet arithmetic behind USSF-317's loud morning, and it is the reason the mission matters more than its classified payload would suggest.

## The double-header that did not happen

Saturday at Vandenberg was supposed to be a double-header. The morning's launch of USSF-317 was to be followed, on Saturday evening, by a second Falcon 9 from the same pad — Space Launch Complex 4E — carrying NROL-136, a classified mission for the National Reconnaissance Office, with booster B1097 flying for the fourteenth time and a planned landing at Landing Zone 4 on the base itself. Two classified Falcon 9 launches from the same pad on the same day would have been a measure of how far Vandenberg's launch tempo has come, and a reminder that USSF-317, for all its delays, was only the first half of the day's work.

It did not happen. NROL-136 was delayed by about 24 hours, with the new target set for Sunday, November 22, at 19:16 WIB — 12:16 UTC — inside a window running until 19:46 WIB. The delay was logged on Friday, November 20, at 16:15 UTC, according to Launch Library 2 as reported by Space Launch Live, and it was recorded as a straightforward schedule slip: no official announcement from SpaceX or the National Reconnaissance Office has been found, and no reason for the delay has been stated by any source. The mission's details are unchanged — a Falcon 9 from SLC-4E, booster B1097 on its fourteenth flight, landing at LZ-4 — but the day is different, and the double-header is not.

The slip is worth reporting carefully, because it corrects a claim that SpaceNews itself published on Saturday morning. The final preview of USSF-317, published in the 07:53 edition, described Saturday as a double-header and set out the evening's NROL-136 launch as the second half of the day. That was accurate when it was written, and it is no longer accurate. The delay was logged on Friday afternoon UTC — before the preview was published — but it only became visible in the trackers on Saturday, and SpaceNews is correcting the record here rather than letting the stale claim stand. The preview's account of the morning's mission was right in every respect; its account of the evening's mission was overtaken by a delay that nobody had announced.

There is a small irony in the timing. USSF-317's own campaign was defined by delays that were never explained, and NROL-136's delay is defined the same way: a new time in the trackers, no statement, no reason. The two missions share a pad, a rocket family, and a classification, and now they share a scheduling habit as well. The pad that launched USSF-317 on Saturday morning will launch NROL-136 on Sunday evening instead, and the turnaround that was supposed to be measured in hours will be measured in a day and a half. For a spaceport flying at this tempo, a day and a half is not a failure. It is a rounding error. But it is also a reminder that the schedule, however crowded, is still written in pencil.

## What comes next

The calendar after USSF-317 does not pause. NROL-136 is now the next launch on the board, on Sunday evening at 19:16 WIB, and its result will be covered in the safe slot on Monday morning. Monday itself is crowded: the Amazon Leo mission LE-04 is targeted for Monday, November 23, at 18:09 WIB, flying on an Ariane 64 from Kourou in French Guiana with 36 satellites aboard, and USSF-266 — another classified Space Force mission on a Falcon 9, with booster B1081 flying for the 29th time — is targeted for late on Monday night at 23:04 WIB. Each of those missions will be covered as it flies, and the R-series will be watched, as it always is, for the satellites that appear in its wake.

The longer question is what the R-series becomes. Six missions in three months, each followed by 23 catalogued satellites, is a constellation by any reasonable measure — and a constellation built at this pace, on reusable rockets, from a single pad, is a capability that did not exist a decade ago. Whether the programme continues at this rate, whether the next missions follow the same pattern, and whether the Space Force ever says anything about what it has built, are questions that will be answered the same way the previous ones were: by watching the sky, counting what appears in it, and reporting what the trackers find. The silence will continue. The satellites, in all likelihood, will not.

For now, the record of Saturday morning is complete, and it is a good one. A rocket that had been scrubbed five times flew on its sixth attempt. A booster on its eleventh flight came home for the eleventh time. A droneship caught its 235th booster, and a rocket family recorded its 679th landing and its 121st launch of the year. The payload is classified, the deployment is unconfirmed, and the programme has said nothing — and none of that changes what happened at 11:20 WIB on Saturday morning, when the waiting finally ended and the sixth attempt flew.
