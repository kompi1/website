Four years ago, humanity deliberately smashed a spacecraft into an asteroid and watched the asteroid flinch. The Double Asteroid Redirection Test, or DART, struck the little moonlet Dimorphos on 26 September 2022 at more than 22,000 kilometres per hour, and telescopes on Earth confirmed that the impact had shortened Dimorphos's orbit around its parent asteroid Didymos by about 33 minutes. It was, by every measure available at the time, a success. But DART was a one-way mission. It carried no camera that survived the impact, no instrument that could linger and look at what it had done. For four years, the most consequential collision humans have ever staged in space has been studied from 11 million kilometres away, through telescopes that could measure the change in the orbit but could not see the crater, the rubble, or the reshaped rock itself.

That has now changed. The European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft, which arrived at the Didymos system after a two-year cruise, has closed to within 25 to 30 kilometres of its target — 15 to 18 miles — and has begun the close-up investigation DART could never perform. In a set of images released in mid-November, Hera's cameras show Didymos in detail down to about 2.7 metres per pixel, its spinning-top shape picked out in crisp black and white from 28.5 kilometres away. And Hera is not looking with one kind of eye. A hyperspectral imager is reading the asteroids in 25 different colour bands, searching for the chemical fingerprints of their minerals. A thermal infrared camera supplied by Japan's space agency is mapping their heat, seeing straight through the shadows that hide Dimorphos from ordinary cameras. ESA calls it superhuman vision, and for the first time, the forensic examination of humanity's first planetary defence test is being conducted at the scene of the crime.

The next milestone is days away. On Wednesday 25 November, ESA will share the first close-up images of Dimorphos itself — the actual rock DART hit — at a live event in the dinosaur hall of Berlin's Museum für Naturkunde, streamed from 19:45 to 20:30 CET. It is, the agency says, only the start. In the coming months Hera will fly closer still, and early in the new year it will release a pair of shoebox-sized CubeSats to take risks the main spacecraft cannot, venturing nearer than ever and eventually attempting to touch down on Dimorphos itself.

## The view from 25 kilometres

Hera's arrival has been choreographed with unusual care. The spacecraft launched on 7 October 2024, when a Falcon 9 rocket lifted the 1,150-kilogram spacecraft from Cape Canaveral, and spent two years crossing the inner Solar System before reaching the Didymos system, some 11 million kilometres from Earth. Its first images, released in late October, were modest by any ordinary photographic standard: from 1,280 kilometres away, Didymos and Dimorphos appeared as two separate points of light, the smaller moonlet occupying barely a single pixel. But that single pixel carried the weight of an entire field of science, as SpaceNews reported in <a href="/blog/hera-first-image-didymos-dimorphos-2026/">Hera First Image of Didymos and Dimorphos 2026: One Pixel, Two Asteroids, and the Future of Planetary Defense</a>. Until that image, Hera had seen its destination only as one unresolved dot. From that moment on, the spacecraft was flying toward a place it could see.

A week later, the view sharpened dramatically. On 5 November 2026, Hera returned its first close-up view of the binary system, photographed from just 60 kilometres away by its black-and-white Asteroid Framing Camera. The image showed Didymos in crisp detail — boulders and craters and strange groove-like patterns spread across a hemisphere no spacecraft had ever seen — while Dimorphos lurked mostly in shadow at the edge of the frame. SpaceNews covered that image in <a href="/blog/hera-didymos-close-up-60-km-november-2026/">Hera Didymos Close-Up 60 km: ESA Sees the Hidden Face of the Asteroid DART Struck</a>, and the mission team described it as like looking at a whole new world.

The November images go further still. ESA announced on 12 November that Hera was now studying the pair from just 25 to 30 kilometres away, and the images released a week later, on 19 November, show what that proximity buys. The Asteroid Framing Camera image of Didymos was taken from roughly 28.5 kilometres — 17.7 miles — with features resolved down to about 2.7 metres per pixel. At that scale, individual boulders the size of a car are visible, and the surface texture of a world only 780 metres across can be read like a landscape. For comparison, the first image Hera sent back in October resolved nothing at all: the entire moonlet was a single pixel. In less than a month, the mission has gone from counting pixels to counting boulders.

The distance matters for more than picture quality. Hera's job is not photography for its own sake but measurement, and measurement needs proximity. The spacecraft must determine the mass of Dimorphos, map the shape of the crater DART excavated — or discover that there is no crater at all — and work out how the asteroid's interior responded to the impact. None of that can be done from 1,280 kilometres. At 25 kilometres, the instruments can begin the real work, and in December Hera will close in further still, to about 20 kilometres. The investigation is planned to last about six months, and every kilometre closer sharpens the answers.

## Three ways of seeing

What makes the November images remarkable is not only their sharpness but their variety. Hera is carrying three different kinds of imaging system, and each one sees the asteroids in a different way. Together, they amount to something no previous asteroid mission has attempted at such close range on a body humans have deliberately altered.

The standard eyes are the Asteroid Framing Cameras, which handle both black-and-white photography and navigation. They are the workhorses, and they produced the crisp 28.5-kilometre image of Didymos that anchors the November release. In it, the asteroid's spinning-top shape is unmistakable: fatter at the middle than at the poles, the silhouette of a body spinning fast enough to push its loose surface material toward the equator.

The second system is the HyperScout H instrument, and it is the one that earns the mission its "superhuman" description. Where the framing cameras see in black and white, HyperScout H monitors the asteroids in 25 different colour bands. That is not photography in the ordinary sense; it is spectroscopy, performed from orbit around a rock the size of a city block. Different minerals absorb and reflect light in different patterns across those bands, so by comparing what HyperScout H records against laboratory experiments on meteorite samples on Earth, scientists can work out what Didymos and Dimorphos are actually made of — not merely what they look like. A boulder that appears grey in a black-and-white image might be revealed as a particular kind of silicate, or a carbon-rich fragment, or a piece of metal, and each of those possibilities changes the story of how the asteroid formed and how it responded to being hit.

The third system is the most striking of all. The Thermal Infrared Imager, known as TIRI and supplied to ESA by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, gives Hera what the agency's press release calls Superman-like heat vision. TIRI maps the temperature of the asteroids' surfaces, and temperature is a proxy for solidity: solid rock holds its heat and releases it slowly, while porous or fragmented rubble cools quickly. By watching how the surface warms in sunlight and cools in shadow, TIRI can tell scientists how solid Didymos really is — whether it is a coherent boulder or a loosely bound pile of gravel held together by its own weak gravity.

That distinction is the heart of the mission. If Dimorphos is a solid rock, a kinetic impactor transfers its momentum cleanly, like a cue ball striking another ball. If it is a rubble pile, the energy goes into shifting and compacting the fragments, and the outcome is harder to predict. The DART impact suggested the truth is complicated: the orbital change was larger than pre-impact models predicted, and part of the reason was the debris. When DART struck, it sent rubble and even boulders flying off Dimorphos, and that escaping material provided an extra kick of momentum beyond the spacecraft's own impact — a recoil effect, working much like the thrust from a rocket exhaust. Understanding how much of the deflection came from the impact itself and how much from the recoil is exactly the kind of question TIRI and HyperScout H are designed to answer, and it is a question that matters enormously for the day — hopefully never — when humanity needs to deflect a real threat.

## The spinning top

The November images also sharpened a picture of Didymos itself that had been emerging since the 60-kilometre view. The larger asteroid is a spinning top, and it spins fast. Didymos completes a rotation roughly every 2.2 hours, a brisk pace for a body 780 metres across, and that spin has consequences for everything on its surface. Centrifugal force pulls boulders and gravel toward the middle, building up an equatorial ridge that makes the asteroid fatter at its equator than at its poles. The shape is a direct record of the asteroid's spin history, and it is also a clue to its internal structure: only a body with a certain balance of cohesion and looseness can hold that shape while spinning that fast.

The surface details now visible — the boulders, the craters, the groove-like patterns — add to that picture. Grooves on an asteroid are often the scars of past stresses: fractures from ancient impacts, or the tracks left as material shifts across the surface. On a spinning top, material migrates, and the grooves may record where it has gone. The craters, meanwhile, are a clock of sorts. Didymos has no atmosphere to erase them, so each crater is a surviving record of a collision, and counting them is one way of estimating the age of the surface. Hera's images, at 2.7 metres per pixel, will let scientists count craters far smaller than any telescope on Earth could resolve, and from that count they can begin to date the surface and reconstruct the system's history.

There is a larger question behind all of this, and it is about how binary asteroids form. Didymos and Dimorphos are a pair, and the leading explanation for such systems is that the parent body spun up — perhaps under the gentle push of sunlight over millions of years — until material shed from its equator coalesced into a moonlet. If that is what happened, then Dimorphos is made of the same material as Didymos's surface, and its internal structure should resemble its parent's. Hera's close-up measurements of both bodies will test that idea directly, and the answer feeds into models of how the Solar System's small bodies evolve.

## Dimorphos, hiding in the shadow

There is one notable absence from the November images, and it is the most important object in the whole mission. Dimorphos itself — the actual rock DART struck — is surprisingly hard to see in the visible-light images. In most of the frames, the moonlet is tucked almost entirely into the shadow of Didymos, appearing as a tiny sunlit speck beneath its parent in one image and as little more than a suggestion in the others. It is a reminder of how small the target is: 151 metres across, orbiting a body only five times its size, at a distance where the geometry of sunlight and shadow can hide it almost completely.

That is where TIRI earns its keep. In the thermal infrared image, the whole of Dimorphos is clearly visible, glowing a shade of cool purple against the dark. Heat does not need sunlight to be seen; it radiates from the rock itself. The infrared view shows the moonlet in full, and it is the first time since the impact that Dimorphos has been seen as a whole body rather than as a speck or a shadow. It is a modest image by the standards of the framing cameras, but it is arguably the most important one Hera has returned so far, because it proves that the mission can study its primary target even when the lighting is against it.

The hiding of Dimorphos also explains why the 25 November event matters so much. ESA has announced that the livestream from Berlin will share the first close-up images of Dimorphos itself — the first views of the transformed moonlet in detail, showing how people altered it. The venue is deliberate: the dinosaur hall of the Museum für Naturkunde, where the skeletons of creatures killed by an asteroid impact 66 million years ago stand as a reminder of what is at stake. On 25 November, the museum will mark a much smaller but consequential impact — the first time that the human descendants of those early mammals managed to shift an asteroid in its course, moving part of the Solar System in a measurable way. The livestream runs from 19:45 to 20:30 CET and will be carried on ESA WebTV and the ESA YouTube channel.

## Why the maths matters

For four years, the DART result has been a number in search of an explanation. The orbit of Dimorphos around Didymos shortened by about 33 minutes — from a period of about 11 hours 55 minutes to about 11 hours 22 minutes — and ground-based observatories measured that change with confidence. But some scientists were surprised by the magnitude of the change, based on the theoretical calculations done before the mission. The impact did more than the models predicted, and the extra effect came from the ejecta: millions of tons of debris liberated by the collision, some of it in boulders large enough to be tracked individually, flying off into space and pushing the moonlet in the opposite direction like exhaust from a rocket.

That is good news for planetary defence in one sense — the deflection was bigger than expected — but it is also a warning. If the extra kick depends on how much debris an impact throws off, and that depends on the asteroid's internal structure, then the outcome of a kinetic impact is only as predictable as our knowledge of the target. Hit a solid rock and you get one result. Hit a rubble pile and you get another. Hit a body whose surface is a thin crust over a hollow interior and you might get something else entirely. The only way to make the technique reliable is to understand the target first, and that is what Hera is for.

Computer simulations published in Nature Astronomy have already suggested that the reality may be stranger than a simple crater. The modelling indicates that Dimorphos may have suffered a global reshaping rather than a localised crater excavation: roughly 1 percent of the moonlet's mass escaped into space as ejecta, while about 8 percent of its internal mass was redistributed, causing the whole body to settle into an altered, triaxial shape. If that is right, then DART did not merely dig a hole in Dimorphos. It rearranged the asteroid. Hera's close-range optical and thermal imaging will test that prediction directly, and the answer will determine how future deflection missions are designed. A crater is a local wound. A global reshaping is a different kind of event altogether, and it changes the arithmetic of how much momentum an impactor can deliver.

The stakes are not abstract. The asteroids Hera is studying are not themselves a threat to Earth — their orbits are well known and they will not strike our planet — but they are stand-ins for the ones that might. Planetary defence is the science of preparing for an event that has happened before, catastrophically, and will happen again on geological timescales. The dinosaur-killing impact 66 million years ago is the famous example, but smaller impacts happen far more often, and the historical record is full of airbursts and crater-forming strikes. The question is not whether another dangerous asteroid will be found on a collision course with Earth, but whether humanity will be ready when it is. Hera is the mission that turns a successful experiment into a reliable technique, and the difference between those two things is the difference between having hit an asteroid once and knowing how to do it again, on a different asteroid, with confidence.

## From impact to investigation

### Timeline

- **26 September 2022** — NASA's DART spacecraft strikes Dimorphos at more than 22,000 km/h, in humanity's first full-scale test of deflecting an asteroid by kinetic impact. Ground telescopes confirm the moonlet's orbit shortens by about 33 minutes.
- **7 October 2024** — ESA's Hera spacecraft launches aboard a Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral, beginning its two-year cruise to the Didymos system.
- **27 October 2026** — Hera resolves Didymos and Dimorphos as two separate bodies for the first time, from 1,280 kilometres away. Dimorphos occupies about one pixel.
- **5 November 2026** — Hera returns its first close-up view from 60 kilometres, showing the far side of Didymos in detail no spacecraft had ever seen.
- **12 November 2026** — ESA announces Hera is now studying the pair from 25 to 30 kilometres away, beginning its close inspection of the impact site.
- **19 November 2026** — ESA releases the new images: an Asteroid Framing Camera view from 28.5 kilometres at 2.7 metres per pixel, hyperspectral data from HyperScout H, and a thermal infrared view from TIRI in which Dimorphos glows cool purple.
- **25 November 2026** — Live event at the Museum für Naturkunde in Berlin, 19:45–20:30 CET: the first close-up images of Dimorphos itself.
- **December 2026** — Hera closes to about 20 kilometres for even sharper views of both asteroids.
- **Early 2027** — The CubeSats Juventas and Milani deploy, using ground-penetrating radar and venturing closer than the main spacecraft, with an eventual attempt to land on Dimorphos.

The timeline shows how quickly the mission has accelerated. Hera spent two years in transit and then, in less than a month, went from a single pixel to a 2.7-metre view. The pace will not slow: the December approach, the CubeSat deployment, and the landing attempt are all milestones that would each have been a mission highlight on their own. Taken together, they amount to the most thorough examination ever conducted of a body humans have deliberately changed.

## What happens next

The 25 November event is the immediate focus, but it is only the start of Hera's exploration, as mission scientist Michael Kueppers has emphasised. In the next few months the spacecraft will fly closer to the asteroids, and early in the new year it will deploy its pair of shoebox-sized CubeSats. The two small spacecraft — Juventas and Milani — will take extra risks and venture even nearer than the main spacecraft can, carrying instruments Hera itself does not have. Juventas carries a low-frequency radar designed to peer inside Dimorphos, mapping its internal structure in a way no external camera ever could. Milani will study the surface composition up close. Together, they will give researchers their first direct contact with a human-made crater on another world's small body — or, if the simulations are right, with a surface that has been globally reshaped rather than locally cratered.

The eventual landing attempt is the most ambitious part of the plan. A successful touchdown on Dimorphos would give scientists their first physical contact with the aftermath of a kinetic impact, and it would test whether the surface can bear the weight of a lander at all. On a body with almost no gravity, landing is less a matter of descending than of docking with a rock that is falling around the Sun, and the attempt will be made only after Hera has mapped the surface in detail and chosen a site. If it succeeds, the data will be the definitive physical evidence of how the asteroid responded to a high-speed impact.

Beyond the Didymos system, Hera's findings will feed into the wider work of planetary defence. Other missions are already watching the sky: NASA's NEO Surveyor, an infrared space telescope designed to find hazardous asteroids, has been through sunshade tests on its way to launch, and ground-based surveys continue to catalogue near-Earth objects. The discovery of small visitors like 2026 QQ8 — a one-metre asteroid that Earth's gravity captured in April 2026 and will release in December, as SpaceNews reported in <a href="/blog/2026-qq8-mini-moon-earth-november-2026/">2026 QQ8 Mini-Moon: Earth's Tiny Temporary Second Moon</a> — is a reminder that the Solar System is full of small bodies, most of them harmless, a few of them not. The difference between a curiosity and a catastrophe is size and trajectory, and the only way to know which is which is to look, to measure, and to understand. That is what Hera is doing, 25 kilometres from a rock that humanity moved.

There is also a human story in the mission that deserves noting. Hera's principal investigator Patrick Michel has described the investigation as the most thorough ever conducted of a deflected asteroid, packed with scientific firsts. Mission manager Ian Carnelli has quoted Steve Jobs — "We're here to put a dent in the Universe" — and added that the team managed to do just that, for the highest of intentions: to help make Earth safer. The team reached this point, Carnelli noted, in the face of an initial failure to secure funding, then a worldwide pandemic, and finally an incoming hurricane as the spacecraft was about to launch. The spacecraft that survived all of that is now 25 kilometres from its target, looking at it in three kinds of light at once, and about to show the world the first close-up images of the rock humanity moved.

## Why it matters

It is easy to treat planetary defence as a distant concern, a problem for some future generation. The sky, after all, looks empty, and the odds of a major impact in any given year are small. But the record of the Earth is written in craters, and the record of the Solar System is written in collisions. The Moon's face is scarred by them. The asteroid belt is the rubble of them. And 66 million years ago, a rock about 10 kilometres across ended the reign of the dinosaurs and cleared the way for mammals — for us.

Hera's work is the practical answer to that history. DART proved that humans can move an asteroid. Hera is proving that humans can understand what they did when they moved it — how much of the deflection came from the impact, how much from the debris, and whether the target was cratered or reshaped. Without that understanding, DART would remain a single successful experiment. With it, kinetic deflection becomes a technique: repeatable, predictable, and ready to be used on a different asteroid, of a different size and structure, with confidence in the result.

The images released on 19 November are the first evidence that the technique is being built properly. A spacecraft 25 kilometres from its target, seeing in black and white, in 25 colours, and in heat, has already shown more about Didymos than four years of Earth-based observation could. On 25 November, the world will see Dimorphos itself, close up, for the first time since a spacecraft struck it at 22,000 kilometres per hour. What Hera finds there — a crater, a reshaped rubble pile, or something nobody predicted — will decide how the next deflection mission is designed. Four years ago, humanity moved an asteroid. Now, at last, humanity is finding out what it did.
