title: "Hubble Overturns Theory of How the Universe's First Light Escaped"
description: "Hubble observations of nearby starburst galaxies show supernova-driven winds trap ionizing radiation rather than clearing its escape — rewriting how astronomers think cosmic reionization happened."
pubDate: 2026-10-23
category: 'Astronomy'
---

For decades, astronomers have puzzled over one of the grandest mysteries in cosmology: how did the universe turn on its lights? In the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, the cosmos was filled with neutral hydrogen gas that soaked up ultraviolet light like a cosmic fog, hiding everything behind it. Then, somewhere within the first billion years, that fog lifted. Starlight flooded intergalactic space, the gas was ionized, and the universe became transparent to ultraviolet light for the first time. Cosmologists call this the epoch of reionization — and according to the field's newest result, one of the leading explanations for how it happened has just been turned on its head.

The finding comes from the Hubble Space Telescope, the veteran NASA observatory that has spent more than three decades mapping the cosmos. A team led by Cody Carr, a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Astronomy at the University of Michigan and an Extremely Large Telescope Fellow, used Hubble to study a set of nearby galaxies furiously forming new stars. Their conclusion, published in the journal *Nature Astronomy* in mid-October 2026, is striking: the blazing winds blown by exploding stars — supernovae — did not clear the way for ultraviolet light to escape those galaxies. Instead, the winds appear to have slammed the escape hatches shut.

The paper, titled "Lyman continuum escape during the onset of galactic winds in compact starbursts," involved nearly 30 collaborators from more than a dozen institutions around the world. Senior authors Renyue Cen, director of the Center for Cosmology and Computational Astrophysics at Zhejiang University, and Stephan McCandliss, director of the Center for Astrophysical Sciences at Johns Hopkins University, led the analysis alongside Carr, who conducted the research while a postdoctoral researcher at Zhejiang University. University of Michigan astronomy professor Sally Oey was also among the contributors. Hubble itself — a NASA mission launched with contributions from the European Space Agency — supported the work through a grant administered by the Space Telescope Science Institute.

The result matters because reionization is not some minor historical footnote. "Eventually, when it ionized the majority of hydrogen filling intergalactic space, that was the end of reionization and we see the universe today," Carr said. "We call reionization the last major phase transition of the universe."

## The last great fog

To appreciate what the team found, it helps to rewind the cosmic clock. The universe began 13.8 billion years ago in the Big Bang, and for its first few hundred thousand years it was a hot, glowing plasma. Then, as space expanded and cooled, protons and electrons combined into neutral hydrogen atoms in an event called recombination — and the lights went out. For the next hundred million years or so, astronomers speak of the cosmic dark ages: no stars, no galaxies, only a cold sea of hydrogen soaking up any stray ultraviolet photons.

Then gravity began its slow construction project. The first stars ignited, the first galaxies assembled, and their ultraviolet radiation started chewing through the fog, carving ionized bubbles that eventually merged until the entire intergalactic medium was reionized. When that process finished — roughly a billion years after the Big Bang — light could travel freely across the universe, and the dark ages were over.

Everyone agrees on the broad strokes. The devil is in the details: how, exactly, did enough ultraviolet light get *out* of those early galaxies to reionize everything? This is a far trickier question than it sounds. A young, star-forming galaxy is a dusty, gassy place, and neutral hydrogen plus dust is brutally effective at absorbing the very ultraviolet photons — the so-called Lyman continuum — that do the ionizing. For reionization to work, a meaningful fraction of that ionizing light had to escape each galaxy and reach intergalactic space.

For years, one of the leading explanations went like this: supernovae did the plumbing. When massive stars die, they detonate with staggering violence, and the collective energy of many supernovae blows powerful winds of gas out of the galaxy. The conventional picture held that these winds punched channels through the surrounding gas, like chimneys through a roof, through which ultraviolet light could stream freely into the intergalactic medium. It was a neat, intuitive story — the universe's most violent explosions literally blasting open the doors for light. It also became baked into the computer simulations cosmologists use to model the early universe, where supernovae play a starring role in explaining how ionizing radiation escaped.

## The experiment: nearby galaxies as time machines

There is an obvious obstacle to testing this story directly. The galaxies that caused reionization are more than 13 billion light-years away, unimaginably faint, and their escaping ultraviolet light is swallowed by the intervening intergalactic medium long before it reaches us. You cannot simply point a telescope at the epoch of reionization and watch photons leak out of a galaxy.

Carr's team took the next best route: they built a time machine out of nearby galaxies. In the local universe there exist compact starburst galaxies — small galaxies forming stars at furious rates — that look physically similar to the kinds of galaxies thought to have driven reionization. Because these galaxies are close, Hubble can dissect their light in exquisite detail.

The technique is a beautiful piece of astrophysical detective work. The team observed the galaxies' spectra — the rainbow of their light spread out by wavelength — and looked for the fingerprints of moving gas. Neutral hydrogen and dust in a galaxy's outflowing wind absorb ultraviolet light at characteristic wavelengths, leaving telltale dips in the spectrum. The positions and widths of those dips reveal how fast the gas is moving. By measuring both the wind speeds and the fraction of ionizing radiation escaping each galaxy, the team could test the supernova-wind story head-on: if the theory is right, galaxies with the fastest, most violent winds should show the most escaping light.

They found the opposite.

Across the observed galaxies, faster winds were consistently associated with *lower* escape fractions of ionizing radiation. The galaxies where ionizing light escaped most freely were the ones where supernova-driven winds were essentially absent — or had not yet had time to develop. In other words, the radiation was getting out *before* the winds formed, not because of them.

According to the team's analysis, ionizing light escapes most efficiently in the first few million years of a starburst, before supernova-driven winds have had time to load the galaxy's outflows with neutral gas. The escape fractions measured by the team fall dramatically with age: from roughly one photon in five escaping when a starburst is only about 2.7 million years old, to fewer than one in 250 by the time the starburst reaches roughly 5.4 million years of age. The window for escape slams shut astonishingly fast — within the lifetime of a single generation of massive stars.

## "Basically, how these galaxies were leaking ionizing radiation isn't what we thought"

The researchers are blunt about what this means. "Basically, how these galaxies were leaking ionizing radiation isn't what we thought," Carr said. "Our observations show that these photons are escaping before galactic winds form."

Stephan McCandliss, one of the senior authors, offered a physical picture of what is happening instead: rather than clearing channels, supernova energy churns up the galaxy's surroundings and drapes more absorbing material across the escape routes. "It appears that energy input from supernovae has stirred up the surroundings and increased, rather than decreased, the coverage of neutral gas and dust that suppresses ionizing radiation escape," McCandliss said.

Think of it this way: instead of a chimney punched through a roof, the supernova winds behave more like a dust storm kicked up around a house. The more violently the wind blows, the more neutral gas and dust gets mixed into the outflow, and the thicker the blanket that traps the ultraviolet light inside. The faster the wind, the darker the galaxy stays.

The findings strengthen an alternate theory that has been gaining ground in recent years. In this view, it is the activity of young stars themselves — their intense radiation and gentler, smaller-scale winds from stellar birth regions — that carves the escape routes for ionizing photons, and all of this happens *before* the first supernovae detonate. By the time the massive stars die and the supernova-driven winds roar to life, the main opportunity for light to escape has already passed. The supernovae arrive too late to help, and their winds only make things worse.

That distinction matters enormously for cosmological simulations. "Supernovae play a central role in cosmological hydrodynamic simulations that explain how ionizing radiation escapes from galaxies," said Renyue Cen. "Our findings highlight the need to examine how radiation and winds from young stars shape the surrounding gas before the first supernovae explode." In other words, the physics engines that cosmologists use to model the early universe may need a significant rewrite in the crucial first few million years of a starburst's life.

## Why the timing works — and why it is so surprising

One reason the result feels counterintuitive is that supernovae really are among the most energetic events in the universe, and they visibly reshape galaxies. A single supernova can outshine an entire galaxy for weeks; the collective energy of thousands can drive gas clean out of a galaxy's gravitational grip. It was natural to assume that anything that violent would blast holes through the surrounding medium.

But the key insight from the new work is a matter of timing. Massive stars — the ones that end their lives as supernovae — live fast and die young, but "young" in cosmic terms still means a few million years. Before they explode, they spend those millions of years pouring out ferocious ultraviolet radiation. The new results suggest that this pre-supernova phase, when the most massive stars are alive and blazing but have not yet detonated, is when a galaxy is at its leakiest. Once the explosions begin, the winds they drive mix neutral gas and dust into the surroundings, and the escape routes clog up.

This also resolves a puzzle that had been nagging at theorists. In detailed simulations, researchers had noticed that ionizing light seemed to escape most readily in the first few million years of star formation — exactly what the new Hubble observations now confirm. The observations and the latest generation of simulations are converging on the same answer: the window that matters is narrow, early, and supernova-free.

There is an important caveat, and the authors are careful to state it. Their galaxies are nearby analogues, not actual reionization-era galaxies. The physics should be the same — hydrogen is hydrogen, whether it sits in a galaxy 50 million light-years away or 13 billion — but an analogue is still an analogue. The team is already working to secure more observing time with Hubble to gather additional evidence, and they note that the results can immediately inform the simulations that astronomers use to model reionization. Smaller-scale winds associated with stellar birth regions could still play a role in the early escape phase, and the authors say more observations will be needed to test that idea.

## A Hubble renaissance

There is a pleasing irony in the fact that this result comes from Hubble rather than the much newer James Webb Space Telescope. Webb has dominated headlines for its views of the early universe — and rightly so — but some of the most incisive work on the physics of escaping radiation is still being done in ultraviolet light, a regime where Hubble remains king. The Cosmic Origins Spectrograph aboard Hubble can measure the exact absorption fingerprints of gas moving at different speeds, a capability that was central to this study.

The result also shows how much mileage remains in the "local analogue" strategy. Because we cannot see escaping ultraviolet light from reionization-era galaxies directly — the intergalactic medium itself absorbs it — the nearby universe is our only laboratory for the physics of photon escape. Every improvement in our understanding of how light leaks out of a galaxy at cosmic noon or in the present day feeds directly into our picture of the first billion years.

It also underlines why ultraviolet astronomy remains irreplaceable. The ionizing photons that drove reionization are, by definition, ultraviolet — shorter in wavelength than visible light — and Earth's atmosphere blocks them entirely, so only space telescopes can study them. Hubble's Cosmic Origins Spectrograph excels precisely here: it can split a galaxy's ultraviolet light into a spectrum fine enough to measure the velocity of gas from the tiny shifts in absorption-line positions, the very technique Carr's team used to clock the galactic winds. Even in the era of Webb's infrared supremacy, some of the sharpest tests of early-universe physics still come from Hubble's ultraviolet eye, trained on galaxies close enough to dissect star by star-forming star.

And that picture is getting sharper. Over the past year, astronomers have watched galaxies grow, merge, and light up the cosmos in ever more detail. Studies of the most distant supernovae, of ancient quasars, of the first generations of stars — all of these are threads in the same tapestry. The Carr team's contribution is to tug on one particular thread — the role of supernovae — and find that it was woven in the wrong direction.

## What happens next

The immediate next step is more data. Carr and his colleagues want more Hubble time to expand their sample of compact starbursts and firm up the relationship between wind speed and escape fraction. If the pattern holds across a larger sample, the implication for simulations is unavoidable: modelers will need to pay far more attention to what young stars do in the first few million years of a starburst's life, before supernovae enter the picture.

Longer term, the new generation of giant ground-based telescopes — including the Extremely Large Telescope, for which Carr is a fellow at the University of Michigan — will be able to dissect the spectra of distant galaxies in finer detail than ever before, testing whether the analogue physics the team has uncovered truly matches what happened in the universe's first billion years.

It is worth stepping back to admire the sweep of the story. About 13 billion years ago, the universe was dark and fogged with hydrogen. Then galaxies lit up, and their ultraviolet light — escaping through channels carved not by explosions, but by the fierce radiation of young stars in their first few million years — ionized the entire cosmos. The supernovae came later, and instead of helping, they stirred up the surroundings and trapped what light remained.

Reionization, Carr reminds us, was the last major phase transition the universe ever underwent. The cosmos went from opaque to transparent, from dark to light, and it has stayed that way ever since. We now know a little more about how that happened — and, just as importantly, a leading idea about how it *didn't*. In science, learning that the obvious answer is wrong is often the most exciting result of all. The universe's first light slipped out through a window that closed almost as soon as it opened, and the explosions everyone thought had held the door were, in the end, the ones slamming it shut.
