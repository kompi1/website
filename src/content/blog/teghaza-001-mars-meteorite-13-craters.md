A rock that spent years sitting unrecognised in the Sahara Desert has turned out to be one of the oldest pieces of Mars ever to reach Earth, and scientists have now narrowed its possible birthplace to just 13 places on the Red Planet. The meteorite, named Teghaza 001, formed more than 4.1 billion years ago, when Mars was a young world still assembling its crust and when water was already changing rocks beneath its surface. A study published in the journal Science on October 29, 2026, describes the rock as a sample of Mars' early crust and mantle, and NASA released a global map the same day showing the 13 locations whose mineralogy and chemistry match the meteorite.

That combination — an extremely old rock, a detailed laboratory analysis, and a planet-wide comparison from orbit — is what makes Teghaza 001 more than a curiosity. Most Martian meteorites are much younger, and most rocks on Mars itself have never been touched by a human hand or a laboratory instrument. Teghaza 001 therefore functions as a free sample return: a fragment of ancient Mars, delivered to Earth by a cosmic collision, that can be studied grain by grain. The new work does not claim to have found the exact crater that launched it. NASA is explicit on that point, noting that scientists can never know for sure where a Martian meteorite originated. What the team has done is turn a whole planet of possibilities into a shortlist, and in doing so, it has pointed future research at the ancient southern highlands, the battered, cratered terrain that preserves the earliest chapters of Martian history.

## A rose-coloured rock from the Sahara

Teghaza 001 was found in the Sahara Desert in Mali in 2022. It arrived on Earth not as a single dramatic stone but as eight stones with a combined mass of nearly 800 grams, less than a kilogram of rock in total. It takes its name from the salt plain in Mali where it was found, following the naming convention for meteorites recovered in deserts, where dry conditions preserve fallen space rocks and make them easier to spot against sand and gravel.

The rock is rose-coloured, and at first glance it did not advertise its age or its origin. Researchers at the University of New Mexico confirmed that it came from Mars by measuring its oxygen isotopes, the slightly different weights of oxygen atoms that act as a signature for where a rock formed. Oxygen isotope ratios differ between planetary bodies because each body formed from different mixtures of material and followed a different thermal and chemical history. For a suspected Martian meteorite, that fingerprint is the first decisive test, separating a genuine visitor from Mars from an Earth rock that merely looks unusual.

Once the Martian origin was established, the question became how old the rock is and what it records. The lead author of the new study is Yang Liu of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Liu has described how the rock's significance only became clear on close inspection. “It was only after we started looking closely that we realized this meteorite is really unique,” Liu said, according to coverage of the study. That understatement captures a common pattern in meteorite science: a stone found by collectors in a desert can sit for months or years before laboratory work reveals that it is not merely old, but among the oldest of its kind.

Teghaza 001 is one of around 435 meteorites from Mars known to have been found on Earth. That total sounds large until it is set against the scale of the problem. Mars has a surface area of about 144 million square kilometres, and the meteorites that reach Earth are a random, tiny sample of its crust, biased toward rocks strong enough to survive ejection, space travel, and atmospheric entry. Among those roughly 435, only a handful are as ancient as Teghaza 001. The new paper's title states the central claim plainly: “A Martian meteorite that formed ≳4.1 billion years ago samples Mars' early crust and mantle.” In other words, the rock is not just old in the casual sense. It dates from the first few hundred million years after Mars itself formed, a period for which direct samples are vanishingly rare.

## How scientists dated a rock older than most of Earth's crust

Dating a rock that is more than 4.1 billion years old requires clocks that run on geological timescales. The Teghaza 001 team used tiny zircon crystals inside the rock, dating them by measuring uranium and the lead it decays into. Zircon is prized by geochronologists because it is exceptionally durable and because it incorporates uranium when it crystallises but largely excludes lead, so the lead found inside it later must mostly have come from radioactive decay. Measuring the ratio of uranium to lead therefore works like a clock that starts when the crystal forms. When that clock was read for Teghaza 001, it pointed to a formation age of at least 4.1 billion years.

The team also used a newer technique to cross-check the story. A co-author from Caltech, Francois Tissot, helped apply a new in-situ rubidium-strontium dating method to a piece of the meteorite smaller than a fingernail. Rubidium-strontium dating exploits the slow decay of rubidium-87 into strontium-87, and an in-situ method allows researchers to date minerals in place, grain by grain, rather than dissolving a larger sample. For a meteorite that weighs less than a kilogram in total, and for a scientific community that must preserve material for future instruments that do not yet exist, working on a fingernail-sized fragment is not a detail. It is a discipline: every destructive analysis must be justified, and the most informative techniques are those that extract the maximum history from the minimum material.

Together, the dating work establishes Teghaza 001 as a witness to early Mars. Four point one billion years ago, the solar system was still in its violent youth. The giant planets had largely formed, but the inner planets were still being battered by leftover debris, their surfaces were still cooling, and their atmospheres and water cycles were still taking shape. On Earth, rocks of comparable age are extraordinarily rare, because plate tectonics, erosion, and later geological activity have recycled or destroyed most of the earliest crust. On Mars, where large-scale plate tectonics never took hold in the same way, ancient crust survived longer — but until a sample like Teghaza 001 is studied, that survival is an inference from orbit, not a measurement in a laboratory.

## Water had already changed the rock 4.1 billion years ago

Age alone would make Teghaza 001 interesting. What makes it scientifically valuable is what happened to it after it formed. The researchers found signs that water had changed the rock around 4.1 billion years ago. That alteration did not happen on Earth, after the meteorite fell in the Sahara. It happened on Mars, below the surface, when water interacted with the rock's minerals and left its chemical signature behind.

This distinction matters because Mars today is cold and dry on its surface, and its thin atmosphere cannot sustain liquid water in the open for long. Evidence for water in the distant past therefore has to be read from minerals that formed in contact with water, from sedimentary structures, and from chemistry that only makes sense if water was present. Teghaza 001 contributes a new, precisely dated data point to that record. It shows that water was not merely present somewhere on early Mars in a vague sense. It was actively altering crustal rock, beneath the surface, at a time when the planet's crust was still young.

The rock's mineralogy tells the other half of the story. Teghaza 001 carries a mix of igneous minerals that records crust forming from cooling magma, followed by alteration by water below the surface. That sequence — magma cools, crystals form, water moves through fractures and pore spaces, minerals change — is the basic biography of a planetary crust. On Earth, similar processes built the continents and concentrated the elements that later made life possible. On Mars, the same processes ran their course on a planet that would eventually lose much of its atmosphere and surface water, freezing its early history in place rather than overwriting it.

Lee Saper, the second author of the study, has described Teghaza 001 as the first piece of Martian crust from a part of Mars never sampled before, according to NASA coverage of the research. That phrasing should be read carefully. It does not mean scientists have pinpointed the exact outcrop. It means the rock's combination of age, chemistry, and mineralogy does not match the other Martian meteorites already in collections. It is a new kind of sample, from a terrain type that had not previously been represented in the laboratory. For a field that depends on a few hundred random samples to reconstruct an entire planet, a new type of sample is a significant expansion of the evidence base.

It is equally important to state what Teghaza 001 is not. The study does not claim evidence of life, and nothing in the new analysis should be read as a fossil or a biosignature. Water alteration is a necessary condition for life as we know it, but it is not evidence that life existed. The rock's importance is geological and chemical: it constrains when water was active, what the early crust was made of, and how the planet's interior and surface interacted. Those constraints, in turn, define the environments in which any search for ancient Martian life must operate.

## Thirteen white circles on a global map

The most visually striking product of the new research is NASA's map, catalogued as PIA26802 and published on October 29, 2026, with credits to NASA, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Caltech, and the United States Geological Survey. The base map was produced with data from the Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter instrument aboard NASA's Mars Global Surveyor mission, which measured the planet's topography in extraordinary detail. Onto that elevation map, the team plotted 13 locations, marked with white circles, where orbital data show mineralogy and chemistry similar to Teghaza 001.

The comparison was made possible by NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, which has spent two decades observing Mars from orbit with spectrometers and cameras that can identify minerals from their spectral fingerprints. The study's authors compared the meteorite's potassium, thorium, silicon, and iron with remote-sensing observations of the Martian surface to find possible source regions. Potassium and thorium are particularly useful because they are measured by gamma-ray and neutron spectrometers from orbit and because their abundances vary with rock type. Silicon and iron anchor the comparison in the bulk composition of the rock. When all four elements, together with the broader mineralogy, line up in the same place, that place becomes a candidate.

All 13 candidate locations lie in the ancient southern highlands. That is not a coincidence, and it is not a trivial result. The southern highlands are the oldest, most heavily cratered terrain on Mars, standing in stark contrast to the smoother, younger northern lowlands. The boundary between them, the Martian dichotomy, is one of the planet's defining features, and its origin remains debated. If Teghaza 001 came from the southern highlands, then it came from the terrain that best preserves the Noachian period, the earliest era of Martian geological history, when heavy bombardment, volcanic activity, and water erosion were all shaping the surface.

Reports of the study note that the leading match lies in the Noachian southern highlands, particularly northwest of Hellas impact crater. Hellas is one of the largest impact basins in the solar system, a scar so vast that it influences regional geology, atmospheric circulation, and the distribution of water ice. A source northwest of Hellas would place Teghaza 001 in terrain that has been battered, buried, exhumed, and altered over billions of years — exactly the kind of terrain that could preserve an ancient crustal rock and then, much later, launch it into space.

But the map must not be over-read. Thirteen candidates is a shortlist, not an identification. Orbiters measure the surface from hundreds of kilometres away, averaging over areas far larger than a single outcrop, and surface dust, later lava flows, and impact gardening can mask or mimic the chemistry of the underlying rock. Two different outcrops can share similar potassium, thorium, silicon, and iron abundances for different geological reasons. NASA's own description of the map is deliberately modest: scientists can never know for sure where a Martian meteorite originated, but Teghaza 001 includes clues that link it to the southern highlands. The white circles are therefore best understood as places where the orbital evidence is consistent with the laboratory evidence — places worth investigating further, not places proven to be the source.

## How a rock leaves Mars and lands in the Sahara

To appreciate why the source can be narrowed but never proven, it helps to follow the journey a Martian meteorite must survive. First, an asteroid or comet must strike Mars with enough energy to accelerate surface rock beyond the planet's escape velocity, about five kilometres per second. Most impact debris falls back to the surface. A tiny fraction is thrown fast enough, and at a steep enough angle, to escape Mars altogether. That debris then orbits the Sun, sometimes for millions of years, perturbed by gravity and collisions, until a small subset happens to intersect Earth's orbit and survive entry through Earth's atmosphere.

Each step erases information. The impact that launches the rock shocks its minerals, creating features such as shocked plagioclase, a mineral transformed by extreme pressure. The journey through space exposes the rock to cosmic rays, which can be used to estimate how long it travelled, but not where it started. Atmospheric entry melts the outer surface, forming a fusion crust, and the fall itself can shatter the rock into multiple stones, as happened with Teghaza 001's eight fragments. By the time a meteorite is found in a desert, the only record of its origin is the chemistry and mineralogy locked inside it, plus whatever orbital data can be matched to that chemistry.

That is why the Teghaza 001 result is methodologically interesting beyond Mars. It demonstrates a workflow that can be applied to other meteorites: date the rock precisely in the laboratory, measure its chemistry in detail, and then search orbital datasets for matching terrain. As orbital datasets improve — higher spatial resolution, better spectral coverage, more complete global maps — the shortlists for other meteorites should also shrink. Conversely, as laboratory techniques improve, including the in-situ rubidium-strontium method used for Teghaza 001, the chemical fingerprints themselves will become sharper, making the orbital match more discriminating.

A separate paper published in Science Advances in June examined the meteorite's water content, and together the two studies show how water shaped the young planet. The pairing is a reminder that a single meteorite can support multiple lines of research, each paper answering a different question from the same small mass of rock. The June work addressed how much water the rock contains and in what form; the October Science paper addresses when the rock formed, what it is made of, and where it might have come from. Future papers may address still other questions, from the rock's magnetic history to the timing of the impact that launched it, as long as material remains for analysis.

## Why the southern highlands matter for early Mars

The southern highlands are not merely old. They are the archive of Mars before it became the cold desert we see today. Their surfaces record valley networks carved by flowing water, lake basins that once held standing water, clay minerals that form only in the presence of water, and impact craters that have been degraded, filled, and exhumed over billions of years. The northern lowlands, by contrast, are smoother and younger, possibly resurfaced by lava, sediments, or even an ancient ocean, and they preserve far less of the earliest record.

Teghaza 001 adds a laboratory sample to that orbital archive. Orbital data can identify clay minerals and measure elemental abundances, but it cannot date a rock, examine its microscopic textures, or measure isotopic ratios with laboratory precision. A meteorite bridges that gap. It allows scientists to calibrate what orbital instruments are seeing: if a particular spectral signature from orbit corresponds to a rock of known age and known water-alteration history in the laboratory, then similar signatures elsewhere on Mars can be interpreted with greater confidence.

The timing is also significant. Water alteration around 4.1 billion years ago places Teghaza 001 in a period when Mars was transitioning from its earliest, most violent phase toward the more familiar Noachian landscape of rivers and lakes. Understanding that transition is central to understanding whether Mars was ever habitable for long enough, and in the right places, for life to arise. A planet can have water briefly, in catastrophic floods, without ever developing stable, long-lived habitats. It can also have water persistently, in subsurface aquifers and hydrothermal systems, long after surface conditions deteriorate. Teghaza 001's subsurface water alteration points toward the second possibility, at least locally and at least early in Martian history.

That finding connects, in a broader sense, to the missions currently exploring Mars. <a href="/blog/perseverance-mars-driving-record-45km/">Perseverance Just Broke the All-Time Off-World Driving Record: 45 Kilometers on Mars</a> follows a rover that is collecting samples from Jezero Crater, an ancient lake basin, for eventual return to Earth. Those samples, if they are returned, will provide the same kind of laboratory precision as Teghaza 001, but from a known location, with full geological context. The contrast is instructive: Perseverance's samples will be valuable precisely because their origin is known, while Teghaza 001 is valuable despite its origin being uncertain, because it samples a type of terrain and an age that no rover has yet visited.

The connection also runs in the other direction. <a href="/blog/mars-sample-return-cancelled-china-race/">NASA Killed Mars Sample Return. China Is Coming for the Rocks Instead.</a> examines the uncertain future of bringing Mars samples back to Earth through a dedicated mission, and the competing plans to do so. In that context, meteorites like Teghaza 001 are a reminder that nature has already been running a sample-return programme for billions of years, albeit a random and uncontrolled one. The challenge for scientists is to read those natural samples as carefully as they would read a sample collected by a rover, and to use them to guide where dedicated missions should go next.

## What remains unknown, and why that is honest science

The Teghaza 001 story is, in its final form, a story about limits. Scientists have narrowed the rock's origin to 13 places, but they have not identified one. They have dated its formation to more than 4.1 billion years ago, but they have not dated the impact that launched it from Mars, nor determined how long it spent in space before falling to Earth. They have shown that water altered the rock on Mars, but they have not reconstructed the full water cycle that produced that alteration, nor determined whether the water was part of a regional aquifer, a hydrothermal system, or a transient event.

Those unknowns are not failures. They are the reason the 13-site shortlist matters. Each candidate site can now be examined more closely with existing orbital data, and future orbital observations can be targeted at the sites that best explain the rock's chemistry. Researchers have not yet said whether the 13 sites can be told apart from one another, or which features at each site best explain the rock's composition. Those are open questions for the next round of work, and they will require both better orbital data and, potentially, more laboratory analysis of the remaining Teghaza 001 material.

There is also a preservation question. Teghaza 001 weighs less than a kilogram, and every analysis consumes or alters some material. The scientific community must balance the desire to answer questions now against the obligation to preserve material for future instruments, which will inevitably be more sensitive and more precise than today's. The use of a fingernail-sized fragment for rubidium-strontium dating is a model for that balance: extract a decisive measurement from a minimal sample, publish the result, and leave the rest for the next generation of questions.

The same discipline applies to interpretation. It would be easy, and wrong, to present the 13 white circles as 13 craters that have been proven to be the source, or to imply that one of them must be the answer in a way that overstates the evidence. The careful claim is narrower and more durable: the rock's chemistry and mineralogy are consistent with those 13 locations, all in the southern highlands, and inconsistent, on current evidence, with the rest of the planet. That is a genuine advance. It transforms a question that once had a planet-sized answer into a question with 13 testable candidates.

## A new tool for reading other fallen rocks

Beyond Mars, the Teghaza 001 workflow has implications for how scientists study meteorites from the Moon, from asteroids, and from Mars itself. Meteorites are the only samples most planetary scientists will ever hold from other worlds, at least until sample-return missions become routine. Learning to extract origin information from them — age, chemistry, alteration history, and a plausible source region — multiplies the value of every meteorite in every collection.

The method depends on two kinds of data improving together. Orbital datasets must become more detailed, so that surface chemistry can be mapped at finer scales and matched to laboratory samples with less ambiguity. Laboratory techniques must become more precise and less destructive, so that rare samples can be dated and characterised without being consumed. Teghaza 001 sits at the intersection of both trends: a new dating method applied to a tiny fragment, matched against two decades of Mars orbital data, producing a map that would have been impossible a generation ago.

It also illustrates the value of desert meteorite recovery. Deserts like the Sahara preserve meteorites because they are dry, sparsely vegetated, and geologically stable, and because dark meteorites stand out against light sand. Collectors, researchers, and institutions all play a role in that recovery chain, and the scientific value of a find often depends on its provenance being recorded: where it was found, in how many pieces, and in what condition. Teghaza 001's provenance — Mali, 2022, eight stones, nearly 800 grams — is part of its scientific record, not merely its backstory.

For the public, the appeal of Teghaza 001 is simpler and no less valid. Here is a rock, found in a desert on Earth, that formed on another planet before most of Earth's own crust existed, was changed by water on that planet, was blasted into space by an impact, travelled through the solar system, fell through Earth's atmosphere, and waited in the Sahara until someone picked it up. That journey, reconstructed from isotopes, zircons, and orbital maps, is a reminder of how interconnected the solar system is, and how much history can be carried in less than a kilogram of stone.

## The road ahead for Mars science

The next steps for Teghaza 001 research will likely be incremental rather than dramatic, and that is appropriate. Orbital scientists can revisit the 13 candidate regions with existing Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter data, looking for finer distinctions in mineralogy or for geological features, such as impact ejecta or exposed bedrock, that might favour one candidate over another. Laboratory scientists can analyse additional grains from the remaining material, refining the age, the water-alteration history, and the rock's relationship to Mars' mantle. Neither effort will, by itself, produce a single definitive crater, and that should not be presented as a disappointment. Science advances by narrowing uncertainty, and Teghaza 001 has narrowed it considerably.

The work also sharpens the questions for future Mars exploration. If the southern highlands hold the key to Mars' earliest crust, then missions that can access ancient highland terrain, sample it in context, and return it to Earth will be especially valuable. Rover missions, helicopter scouts, and orbital instruments each contribute a different piece: rovers provide context and samples from known locations, helicopters extend the reach of rovers across difficult terrain, and orbiters provide the global maps that link local observations to planetary history. <a href="/blog/nasa-roamx-0201-mars-helicopter-blade-design/">NASA's Radical New Mars Helicopter Blades Could Fly 27 Times Farther Than Ingenuity</a> explores one such extension, a rotor design intended to let future Mars helicopters cover far greater distances than Ingenuity ever did. A helicopter that can scout highland terrain, or ferry samples across it, would change what kinds of ancient rocks become accessible to rovers and, eventually, to laboratories on Earth.

In the meantime, Teghaza 001 will remain in collections, available for re-examination as new questions arise. That is the quiet power of meteorites: they do not expire. A rock studied today with one technique can be studied tomorrow with another, and a conclusion drawn today can be tested against a measurement made a decade from now. The 13 white circles on NASA's map are therefore not a final answer but an invitation — to look again at the southern highlands, to ask which of those places best matches a rose-coloured rock from the Sahara, and to remember that the oldest stories Mars has to tell may already be sitting, unrecognised, in a desert on Earth, waiting for someone to look closely enough to realise how unique they really are.
