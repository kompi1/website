Five hundred and thirty-eight million light-years from Earth, a black hole roughly 800 million times the mass of the Sun is firing a jet of plasma across space at almost the speed of light. The jet is aimed, broadly, at us. That much astronomers have known for decades: 3C 279 is a blazar, a galaxy whose central engine points its beam almost directly down our line of sight, which is why it is one of the brightest objects in the radio sky. What they did not know, until now, is what the jet does in the first fraction of a light-year after it leaves the black hole — the innermost region where the plasma is born, accelerated, and shaped. New images from the Event Horizon Telescope have now resolved that region in unprecedented detail, and the picture is stranger than anyone expected. The jet does not shoot straight out of the black hole. It bends toward us.

The result, described in a paper published in the journal *Astronomy & Astrophysics* and reported in late November 2026, comes from observations the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) made in April 2021. On three separate occasions that month, the planet-spanning network of radio telescopes turned its combined gaze on 3C 279 at a frequency of 230 gigahertz, the same millimetre-wave band that produced the famous first image of a black hole in 2019. The 2021 campaign was the largest EHT array configuration published to date, and the new images it produced show the jet's dynamics and geometry at sub-parsec scales — distances smaller than a single light-year from the black hole itself.

The headline finding is a matter of geometry with profound consequences. The reconstructed images consistently reveal a compact core elongated almost north to south, nearly perpendicular to the jet's large-scale axis. That shape is exactly what astronomers would expect to see if the jet starts out pointing almost directly at Earth and then bends — the same way a road that runs straight toward you looks compressed, foreshortened, and oddly wide when you look along it. The team considers a faint spot at the northern edge of this elongated structure to be the base of the jet, close to the black hole, and every measured velocity in the study depends on that identification.

"Because the plasma is moving close to the speed of light, its radiation is concentrated in the direction it's traveling," the study's lead author, Hendrik Müller, a staff scientist in the Data Management and Software group at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory (NRAO) and formerly a researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy, told Universe Today by email. "When that direction lines up more closely with Earth, we see brighter emission. At the same time, features in the jet are moving more nearly straight toward us, so they can appear to move more slowly across the sky. Think of a car coming almost head-on: it can be moving fast while barely shifting left or right in your field of view."

That single insight — the bend — elegantly explains two puzzles that have long surrounded 3C 279. Farther along the inner jet, astronomers see brighter emission and slower apparent motion. Both effects can result from a change in direction, even if the plasma itself keeps moving just as fast. And the brightest spot in the image is not at the base of the jet, next to the black hole, but downstream, where the jet points most directly at us. A shock wave where the fast flow is compressed most likely also contributes to the brightness at the bend, the authors suggest.

## A blazar, and why it matters

To understand why this result matters, it helps to know what a blazar is. At the centre of 3C 279 sits an active galactic nucleus: a supermassive black hole surrounded by a swirling disk of gas that feeds it, and by magnetic fields that channel some of the infalling material into twin jets of ionised plasma, accelerated to within a whisker of the speed of light. In most active galaxies, those jets point off to the side, and we see them from the flank. In a blazar, one jet points almost straight at Earth. The radiation from plasma racing toward us is boosted by relativistic beaming — concentrated and amplified along its direction of travel — so the object appears enormously bright, and its light varies on short timescales. 3C 279 is so bright at radio wavelengths that it is routinely used as a calibration source for very long baseline interferometry (VLBI), the technique that links telescopes across continents into a single virtual instrument.

The black hole powering it is a monster by any measure. At roughly 800 million solar masses, it outweighs the supermassive black hole at the centre of our own Milky Way, Sagittarius A*, by a factor of nearly two hundred; Sagittarius A* weighs in at about 4.3 million solar masses. Yet 3C 279's engine is not the biggest known, nor the closest. What makes it special is its orientation. Because the jet points at us, astronomers get a front-row view of the most extreme physics in the universe — plasma moving at 99.5 percent of the speed of light, magnetic fields twisted by a spinning black hole, and shock waves that can outshine entire galaxies. The price of that front-row seat is that the geometry is fiendishly hard to untangle: when something moves almost directly toward you, its motion across the sky is tiny, and small changes in angle produce huge changes in apparent brightness and speed.

That is precisely the trap the new study springs. For years, observations of 3C 279 at larger scales showed features that appeared to move at many times the speed of light — apparent superluminal motion, a well-known illusion of relativity — while other features seemed to crawl. The new EHT images, which probe scales a thousand times smaller than the familiar radio maps, show that the explanation may be simpler, and more elegant, than a complicated pattern of acceleration and deceleration. The jet is not speeding up and slowing down. It is turning.

## The largest EHT array ever published

The clarity of the new images is no accident. It is the payoff of a bigger, better telescope network. The 2021 campaign added two new stations to the EHT for the first time: a 12-metre radio telescope at Kitt Peak Observatory in Arizona, and the Northern Extended Millimeter Array (NOEMA) interferometer in the French Alps. Both filled a crucial gap. In interferometry, the distance between two antennas — the baseline — determines which details the array can resolve. Long baselines give fine resolution but miss broad structure; short baselines capture the big picture but blur the fine detail. Kitt Peak and NOEMA provided intermediate baselines that had been missing, significantly improving the array's coverage at the scales in between.

"The physical baseline between two antennas in the array determines the spatial scale that this baseline can resolve," Müller explained. "That is, for example, the reason why the Very Large Array in New Mexico is built on railway tracks: when you move the antennas closer to each other, you zoom out of the image and see the larger field of view. If you move them closer to each other, you zoom into the image, achieve higher resolution, but see only a small view of the sky. The locations of the radio telescopes in the EHT could of course not be changed, but Kitt Peak and NOEMA were important because they are relatively close to other telescopes in the array — NOEMA in the French Alps to Pico Veleta close to Granada, Spain. They added information that was not available to us in previous years."

The EHT itself is a collaboration that keeps growing. It produced the first image of a black hole — the supermassive black hole at the heart of the galaxy M87, known as M87* — in 2019, and the first image of Sagittarius A* in 2022. <a href="/blog/m87-black-hole-image-confirmed-2026/">M87 Black Hole Image Confirmed 2026: An AI That Never Saw a Black Hole Found the Same Ring</a> describes how an independent analysis later confirmed that famous ring. Between 2024 and 2026, the array also produced detailed images of the relativistic jets emanating from the galaxies 3C 84 and M87*. The Greenland Telescope joined the network in 2018. Each addition has sharpened the array's vision, and the 2021 campaign on 3C 279 is the largest configuration the collaboration has published so far.

Turning the raw signals from a dozen telescopes on three continents into an image is itself a formidable task. The team processed the data with four different imaging software packages — Comrade, DoG-HiT, kine, and Difmap — a strategy similar to the one used to reconstruct the famous M87 black hole image in 2019. Each package makes different assumptions about the image, so agreement between them builds confidence that a feature is real rather than an artefact of one particular method. The elongated core, the bend, and the downstream bright spot survived that cross-check: they recur across multiple epochs and across frequencies from 22 to 230 gigahertz, which the authors take as evidence that the morphology is intrinsic to the source rather than an imaging artefact.

## Faster than light — apparently

The numbers the team extracted from the images are extreme even by the standards of black hole astrophysics. Geometric model fitting identified several components in the jet with apparent speeds of up to around ten times the speed of light. Nothing, of course, is actually moving faster than light. Apparent superluminal motion is a geometric illusion: when plasma moves toward an observer at nearly the speed of light, the light it emits later has a shorter distance to travel, so the object appears to cross the sky faster than it really does. The measured apparent speed of about 10c requires the plasma's true bulk motion to have a Lorentz factor greater than about 10.3 — a measure of how strongly relativistic time dilation and length contraction are at work — and constrains the viewing angle to less than about one degree. In other words, the jet is pointed at us with an accuracy that would be remarkable if it were deliberate. It is not deliberate. It is the reason 3C 279 is a blazar at all.

The brightness temperatures the team measured are systematically low, around 10^9 to 10^10 kelvin in the rest frame, consistent with optically thin emission at 230 gigahertz — the plasma is transparent enough at that frequency for the telescope to see through it, rather than being blocked by its own glow. That transparency is part of what makes the millimetre band so valuable: it lets the EHT peer closer to the black hole than longer radio wavelengths can.

Perhaps the most telling detail is where the speed is found. Even close to the apparent base of the jet, some features are already moving across the sky at apparent speeds comparable to those much farther downstream. "We also found a clue about where the jet picks up speed," Müller said. "Even close to the apparent base of the jet, some features are already moving across the sky at apparent speeds comparable to those much farther downstream. If those features travel with the plasma, that suggests the jet gains much of its speed over a short distance near its base. As the fast flow narrows again, it may form a shock that adds to the brightness we see near the bend."

That is a significant clue to one of the oldest questions in the field: how does a black hole launch a jet, and how does that jet accelerate to nearly the speed of light? The new images suggest the acceleration happens quickly, close to the black hole, rather than gradually over long distances. The jet emerges already fast, then bends, and the bend — not a change in speed — is what makes it look brighter and slower farther out. Other jets are teaching the same lesson from a different angle: <a href="/blog/3c-120-black-hole-jet-2026-xrism-hot-inner-flow/">3C 120 Black Hole Jet 2026: XRISM Finds the Hot Inner Flow That Launches It</a> reports on X-ray observations that traced the hot gas feeding a jet in a neighbouring galaxy.

## A jet that changes direction

The images also show that the inner jet's direction changes over time. Compared with earlier observations from 2011 and 2017, the region closest to the base has turned noticeably, although the jet remains straight farther out. The bend, in other words, is not a fixed kink in a rigid pipe. It is a living structure, shifting on timescales of years.

The authors cannot yet explain why the jet bends, or why the bend moves. They list four candidate causes, each of which would tell a different story about the environment around the black hole. The jet could be interacting with the surrounding interstellar gas, ploughing into material that deflects it. It could be suffering instabilities within the flow itself — kink or Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities, the same kinds of wobbles that make a fast stream of water break into ripples and curls. The magnetic field near the event horizon could be reconnecting, releasing energy and redirecting the flow. Or the jet's axis could be wobbling because a second black hole is orbiting the first, its gravity tugging the jet around in a slow precession. A binary supermassive black hole at the heart of 3C 279 is a real possibility: many galaxies are built from mergers, and their central black holes can spend millions of years spiralling toward each other before they collide.

Distinguishing between these explanations will take more data, and the team is already working on it. "This paper is just the beginning," Müller said. "We have EHT observations from several years, as well as observations at other frequencies that we scheduled around the same time, that are currently being analysed. Observing at different frequencies lets us see different parts of the jet. Taking those observations close together helps us tell which differences come from the frequency we're using and which come from the jet itself changing. "We expect a series of results from these data over the next few years. Very few quasars have been studied in this much detail. That makes 3C 279 one of our best opportunities to understand how a black hole launches a jet and how that jet gets up to nearly the speed of light, and the current manuscript is just the beginning of that journey." Quasars remain among the most extreme objects in the sky: <a href="/blog/euclid-quasar-j1504-6252-redshift-8-13-november-2026/">Euclid Quasar J1504+6252 Redshift 8.13: The Most Distant Quasar Ever Seen, 630 Million Years After the Big Bang</a> covers a quasar seen when the universe was barely 630 million years old."

The multi-frequency campaign matters because different radio frequencies probe different parts of the jet: lower frequencies see the broader, more extended structure, while the highest frequencies peer closest to the black hole. Observing at several frequencies at nearly the same time lets astronomers separate genuine changes in the jet from differences caused by looking at different layers of it. Future observations will also test whether the bend is stable, whether it migrates, and whether the bright spot at the bend flares or fades as the jet's direction shifts.

## Why 3C 279 is the perfect laboratory

3C 279 has been one of the best-studied quasars in radio astronomy for decades, and that long baseline of observations is part of what makes the new result so powerful. Astronomers have decades of data on how its jet behaves on large scales, but only a handful of images of how it behaves at sub-parsec scales. The EHT is closing that gap, one campaign at a time. The same collaboration that imaged the shadow of M87* and the ring around Sagittarius A* is now extending its programme from black hole shadows to the jets those black holes launch — the other half of the story of how black holes shape their galaxies.

That story matters far beyond one blazar. Jets are one of the main ways black holes feed energy back into their host galaxies, heating gas, suppressing star formation, and regulating the growth of the galaxy itself. A jet that bends, precesses, or wobbles will deposit its energy in a different place than a straight jet would, and over millions of years that difference can reshape a galaxy. Understanding the launch and the bend is therefore not a curiosity about a distant object. It is a piece of the puzzle of how galaxies — including, in the distant past, our own — grew to look the way they do.

The result also showcases a quieter theme in modern astronomy: the value of patience and of archives. The observations were made in April 2021. It took years of careful calibration, imaging with four independent software packages, and cross-checking across frequencies and epochs before the team was confident enough to publish. The payoff is a measurement at a scale no earlier instrument could reach: a jet, a fraction of a light-year wide, bending toward us from 538 million light-years away, its plasma already racing at 99.5 percent of the speed of light within a whisker of the black hole that made it.

There is a pleasing irony in the geometry. The EHT is an Earth-sized telescope, built by linking dishes across the planet. Its target is a jet that points almost exactly at Earth, along a line of sight less than a degree wide. The telescope and the jet are aligned with each other by chance, across half a billion light-years, and that alignment is what makes the measurement possible at all. A jet pointed a few degrees to either side would be a different object to us — dimmer, less variable, and far less informative. 3C 279 happens to be pointed at us, and we happen to have built the instrument that can see it. The new images are the result of that double coincidence, and they are only the beginning. The EHT has more years of 3C 279 data in hand, more frequencies to analyse, and more results to come. The jet will keep bending, and astronomers will keep watching to see where it points next.

## The EHT and 3C 279: a timeline

- **2011** — Earlier very long baseline observations image the inner jet of 3C 279 at larger scales, providing a baseline for later comparison.
- **2017** — Further observations capture the jet's inner structure; the region near the base will later be seen to have turned noticeably relative to this epoch.
- **2018** — The Greenland Telescope joins the Event Horizon Telescope network.
- **April 2019** — The EHT Collaboration releases the first image of a black hole, the shadow of M87*, in the galaxy Messier 87.
- **April 2021** — The EHT observes 3C 279 on three occasions at 230 GHz, in the largest array configuration published to date, with the 12-metre Kitt Peak telescope and the NOEMA interferometer joining for the first time.
- **May 2022** — The EHT releases the first image of Sagittarius A*, the supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way.
- **2024–2026** — The EHT produces detailed images of the relativistic jets of 3C 84 and M87*.
- **November 2026** — Müller and colleagues publish the 2021 3C 279 results in *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, showing the inner jet bending toward Earth at sub-parsec scales, with apparent speeds up to about 10 times the speed of light.
- **Next few years** — The team expects a series of further results from the 2021 data and from multi-frequency observations taken around the same time, testing what drives the bending.

## What comes next

The immediate future of this story is written in observing schedules and data pipelines rather than in the sky. The EHT team is analysing several more years of observations of 3C 279, taken at other frequencies and scheduled close together in time so that frequency effects can be separated from real changes in the jet. Those data should show whether the bend is a stable feature or a passing phase, whether the bright spot at the bend is a standing shock or a moving knot, and whether the inner jet's direction keeps drifting in the same sense or swings back.

Each of the four candidate explanations for the bending makes a different prediction. If the jet is ploughing into surrounding gas, the bend should track the density of the material it meets. If the flow is unstable, the bend should grow, ripple, and perhaps break into new structures. If magnetic reconnection near the horizon is responsible, the bending should be accompanied by flares. And if a second black hole is tugging the jet around, the wobble should be periodic — a slow, regular precession that repeats over years or decades. Long-term monitoring is the only way to tell, and 3C 279, bright and conveniently aimed at Earth, is the ideal target for it.

The wider lesson is about the EHT itself. The collaboration began with a single goal: to image the shadow of a black hole. It achieved that in 2019, and again in 2022, and in doing so it built an instrument capable of far more. The same array that resolved a ring of light around M87* can now trace a jet's geometry within a light-year of its launch point, in a galaxy more than half a billion light-years away. The jet of 3C 279 bends toward us, and the telescope that caught it bending is pointed, quite literally, at the same sky. It is a reminder that the most powerful instruments are the ones that keep finding new questions after they have answered the ones they were built for — and that a black hole's jet, seen head-on, still has surprises left.

Its jet is bending now, its plasma is racing at 99.5 percent of the speed of light now, and its bright spot is shining downstream of the black hole now — a lighthouse that happens to point at us across 538 million light-years of darkness. The EHT has shown us the innermost stretch of that beam for the first time. What it does next is a question for the years of data already in hand, and for the telescopes that will keep watching a jet that never stops turning toward Earth.
