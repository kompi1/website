For the busiest launch coast on Earth, silence is news. The Florida Space Coast, which recorded 109 launches in 2025 and grew accustomed to sonic booms several times a week, is now entering an unusual quiet stretch. After the Bandwagon-5 rideshare mission lifted off from Cape Canaveral on October 20, 2026, SpaceX will not launch another Falcon 9 from Florida for at least six weeks. The gap is not a grounding, not a stand-down, and not a sign of trouble. According to SpaceX itself, it is the clearest signal yet that Starship's Florida debut is close — and that the company is willing to let its workhorse rocket go quiet on one coast in order to prepare the ground for a much bigger one.

SpaceX Vice President of Launch Kiko Dontchev disclosed the gap on October 21, during a speaking engagement at the Cocoa Village Playhouse, as reported by Florida Today. Asked about the slowing Florida cadence, he did not hedge. "We have a six-week gap before the next Falcon 9" from Florida, he said. Then he framed the pause as a deliberate part of bringing Starship to Kennedy Space Center. "If you haven't seen a signal that it's 'game time' on Starship, that's it," Dontchev said. In a business that usually measures progress in launches per week, a senior launch executive publicly announcing six weeks without one is a remarkable statement of priorities. It says that, for now, preparing the future matters more than padding the present count.

The pause now has a scheduled end. The next Florida Falcon 9 on the schedule is the Cygnus CRS-2 NG-25 cargo flight to the International Space Station, listed for November 29, 2026, from Space Launch Complex 40 — the same pad Bandwagon-5 left on October 20. If that date holds, the gap lands almost exactly on the six weeks Dontchev described. And the Space Coast calendar is not entirely empty in the meantime: United Launch Alliance's Vulcan rocket is scheduled to launch Amazon Leo satellites on November 13, breaking the silence midway through the window. So the drought is real but no longer absolute. Two November launches — one ULA, one SpaceX — punctuate it, while the broader Falcon 9 cadence stays in California and Starship's Florida debut remains a next-year event. As with every date in this article, both are subject to change with weather, technical readiness, and range availability.

## The six-week gap, in numbers

To understand why six weeks without a Florida Falcon 9 counts as news, it helps to remember what normal looks like. The Space Coast recorded 109 launches in 2025, a pace of roughly two per week across all operators, with SpaceX's Falcon 9 accounting for the large majority. Residents plan their evenings around launch windows. Hotels near the Cape sell rooms on the promise of a visible ascent. Webcams point at pads that are rarely idle for long. A six-week Falcon 9 absence from Florida is therefore not a rounding error in the manifest. It is the longest deliberate quiet stretch for SpaceX's Florida operation in the Falcon 9 era's mature phase, and it arrives at a moment when the company's overall launch rate has never been higher.

The gap applies to SpaceX's two Florida Falcon 9 pads: Launch Complex 39A at NASA's Kennedy Space Center and Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station. Both are affected, which is what makes the pause a coast-wide phenomenon rather than a single pad's maintenance outage. A single pad can be down for refurbishment while the other carries the load; that is routine. Both pads going quiet for Falcon 9 at once, for six weeks, is a choice about where the company's people, hardware, and attention are going. The last Florida Falcon 9 before the pause was <a href="/blog/spacex-bandwagon-5-rideshare-launch/">SpaceX Bandwagon-5 rideshare launch</a>, which departed Space Launch Complex 40 on October 20. The next, if the schedule holds, is Cygnus NG-25 from that same pad on November 29.

That framing matters because it corrects the most common misunderstanding about the pause. SpaceX has not stopped launching. It has stopped launching Falcon 9s from Florida. The distinction is the whole story. Falcon 9 launches continue from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California, where the company's Starlink missions moved earlier this year, and Starship test flights continue from Starbase in South Texas. Anyone watching only the Florida sky will see an empty calendar. Anyone watching the company's full manifest will see a machine still running at full speed, just rearranged geographically while one coast is rebuilt for a bigger rocket.

## Why SpaceX is going quiet in Florida

The reason for the pause is construction, not failure. Starship launch infrastructure at Kennedy Space Center's Launch Complex 39A is, in Dontchev's words, "effectively complete at this point," with engineers applying lessons from SpaceX's newest Starship launch tower in Texas. Doors will soon be installed at Gigabay, the roughly 380-foot facility near Roberts Road at Kennedy designed for stacking and preparing Super Heavy boosters. Those are the visible last steps of a multi-year build-out: a second Starship launch site, on the East Coast, capable of supporting the high flight rates SpaceX says the program needs for Starlink deployment, lunar missions, and eventually Mars.

Starship is the largest rocket ever flown, more than 400 feet tall when fully stacked, and it has launched 14 times from Starbase in South Texas. Florida is next. But "next" does not mean "soon" in the sense of weeks. There is no firm date for a first Florida Starship launch. The next Starship flight on the schedule is Flight 15 from Texas, targeted for November, and Florida Starship launches are currently listed for next year. Florida Today reported that SpaceX hopes to launch Starship from Florida by the end of the year, but that is a hope, not a confirmed target: Launch Complex 39A still needs activation work and licensing. The honest reading of the schedule is that the six-week Falcon 9 pause is preparation, not prelude — the pad work comes first, and the first Florida Starship flight follows when the site, the hardware, and the licence are all ready.

The logistics of that first Florida Starship are themselves a story. The first Florida Starships will not be built in Florida. Crews will manufacture boosters and ships in Texas, then transport them horizontally aboard SpaceX's barge You'll Thank Me Later around the Florida peninsula to Port Canaveral and the Kennedy Turn Basin. Both the booster and the ship will visit Gigabay, be raised vertical, and then roll to the pad. It is a sea voyage of more than a thousand miles for a vehicle taller than a 30-storey building, and it is the kind of operation that only makes sense once a company has decided that its Texas factory is the single production line for a national launch system. Watching for that barge is, for space watchers, one of the three signals that the pause is ending. The others are a firm date appearing on SpaceX mission pages and visible final work at Gigabay and 39A.

There is also a strategic logic to pausing Falcon 9 in Florida that goes beyond pad construction. SpaceX has said the next Starlink launch from Florida will be on a Starship, carrying the larger next-generation satellites that the Falcon 9 cannot. That single sentence reframes the entire pause. Florida is not losing Starlink launches permanently; it is being reserved for a version of Starlink that only Starship can fly. The V3 satellites, larger and more capable than the current generation, are central to SpaceX's plan to grow the constellation's capacity, and launching them in bulk requires Starship's payload volume. In that light, six quiet weeks are the price of converting the Space Coast from a Falcon 9 port into a Starship port — a conversion that, if it works, ends with more launches, not fewer, and with bigger payloads on each one.

## The slowdown started earlier than October

The October 20 Bandwagon-5 flight was the last Florida Falcon 9, but the slowdown began well before it. Florida's Falcon 9 cadence has dwindled since August 25, when Starlink 10-49 was the last Starlink mission to launch from Florida. SpaceX has shifted Starlink launches entirely to California, and the rhythm of the Space Coast changed accordingly. Starlink missions were the metronome of the Florida manifest: frequent, predictable, and numerous. Removing them left a calendar dominated by occasional rideshares, commercial missions, and cargo flights — important missions, but not enough to sustain the several-times-a-week pace residents had grown used to.

That shift is worth pausing on, because it explains why the six-week gap feels so abrupt even though its causes accumulated over months. When a launch site's most frequent customer moves its flights elsewhere, the remaining manifest looks thin long before any formal pause is announced. Bandwagon-5, a rideshare mission carrying multiple small payloads, was a fitting final flight before the quiet: useful, commercial, and representative of the non-Starlink work that still uses Florida pads. After it, there was simply nothing left on the Florida Falcon 9 schedule until Cygnus NG-25. Dontchev's October 21 announcement did not create the gap; it named it, and gave it a reason.

The California side of the story deserves equal weight. Falcon 9 pads at Vandenberg Space Force Base remain fully operational and continue to fly Starlink and polar-orbit missions throughout the Florida pause. The <a href="/blog/spacex-vandenberg-50th-launch-surge/">SpaceX Vandenberg 50th launch surge</a> earlier in the year showed how thoroughly the West Coast operation has scaled, and the early-November manifest confirms it: a United States Space Force mission, USSF-432, was scheduled from Vandenberg in the first week of November, and a Starlink Group 15-21 mission carrying 27 satellites was set for November 5 from the same base. For satellite operators and for SpaceX's own constellation, nothing has stopped. The launches moved west, where the pads, the range, and the polar-orbit geometry suit the missions that dominate the current manifest.

This geographic reshuffle also clarifies what the pause is not. It is not a response to a Falcon 9 failure, and it is not a regulatory grounding. Falcon 9 remains the most-flown rocket in history, and its California operations continue without interruption. It is not a demand problem either: Starlink deployment, national-security missions, and commercial rideshares continue to fill the manifest. It is, in the plainest terms, a construction schedule colliding with a manifest schedule. The Florida pads need work, the California pads can absorb the load, and the company has chosen to let Florida go quiet rather than split its workforce and attention between flying Falcon 9s and finishing Starship infrastructure at the same site.

## What still flies: Texas, California, and the next Starship

While Florida waits, the rest of SpaceX's test and operational programme moves on. The next Starship flight from Texas, Flight 15, is targeted for November, and it carries an ambition that would have sounded fanciful a few years ago: SpaceX plans to go beyond static firings and attempt to catch both rocket stages. Media reports in early November described completed engine tests on Ship 42 ahead of Flight 15, including a burn run for the full intended duration and a simulated deorbit burn. The <a href="/blog/starship-flight-15-first-ship-catch-attempt/">Starship Flight 15 first ship catch attempt</a> would extend the tower-catch technique already demonstrated for the Super Heavy booster to the Ship upper stage itself — a manoeuvre that remains subject to regulatory approval and to the unforgiving physics of bringing a returning spacecraft back to a tower with precision measured in metres.

That context matters for the Florida story because it shows where SpaceX's engineering attention is concentrated during the pause. The company's most experienced Starship teams are in Texas, working through the Flight 15 test campaign. The Florida infrastructure team is finishing 39A and Gigabay. The Falcon 9 teams are in California, flying the operational manifest. It is a three-site division of labour, and the Florida pause is what makes it possible without overextending any one team. When Flight 15 flies from Texas, it will test the hardware and procedures that Florida will eventually inherit. When the first Starship arrives in Florida by barge, it will arrive at a site whose tower, tank farm, and processing facility have been finished without the distraction of a weekly Falcon 9 countdown.

The broader launch market is also moving during the pause, and Florida's quiet weeks are not quiet for the industry. SpaceX crossed its 700th Falcon 9 flight in early November, lofting 27 Starlink satellites from Vandenberg — a round number that says a great deal about industrial cadence. The company's third-quarter results, reported in the same week, showed revenue of about 14.6 billion dollars, a reminder that the launch business now sits inside a much larger enterprise spanning satellites, broadband service, and artificial intelligence infrastructure. None of that depends on a Florida Falcon 9 launching in a particular week. But all of it depends, eventually, on Starship working at scale — which is why a six-week pause in one state can be, paradoxically, a sign of confidence rather than caution.

## Who fills the gap: Vulcan returns on November 13

The Space Coast will not be entirely silent during the pause, thanks to a rocket that has itself been waiting. United Launch Alliance's Vulcan rocket is scheduled to return to flight on November 13, 2026, carrying Amazon Leo satellites from Space Launch Complex 41 at Cape Canaveral. The mission, designated LV-01 or Vulcan Leo 1, will carry 40 Amazon Leo broadband satellites into low Earth orbit, and it will be the first of 38 Vulcan flights designated to carry Amazon's satellites. It is also Vulcan's fifth flight since its debut in 2024, and its first since a solid rocket booster nozzle anomaly grounded the vehicle in February 2026 during a United States Space Force mission.

That grounding is part of why November 13 matters beyond the satellite count. Vulcan has been grounded for nine months following the February anomaly, during which military officials reassigned national-security flights to SpaceX and ULA worked through a nozzle redesign and testing programme. The return flight uses the heavy-lift VC6L configuration, with six Northrop Grumman GEM 63XL solid rocket boosters fitted with reinforced thermal nozzle insulation, alongside an optimised Centaur V upper stage. It will be the first mission to use all six of the rocket's available boosters, and the 40 satellites will fly inside a 5.4-metre payload fairing — reported as the single largest payload volume launched for the Amazon network to date. The launch window is listed for 12:54 p.m. Eastern Time on November 13, subject to range approval, which means the date itself could still move.

For Amazon, the stakes are as high as they are for ULA. Amazon has placed fewer than 400 operational broadband satellites into orbit for its Amazon Leo constellation — formerly known as Project Kuiper — after cascading development and grounding delays across its primary heavy-lift launch providers, according to SpaceNews reporting on November 3, 2026. The company originally contracted 77 heavy-lift missions across Arianespace, Blue Origin, and ULA to deploy its 3,232-satellite constellation, but technical setbacks have constrained its cadence. Under its Federal Communications Commission authorisation, Amazon must deploy half of the constellation by the middle of the decade and complete all 3,232 satellites by July 2029. That deadline is what makes every Vulcan flight, and every subsequent launch, count: the constellation cannot be built at a trickle. Reports also point to a further 36 satellites planned for launch on November 23 aboard an Arianespace Ariane 6 rocket from French Guiana, which would make November a genuine acceleration month for Amazon even while SpaceX's Florida pads sit quiet.

The contrast between the two companies' Novembers is instructive. SpaceX, the market leader, is deliberately quiet in Florida because it is rebuilding for a bigger rocket. Amazon, the challenger, is urgently loud because it is racing a regulatory clock with a constellation still in its early hundreds. ULA, the incumbent launch provider, is returning a grounded rocket to flight because its biggest commercial customer needs it. All three stories intersect on the same stretch of Florida coastline in the same month, and none of them would be visible if you only counted Falcon 9 launches. The Space Coast's November is not empty; it is redistributed, with the headline act changing from SpaceX to ULA for a few weeks.

Blue Origin, notably, still has no firm, dated Florida flight in this window. The company has said it would fly its New Glenn rocket again before the end of 2026, and reports in late October noted that it has rebuilt the pad's tower and could return to flight as early as November. But as of the latest schedule updates, no date is fixed. That leaves ULA's November 13 Vulcan flight as the sole Florida launch between Bandwagon-5 on October 20 and Cygnus NG-25 on November 29 — a single event carrying the whole coast's launch-watching hopes for more than five weeks.

## The return: Cygnus NG-25 ends the pause

If the schedule holds, the pause ends the way it should: with a cargo run to the space station. Cygnus CRS-2 NG-25 is a Northrop Grumman cargo resupply flight to the International Space Station, the 25th Cygnus flight and its 24th trip to the station under NASA's Commercial Resupply Services contract. It is listed for November 29, 2026, from Space Launch Complex 40, the same pad Bandwagon-5 left on October 20. The symmetry is neat — the same pad, the same company, six weeks apart — and it underscores that the pause was always a gap in the schedule, not a change in the relationship between SpaceX and its Florida pads.

Cargo flights are easy to overlook beside crew launches and constellation deployments, but they are the backbone of station operations. Cygnus carries food, experiments, hardware, and supplies that keep a continuously crewed outpost running, and its arrival is a logistical event planned months in advance. That a station cargo flight is the mission that reopens Florida Falcon 9 operations is fitting: it is routine, essential, and unglamorous in exactly the way that a mature launch industry should be. The Space Coast does not need a spectacular return. It needs its workhorse back on its regular route, and Cygnus NG-25 provides it.

There is, of course, a caveat that applies to every date in this article. Dates can still move, and they often do, with little notice, for weather, technical readiness, or range availability. The six-week figure itself is approximate: from October 20 to November 29 is forty days, just under six weeks, and a slip of a few days in either direction would stretch or shrink it slightly. What is firm is the shape of the story, not the exact edges. Florida's Falcon 9 operations paused after October 20. They are scheduled to resume on November 29. In between, one ULA Vulcan flight carries the coast's launch calendar. And behind all of it, Starship infrastructure at 39A moves toward completion.

## What to watch next

Three signals will tell observers that the pause is truly ending, and they are worth watching in order. The first is a Starship arriving in Florida. Watch for the barge You'll Thank Me Later delivering the first booster and ship to Port Canaveral and the Kennedy Turn Basin — a visible, photographable event that no schedule update can substitute for. The second is a firm date appearing for the first post-pause Florida Falcon 9, or the first Florida Starship, on SpaceX mission pages and launch schedules. The third is Gigabay and 39A activity: doors on Gigabay and final pad work at 39A are the visible last steps Dontchev described, and their completion will be evident to anyone watching the Kennedy construction sites.

For visitors planning a trip to watch a launch in November, the practical advice follows directly. The calendar is no longer empty: ULA's Vulcan launches Amazon Leo satellites on November 13, and SpaceX's Falcon 9 returns with the Cygnus NG-25 cargo flight on November 29. But the Space Coast will still be far quieter than usual, and dates can move with little notice, so checking the Florida launch schedule before booking around either date is essential. The payoff for the wait is still coming, just not on a firm date yet. When Starship finally lifts off from Florida for the first time — currently expected next year, not this year — it will start the next era of Space Coast launches, with a booster catch attempt at the tower planned to follow.

## Why a quiet coast is a confident coast

It is tempting to read any pause in launches as decline. The history of the Space Coast counsels against it. The Cape has gone quiet before — between programmes, between rocket generations, between eras — and each quiet stretch has preceded a louder one. The Saturn era gave way to the Shuttle era; the Shuttle era gave way to a commercial era nobody quite predicted; the Falcon 9 era, which made weekly launches routine, is now giving way to something its own builders describe as larger in every dimension. A coast that recorded 109 launches in a single year does not go quiet because it is failing. It goes quiet because it is changing tools, and changing tools takes time.

The deeper point is about what launch cadence actually measures. Counting launches is easy, and it is often the only metric the public sees. But a launch count does not distinguish between a rocket flying its hundredth routine mission and a rocket flying its first test of a new capability. It does not capture pad construction, factory throughput, or the satellite production lines that determine whether there is anything worth launching. SpaceX's Florida pause is a reminder that the most important work in spaceflight sometimes happens when nothing is leaving the pad — when a tower is finished, a processing facility is fitted out, and a barge route is proven. The launches that follow are the payoff, not the work itself.

There is also a human dimension that launch counts miss. The engineers, technicians, and range personnel who spent years making Falcon 9 launches from Florida routine are now, in many cases, the same people finishing Starship's Florida home. Their work has not stopped; it has changed shape, from countdown operations to construction and activation. When the first Starship does lift off from 39A, it will be their work, too, that makes it possible. The six quiet weeks are, in that sense, not empty at all. They are full of the unphotogenic, essential labour that every new era of spaceflight requires before it can announce itself with fire and noise.

For now, the Space Coast waits. Californians will see Falcon 9s rise from Vandenberg several times a week. Texans will watch Starship Flight 15 attempt something no rocket has tried before. Floridians will watch a single Vulcan climb on November 13, carrying 40 satellites for a constellation racing a deadline, and then wait for a cargo ship to the space station on November 29 to reopen the Falcon 9 era's Florida chapter. And somewhere near Roberts Road, behind the doors soon to be fitted to a 380-foot building, the next chapter is already being stacked, piece by piece, for a debut that has no date yet but has, for the first time, a visible shape. The silence, it turns out, is not the story ending. It is the sound of the next one being built.
