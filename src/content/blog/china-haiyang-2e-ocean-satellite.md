At 7:46 on a Thursday morning, with the summer heat already rising off the Gobi Desert, a Long March 4B rocket climbed away from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center carrying a machine whose job is to read the ocean. The satellite aboard, Haiyang-2E, is China's newest eye on the sea — a 1,500-kilogram spacecraft built to measure the waves, winds, and surface temperatures of the world's oceans with better precision than anything the country has flown before in nearshore waters.

The launch, which took place on July 2, 2026 at 07:46 Beijing time (23:46 UTC on July 1), marked the 654th flight of a Long March series rocket. Within minutes, the satellite was confirmed in its preset orbit, and the mission was declared a complete success. For China's marine remote sensing program, it was a relay handoff years in the making: Haiyang-2E will take over from Haiyang-2B, which has been on station since 2018, and join Haiyang-2C and Haiyang-2D to keep the country's ocean-monitoring network running without interruption.

It is easy to overlook an ocean satellite in an era of lunar missions and megaconstellations. But the oceans drive weather, feed hundreds of millions of people, and carry most of the world's trade. A satellite that can see a storm building over the Pacific a week out, or measure sea levels creeping upward year after year, is infrastructure in the truest sense — the kind of capability that shows up not in headlines but in harvests, shipping schedules, and lives saved during typhoon season.

## A dawn launch from the Gobi

The Long March 4B lifted off from Launch Site 94 at Jiuquan, China's historic spaceport in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, heading southeast toward a sun-synchronous orbit roughly 970 kilometers above the Earth. The vehicle, developed by the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology, is a three-stage liquid-propellant rocket designed for exactly this kind of work: delivering satellites of up to 2.5 tonnes into sun-synchronous orbits at around 700 kilometers altitude.

This particular flight carried an operational footnote that rocket engineers will find interesting. It was the first time the Long March 4B used a parallel fueling mode under high-temperature conditions — a procedural change aimed at handling propellant loading more efficiently when desert summer temperatures complicate ground operations. Jiuquan in early July is no place for leisurely countdowns; adapting the fueling flow to the heat is the kind of unglamorous innovation that keeps a high-cadence launch program on schedule.

Riding on top of the rocket was the Haiyang-2E spacecraft, developed under the overall leadership of the China Academy of Space Technology (CAST) on behalf of the National Satellite Ocean Application Service, which operates under the State Oceanic Administration. China's official statements list its missions plainly: safeguarding maritime rights and interests, disaster prevention and mitigation, marine resource development, and marine scientific research. That quartet of roles captures how ocean satellites sit at the intersection of science, economy, and national strategy — and it is worth taking each one seriously.

## What the satellite actually measures

Haiyang-2E is not a camera in the conventional sense. It carries no big telescope for taking pretty pictures of coastlines. Instead, it is a microwave instrument package — a flying physics lab that probes the ocean with radar and listens to the faint thermal glow the sea emits naturally. Microwaves have a superpower that visible light lacks: they pass through clouds. A typhoon that hides the ocean from optical satellites cannot hide from Haiyang-2E, which is precisely the point.

The payload suite consists of five elements, each with a distinct job:

**The microwave radiometer imager** sweeps a 1,600-kilometer-wide path beneath the satellite, measuring sea surface temperature, surface winds, and atmospheric water vapor. Temperature and vapor readings feed directly into weather models; a warm patch of ocean is the fuel a typhoon burns.

**The dual-beam scatterometer** works in the Ku band and covers a 1,300-kilometer swath. It measures ocean surface wind vectors by firing radar pulses at the sea and reading how the roughness of the water scatters the signal back. Wind fields over remote ocean — where no buoy or ship can report — are among the most valuable data points in marine meteorology.

**The calibration microwave radiometer**, with 25-kilometer resolution, measures atmospheric water vapor so that the other instruments' readings can be corrected for moisture in the air. It is the quiet quality-control specialist of the payload family: unglamorous, essential.

**The radar altimeter**, resolving features down to 16 kilometers, tracks wave height by timing radar echoes bounced off the sea surface to extraordinary precision. Altimeters are also the workhorses of sea-level monitoring; years of altimetry data build the climate record of a rising ocean.

**The laser retroreflector array** enables ground stations to bounce laser pulses off the satellite and measure its range with centimeter-level precision. Precise orbit determination sounds like bookkeeping, but every altimeter measurement depends on knowing exactly where the satellite was when it took it.

Taken together, the package gives China an upgraded capability for nearshore observation in particular. Chinese reports emphasize that Haiyang-2E features improved payload technology that significantly strengthens monitoring of coastal waters — the busy, economically vital band of ocean where shipping lanes, fishing grounds, offshore wind farms, and storm surge zones all overlap.

## A constellation fifteen years in the making

Haiyang-2E did not arrive out of nowhere. It is the latest member of the Haiyang-2 series, China's dedicated ocean dynamics constellation, and its story stretches back a decade and a half.

Haiyang-2A launched in August 2011 as the country's first dedicated ocean dynamics satellite, proving that China could do sea-surface wind and wave measurement from orbit. Haiyang-2B followed in October 2018, the workhorse that 2E now replaces — a reminder that satellites have finite lives and constellations must be fed new members to survive. Haiyang-2C (September 2020) and Haiyang-2D (May 2021) introduced a twist: instead of sun-synchronous orbits, they fly in inclined orbits that revisit the same mid-latitude ocean regions more quickly, an arrangement tuned for catching fast-developing storms like typhoons.

That mix of orbits is the constellation's quiet cleverness. The sun-synchronous members give stable, consistent global coverage — the same lighting geometry on every pass, ideal for building long climate records. The inclined members swing over typhoon-prone waters more often. With 2E refreshing the sun-synchronous backbone, the network is positioned to operate continuously for years, and China has said the satellites will work together with other orbiting assets to keep marine dynamic environment data flowing without gaps.

The continuity matters more than any single launch. Ocean science runs on time series: one measurement of sea level is trivia, twenty years of measurements is a climate record. Replacing 2B before its retirement degrades coverage is how you keep that record intact.

## Reading the wind without seeing it

Scatterometry — the technique behind Haiyang-2E's dual-beam instrument — deserves a closer look, because it is one of the cleverest tricks in remote sensing. The satellite fires Ku-band radar pulses at the ocean surface from several angles and measures the energy scattered back. The physics is intuitive: a calm sea is a mirror that bounces most of the radar energy away from the satellite, while a rough, wind-churned sea scatters energy back toward the receiver. By comparing backscatter from multiple look directions, the instrument can solve for both wind speed and wind direction across a 1,300-kilometer swath — thousands of wind vectors per orbit over waters no ship or buoy will ever sample.

Why does that matter? Numerical weather prediction models are hungry for ocean wind data. The western Pacific, where Haiyang-2E's predecessors have been feeding data for years, is the birthplace of the typhoons that strike China, Japan, Korea, and the Philippines. A scatterometer pass over a developing tropical disturbance can reveal the surface wind structure that tells forecasters whether a tropical depression is about to become a typhoon — and how strong it may get. Every major weather agency in the world assimilates scatterometer data; China's constellation gives it an independent source for its own models and a bargaining chip in international data exchange.

The radar altimeter plays a different, longer game. By timing the round trip of radar pulses to a fraction of a nanosecond, it measures the distance from the satellite to the sea surface. Combined with the laser-ranging precision orbit from the retroreflector array, that yields sea surface height — and therefore wave height, and over months and years, sea level itself. The global network of altimeters has shown that sea level is rising at more than 3 millimeters per year and accelerating. For China's low-lying coastal deltas, where hundreds of millions of people live within a few meters of the sea, that slow-motion number is one of the most consequential measurements in all of Earth science.

## The wider Haiyang family

The Haiyang-2 series is only half of China's ocean remote sensing effort. Its sibling, the Haiyang-1 series, looks at the ocean in a completely different way: instead of dynamics — waves and winds — it studies ocean color. Ocean color instruments measure the subtle greens and blues of seawater, which reveal chlorophyll concentrations, sediment plumes, red tides, and coastal pollution. Haiyang-1C and Haiyang-1D have been flying since 2018 and 2020, watching the biological pulse of the sea.

Together, the two series give China a full-spectrum view: the HY-1 satellites see what the ocean looks like and what lives in it; the HY-2 satellites measure how it moves and how high it stands. Add in the country's land-observation Gaofen satellites, which contribute coastal imaging, and the picture is a comprehensive national remote sensing architecture — one that China built deliberately over three decades, starting from its first ocean color satellite, Haiyang-1A, in 2002.

Haiyang-2E's upgraded nearshore capability plugs a specific gap. Coastal waters are the hardest to observe well: altimeters struggle near land where radar echoes get contaminated by terrain, and scatterometers lose accuracy at the swath edges over complex coastlines. Yet the coast is where the economic value concentrates — ports, aquaculture, tourism, and the storm surges that threaten them all. Better nearshore observations mean better surge forecasts when the next typhoon makes landfall, and better monitoring of the fishing grounds and shipping lanes that hug China's 18,000-kilometer coastline.

## Why the ocean, why now

China's interest in ocean remote sensing is not abstract. The country has more than 18,000 kilometers of mainland coastline and an enormous stake in what the sea does. Typhoons barrel in from the western Pacific every summer, threatening megacities along the coast; better wind and wave data translates directly into better storm forecasts and better evacuation decisions. Sea surface temperature readings feed the models that predict those storms' intensity. Wave height data guides shipping and offshore operations. Wind data informs the placement and operation of the offshore wind farms that are a growing part of China's energy mix.

Fisheries are another piece of the puzzle. Ocean temperature fronts and currents concentrate the plankton that feed fish, and satellite data helps fleets find productive waters while helping regulators manage stocks. Disaster mitigation extends to storm surge, red tides, and oil spills — all phenomena that satellites can spot before they reach shore.

Then there is the strategic dimension, which Chinese statements do not hide: "safeguarding maritime rights and interests." Ocean satellites map the waters China claims, monitor activity in disputed areas, and provide the environmental baseline for operations at sea. Remote sensing constellations have always been dual-use; the same wind field that steers a cargo ship safely also steers a naval task force.

Climate science is the longest game of all. Radar altimeters like the one on Haiyang-2E are the primary tool for measuring global sea level, which has been rising at an accelerating rate. China's coastline includes low-lying deltas — the Pearl River Delta, the Yangtze Delta — where centimeters of sea-level rise carry enormous economic consequences. Keeping a Chinese altimeter flying means China owns its own climate record rather than depending on foreign data.

## The 654th flight and the machine behind it

For the rocket industry, the headline number was 654: the total count of Long March series flights now stands at six hundred and fifty-four, a cadence tally that few launch families in history can approach. The Long March 4B itself has been flying since 1999 with a long string of consecutive successes, and it remains one of China's most dependable workhorses for sun-synchronous missions — the weather satellites, ocean satellites, and land-observation spacecraft that fly in those dawn-dusk orbits.

The parallel fueling innovation on this flight deserves a moment of attention because it illustrates how launch operations evolve. Rocket propellant loading is a carefully choreographed process, and high ambient temperatures change the behavior of cryogenic and storable propellants alike, complicate ground equipment, and stress the teams working the pad. Running fueling steps in parallel rather than sequence — while keeping everything within safety margins — trims the countdown and widens the launch window. It is the kind of operational refinement that matters enormously when a country is flying dozens of missions a year and cannot afford weather or heat to keep scrubbing its rockets.

## A crowded but cooperative sky

Haiyang-2E joins a global fleet of ocean-observing satellites that, remarkably, works as one system despite being built by rival powers. Europe's Copernicus program flies Sentinel-3 satellites with altimeters and ocean color instruments, and Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich — a joint U.S.-European mission — continues the Jason series sea-level record that stretches back to 1992. France and the United States operate the SWOT mission, which maps ocean surface topography in two dimensions rather than along a single track. India's Oceansat series, Japan's GCOM satellites, and others add their own sensors.

For climate records, this redundancy is a feature, not waste. Sea-level rise is measured in millimeters per year against an ocean that heaves with tides, currents, and weather; detecting the trend requires overlapping independent instruments that can be cross-calibrated. When one nation's altimeter confirms another's, confidence in the number grows. China has shared Haiyang data through international channels in the past, and the continuity that 2E provides strengthens the global dataset — even as the same satellites serve national security and economic interests at home.

The dual-use reality is unavoidable. An ocean wind map that guides a container ship around a storm also guides a navy around a storm; a wave forecast that protects an offshore wind farm also protects an offshore operation. Remote sensing has been strategic since the first weather satellites, and no country's constellation is purely civilian. Haiyang-2E is no exception — but neither is that the whole story. The instrument's data will feed typhoon warnings that save lives regardless of whose flag is on the satellite bus.

## What comes next

With Haiyang-2E on orbit and undergoing commissioning, China's ocean-monitoring network enters a period of full-strength operation: a fresh sun-synchronous backbone member, two inclined-orbit rapid-revisit satellites, and the accumulated archive of its predecessors. Data from the constellation flows to Chinese weather services, marine agencies, and research institutions, and China has historically shared ocean satellite data through international cooperation channels, including with agencies tracking global sea level and tropical cyclones.

The bigger picture is that marine remote sensing is getting crowded — in a good way. Europe's Copernicus program flies Sentinel altimeters and scatterometers; the United States and Europe jointly operate the Jason/Sentinel-6 altimetry line; India, Japan, and others contribute their own instruments. Haiyang-2E adds redundancy and coverage to that global system, and for climate records, more independent sensors mean more confidence in the numbers.

A single satellite launch rarely changes the world. But some launches quietly make the world more legible — and a sharper eye on the ocean, watching waves rise and winds gather and temperatures climb, is exactly that kind of launch. The next time a typhoon turns toward the Chinese coast, forecasters will have one more instrument feeding their models. That is the real payload of Haiyang-2E, and it will be delivering it for years.
