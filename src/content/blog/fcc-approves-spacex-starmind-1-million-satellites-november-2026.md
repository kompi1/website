The Federal Communications Commission has given SpaceX permission to do something no regulator has ever allowed before: put one million satellites in orbit, not to beam the internet down to Earth, but to move the data center itself into space.

The approval, announced on Wednesday, 25 November 2026, covers SpaceX's Starmind constellation — a fleet of orbital data centers, or orbital compute modules, that would process the enormous workloads of artificial intelligence above the atmosphere, powered by unbroken sunlight and cooled by the cold of space. The FCC called the decision a historic first. Its chairman, Brendan Carr, framed it as a move in the race for what he calls super intelligence. Astronomers, rival satellite operators, and space-sustainability advocates called it something else: a decision taken at a scale, and at a speed, that the rules for low Earth orbit were never designed to handle.

The numbers alone mark a break with everything that has come before. Roughly 17,000 satellites are in orbit today, according to Reuters reporting on the decision. Starmind alone would be authorised for nearly 60 times that number. It would be about 24 times larger than Starlink, SpaceX's own broadband constellation, which is authorised for 42,000 satellites. And the approval is only the first: the FCC says it is reviewing other orbital data center applications and intends to decide them quickly, including an 88,000-satellite filing from Starcloud and a 51,600-satellite constellation called Project Sunrise from Blue Origin. If all of them were built, the sky would hold more satellites than humanity has launched in its entire history — several times over.

Whether all of them will be built is a different question, and it is one the FCC pointedly declined to answer. In its order, the agency said that doubts about the feasibility and long-term impact of orbital data centers were outside its regulatory mandate. "In many cases, the comments seek to substitute the government's judgment regarding the viability of business endeavors for that of the applicant and private enterprise. We decline to do so," the FCC wrote. The licence is real, the milestones are binding, and the satellites are, for now, largely hypothetical.

## A million machines, and a deadline to start

SpaceX revealed its plan to build one million orbital data centers in January 2026, and filed for the constellation with the FCC at the end of that month, around the time SpaceX acquired xAI, bringing Elon Musk's space and AI businesses under one roof. Ten months later, the approval arrived — fast by the standards of satellite licensing, and a sign of how far the political wind has shifted behind the idea.

The licence, issued as FCC order DA 26-1273, was granted in part and deferred in part, with conditions. It authorises an optically linked constellation of one million satellites at altitudes between 500 and 1,002 kilometres, across six permitted altitudes and inclinations. The satellites are designed to communicate with each other primarily by laser, through optical inter-satellite links, with Ka-band radio reserved for limited backup and emergency operations, including telemetry, tracking, and command. The authorisation runs for 15 years, with the clock starting once the first Starmind satellite reaches orbit.

Each satellite is, in effect, a small data center. SpaceX describes the Starmind spacecraft as carrying a compute payload of 175 kilowatts on average, and up to 250 kilowatts at peak. They are designed with deployable liquid radiators to shed the heat that computing generates, and solar arrays based on the arrays used in the Starlink constellation. The physics is the pitch: in orbit, a satellite can draw on sunlight almost without interruption, and it needs no water for cooling, because space itself is cold. On Earth, the data centers that train the largest AI models are constrained by power grids, water supplies, and planning permission. In orbit, at least in theory, those constraints fall away.

The approval comes with a deployment schedule that is generous in the near term and unforgiving in the long term. SpaceX must launch one Starmind satellite by 25 November 2033, the first 10 percent — 100,000 satellites — by 25 November 2035, half the constellation by 25 November 2038, and the full million by 25 November 2040. The milestones are the FCC's standard tool for preventing spectrum warehousing: a company cannot sit on a frequency assignment for a constellation it never builds. But the first milestone is seven years away, and it requires a single satellite. SpaceX's president and chief operating officer, Gwynne Shotwell, told investors in October that the company plans to begin launching Starmind satellites in 2027 — which would put hardware in orbit long before the licence demands it.

That launch schedule, if it holds, is where the plan meets its first hard test. A million satellites cannot be launched one at a time. Even spread across the fifteen years of the licence, a million spacecraft means roughly 180 launches a day at Starship scale, or a production line that treats satellites the way a car factory treats cars. SpaceX has not published a detailed manufacturing or launch plan for Starmind, and analysts quoted in the trade press note that orbital data centers remain technically and economically unproven, even as they fit SpaceX's long-term strategy of vertical integration — combining compute, satellite operations, and launch under one company. The FCC's answer to that uncertainty was to approve first and let the market decide.

## Why orbit, and why now

The case for data centers in space rests on two scarcities on Earth: power and cooling. Training a frontier AI model now consumes as much electricity as a small city, and the data centers that do the work are among the largest single consumers of power and water in the regions where they are built. In a Sun-synchronous orbit — the dawn-dusk orbit that Starmind and its competitors favour — a satellite crosses the equator at 6 a.m. and 6 p.m., staying in sunlight almost continuously, and its solar arrays can generate power around the clock. There is no night to ride through on batteries, and no water to evaporate for cooling. The heat radiates away into space.

Carr made the political case in his statement announcing the approval, calling the decision the opening of "a bold new frontier." "Compute campuses or data centers are essential to sustaining America's leadership in [super intelligence]. By moving compute capacity beyond Earth, SpaceX and the many competitors working on their own orbital data centers are meeting the moment. This is the kind of American ingenuity that can unite our strengths in space and advanced technology, reinforce our economic leadership, and expand prosperity here at home," he said. Announcing the decision on X, he wrote that the Starmind and competitor constellations "will use solar power to extend America's lead in Super Intelligence." Musk replied that it would be "a great asset to America and the world."

The timing is not accidental. The AI industry's demand for computing power has grown faster than the power grid in the places where data centers are built, and the companies building the largest models have begun looking for power wherever it can be found — including, now, above the atmosphere. SpaceX is not alone in that search. The Secure World Foundation, which tracks the field, lists nine companies with orbital data center plans, five of them American and therefore in need of FCC approval for the radio frequencies that link ground and space. Starcloud, which has already flown a GPU in orbit and raised hundreds of millions of dollars to industrialise the idea, has filed for 88,000 satellites. Blue Origin's Project Sunrise would fly 51,600. SpaceNews has covered Starcloud's rise in <a href="/blog/starcloud-orbital-ai-data-centers-nvidia-250m-extension/">Nvidia Bets on the Sky: Starcloud's $250 Million Extension Takes Orbital AI Data Centers From Demo to Factory Floor</a>, and the FCC's earlier, much smaller satellite approvals in <a href="/blog/spacex-starlink-mobile-fcc-15000-satellites-approval/">FCC Clears SpaceX's 15,000 Starlink Mobile Satellites, a Direct Carrier Challenge</a>.

What changed in 2026 is that the idea moved from pitch decks to licences. Starmind is the first orbital data center constellation the FCC has ever approved, and the agency says the others will follow soon. The question is no longer whether regulators will allow data centers in space. It is whether anyone can build them at the scale now permitted — and what happens to the sky if they try.

## The objections the FCC set aside

The approval did not go unchallenged. Amazon, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society all filed petitions to deny the application, and the Secure World Foundation was among the organisations that filed public comments calling for more scrutiny. Their concerns fell into three groups: collisions, the atmosphere, and the night sky. The FCC's order addressed each of them largely by declaring them someone else's problem.

On collisions, the arithmetic is stark. A million satellites travelling in the same orbital bands, even spread across six altitudes and inclinations, would multiply the number of objects that operators must track and dodge. SpaceX's satellites would communicate by laser, which avoids adding to radio congestion, but no laser link prevents a physical collision. At a Secure World Foundation event on 29 July 2026, speakers warned that there is still no global "rules of the road" agreement for these dawn-dusk orbits — no settled answer to whether millions of satellites will all use the same route, crossing the equator at 6 a.m. or 6 p.m., or split between the two. Matias Cava, from the Department of Commerce's Office of Space Commerce, encouraged operators to develop and follow responsible practices now, based on how they would want their competitors to behave — an admission, in effect, that formal regulation is not arriving fast enough. The FCC, for its part, argued that it does not place limits on the number of space stations that can be authorised with similar orbital parameters.

On the atmosphere, the concern is what happens when satellites die. Every satellite in low orbit eventually re-enters and burns up, and a constellation of a million would mean a continuous rain of vaporised spacecraft — aluminium and other materials deposited in the upper atmosphere at a rate no one has yet measured at scale. The FCC declined to require further environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act, consistent with its October 2026 order modernising its environmental rules, which concluded that FCC-licensed space-based operations do not constitute a major federal action because they take place outside United States territory. The order said concerns about the atmospheric effects of re-entering satellites "remain speculative and not supported in the record."

On the night sky, the objection was the most direct, and the most emotional. The American Astronomical Society said it was "disheartened and dismayed" by the decision, because it will "significantly undermine (i) decades of federal investment in astronomical facilities and (ii) the ability of astronomers and the public to enjoy and study the night sky." The society argued that it is critical for the FCC to consider the protection of optical and infrared astronomy when assessing applications from satellite operators, in the absence of another licensing agency that can take these concerns into account. "We continue to emphasize that American technical innovation in space cannot come at the expense of imperiling scientific research, which has been foundational for American innovation and global leadership for decades," the society said. The FCC's ruling held that any impacts on the environment or astronomy are outside its jurisdiction.

The Secure World Foundation, in its comments on the SpaceX, Blue Origin, and Starcloud plans, called for system-level analysis and disclosure, and for a phased, demonstration-based approach to authorisation — prove a small number first, then scale. It described the current wave of applications as "a qualitative shift in the scale of proposed satellite constellation operations" that "raises novel technical, environmental, and governance considerations." Its conclusion was blunt: "These are not incremental expansions of existing Non-Geostationary Orbit licensing practice. They raise novel technical, environmental, and governance questions that extend beyond the FCC's routine licensing practice and expertise, and they warrant closer regulatory scrutiny and system-level evaluation." The FCC granted the application anyway, with waivers — including for spectrum for telemetry, tracking and command, and for processing-round rules, with SpaceX instructed to operate on an unprotected and non-interference basis in certain bands. The agency said it grants similar waivers for innovative and untested technologies.

## How Starmind compares

The scale of what the FCC has authorised becomes clearest when Starmind is set against the constellations that already exist, and the ones its competitors are planning. Starlink, the largest constellation ever flown, is authorised for 42,000 satellites; roughly 17,000 satellites of all kinds are in orbit today. Starmind alone would be more than twice the size of every orbital data center constellation proposed by every other company combined.

| Constellation | Operator | Satellites authorised / proposed | Purpose | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Starmind | SpaceX | 1,000,000 | Orbital data centers (AI compute) | Approved by FCC, 25 Nov 2026 |
| Starlink | SpaceX | 42,000 | Broadband internet | Operating; plus Starshield military variant |
| Project Sunrise | Blue Origin | 51,600 | Orbital data centers | Filed with FCC; decision pending |
| Starcloud | Starcloud | 88,000 | Orbital data centers (AI compute) | Filed with FCC; decision pending |
| Starlink Mobile | SpaceX | 15,000 | Direct-to-phone service | Approved by FCC, Oct 2026 |
| All satellites in orbit today | All operators | ~17,000 | All purposes | Operating |

The table carries a warning of its own. Authorisation is not deployment. Starlink's 42,000-satellite authorisation took years to build toward, and the company still has thousands of satellites to launch to reach it. Starmind's milestones give SpaceX until 2040 to reach a million — fifteen years of launches at a pace no one has ever sustained. The gap between a licence and a constellation is where most mega-constellation plans have historically died, and the FCC's order does not close it. It merely removes the regulatory obstacle and starts the clock.

## A timeline of the orbital data center race

The idea of computing in orbit is older than the term "orbital data center," but the race to licence one is barely a year old. The milestones below trace how quickly the field moved from announcement to approval.

## Timeline: from pitch to permission

- **January 2026** — SpaceX reveals plans to build one million orbital data centers, and files for the Starmind constellation with the FCC at the end of the month, around the time SpaceX acquires xAI.
- **February 2026** — The Secure World Foundation and other organisations begin filing public comments on the orbital data center applications from SpaceX, Blue Origin, and Starcloud, calling for system-level analysis and a phased, demonstration-based approach.
- **July 29, 2026** — At a Secure World Foundation event, experts warn there is no global "rules of the road" for dawn-dusk orbits, and that formal regulation may not arrive fast enough for the scale of what is proposed.
- **October 2026** — The FCC issues its order modernising its environmental rules, concluding that space-based operations do not trigger its National Environmental Policy Act obligations. In the same month, Gwynne Shotwell tells investors SpaceX plans to begin launching Starmind satellites in 2027.
- **October 2026** — The FCC approves SpaceX's 15,000-satellite Starlink Mobile constellation, a separate authorisation that shows the agency's willingness to grant large-scale satellite licences at speed.
- **November 25, 2026** — The FCC approves Starmind: one million satellites, six altitudes between 500 and 1,002 kilometres, with deployment milestones running to 2040. It is the first orbital data center constellation ever approved.
- **2027 (planned)** — SpaceX aims to begin launching Starmind satellites, according to Shotwell's October investor briefing.
- **November 25, 2033** — First binding milestone: at least one Starmind satellite must be in orbit.
- **November 25, 2035** — 10 percent of the constellation, 100,000 satellites, must be deployed.
- **November 25, 2038** — Half the constellation, 500,000 satellites, must be deployed.
- **November 25, 2040** — The full one million satellites must be deployed, or the authorisation lapses.

## What the rivals do next

The FCC's decision was written for SpaceX, but its consequences fall on everyone else in the race. Carr's announcement on X noted the "competitor constellations" by name, and the agency says it intends to decide the pending applications expeditiously. Starcloud and Blue Origin are now the obvious next decisions, and both have reason to expect approval on similar terms: the FCC has now established that it will not block an orbital data center on feasibility, environmental, or astronomical grounds, and that it will grant waivers for untested technology.

That precedent matters more than the Starmind licence itself. If Starcloud's 88,000 and Blue Origin's 51,600 are approved on the same basis, the total authorised orbital data center population would approach 1.14 million satellites — and the nine companies on the Secure World Foundation's list include others that have not yet filed. Europe, meanwhile, is moving in the opposite direction on sovereignty rather than scale: Brussels is fast-tracking its own satellite giant, as SpaceNews reported in <a href="/blog/project-bromo-europe-fast-track-starlink-rival/">Project Bromo: Europe Fast-Tracks Its Starlink Rival as Brussels Races for Satellite Sovereignty</a>, a reminder that the contest over who controls the sky is now fought in antitrust reviews as well as in launch manifests.

For Amazon, which filed a petition to deny the Starmind application, the decision is a defeat with a long tail. Amazon operates its own broadband constellation and has its own ambitions in orbit; a million-satellite competitor with a fifteen-year licence and a first launch planned for 2027 changes the competitive arithmetic of every filing it makes from now on. For Viasat, the other commercial petitioner, the concern was spectrum and interference — the radio frequencies that keep satellites talking to the ground. The FCC's answer, that SpaceX must operate on an unprotected and non-interference basis in certain bands, is a compromise that puts the burden of avoiding interference on the newcomer, but it does not reduce the number of transmitters in the sky.

For astronomers, the decision is the one they feared. The American Astronomical Society's statement was not a technical objection but a plea: that the protection of optical and infrared astronomy be weighed at all, by somebody, before a million new objects are authorised. The FCC's response — that such impacts are outside its jurisdiction — leaves the society with no regulator to appeal to, and no licensing process in which the night sky is a factor. The telescopes now under construction, the product of decades of federal investment the society cited, will share their sky with whatever Starmind becomes. The society's warning, and the Secure World Foundation's call for system-level analysis, will now be tested against reality rather than against a licence application: if the constellation is built, the sky will change; if it is not, the licence will expire quietly in 2040.

## The bet at the heart of the decision

Strip away the politics, and the FCC's approval is a bet on a single idea: that the hardest problems in artificial intelligence — power, cooling, and scale — are easier to solve in orbit than on Earth, and that the right response to a technology nobody has yet proven is to permit it at the largest possible scale and let the builder prove it. The milestones are the hedge: one satellite by 2033, a hundred thousand by 2035, half a million by 2038, a million by 2040. If SpaceX cannot build them, the licence dies in stages, and the sky stays as it is. If it can, the sky will hold more machines than stars visible to the naked eye on a clear night — and the data centers that train the models of the 2030s will be the brightest moving objects in it.

That is the scale of the thing the FCC approved on Wednesday: not a satellite constellation, but a new kind of infrastructure, authorised before it exists, at a size no one has ever attempted, on a timetable that gives its builder fifteen years to make it real. The first satellite is due in orbit by 2033. The first launches are planned for 2027. Between now and then, the argument that began in the FCC's comment files — about collisions, the atmosphere, and the night sky — will move from paper to orbit, one launch at a time.
