Saturn has a second polygon. For more than forty years, astronomers have known about the six-sided jet stream circling the planet's north pole — a geometric oddity so strange it has never stopped being studied. Now the ringed giant has produced a companion: a ten-sided atmospheric wave encircling its south pole, roughly 104,000 miles across, and, unlike its ancient northern twin, it appears to be growing stronger before our eyes. The discovery, announced by NASA and the European Space Agency in early September from observations by the Hubble Space Telescope, gives planetary scientists something they have never had before — a chance to watch a giant polar pattern on a gas giant develop in near real time. And this October, you can look at the planet it sits on: Saturn reached opposition on October 4, 2026, rising at sunset and blazing brighter than it will on any other night of the year.

## A Storm With Ten Sides

The finding was reported in the journal *Science Advances* on September 2, 2026, in a paper led by Agustín Sánchez-Lavega of the University of the Basque Country in Bilbao, Spain, with collaborators including Amy Simon of NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center and Mike Wong of the University of California, Berkeley. It is the first time a large, regular-sided jet pattern has ever been observed in Saturn's southern hemisphere — the first southern answer to the northern hexagon that has been photographed on every look since the Voyager era.

The numbers are difficult to picture. The structure spans about 104,250 miles (167,820 kilometers) across — wide enough to swallow a dozen Earths laid end to end. Each of its ten sides runs roughly 10,425 miles (16,782 kilometers), longer than Earth's entire diameter. It rides a fast circumpolar jet centered near 63 degrees south latitude, drifting slowly eastward at about 10 kilometers per hour, taking on the order of 800 days to complete one full circuit around the pole. Crucially, the pattern is not static. Hubble's multi-year record shows it strengthening — sharpening into a clearer polygon rather than dissolving back into the surrounding clouds. The researchers caution that the precise figures should be held loosely, since the "sides" are a wave moving through a churning atmosphere, not lines drawn on a map. But the geometry is real, and it is new.

"We've never seen anything quite like this in Saturn's southern hemisphere," said Simon, who is also the principal investigator of Hubble's yearly survey of the outer planets. "The northern hexagon has been there every time we've looked for more than 40 years. This feature is different — it appears to be strengthening, giving us the rare opportunity to watch a giant atmospheric pattern develop."

That contrast — ancient stability in the north, active development in the south — is what makes the decagon more than a curiosity. The hexagon arrived on the scene already finished; nobody saw it form. The decagon offers something scientists rarely get from a gas giant: a formation event caught in the act.

## Reconstructed, Not Witnessed — But Carefully

Astronomers did not photograph the decagon being born. They reconstructed its emergence from years of images. Hubble observations tracing back to 2023 hold the earliest subtle hints of the wave, gathered under the telescope's Outer Planet Atmospheres Legacy program, which has photographed the outer planets once a year for more than a decade. Ground-based observers noticed an undulating band near Saturn's south pole in 2024 — including amateur astronomers Trevor Barry and Jean-Paul Oger, whose images feed the online platforms where professional and amateur planetary observers pool their work. By 2025 the ten-sided structure was becoming clearer, and the newest Hubble frames show its corners fully developed, with some sides sharper and more prominent than others.

What did not happen matters, too. NASA's Cassini spacecraft orbited Saturn for thirteen years, from 2004 to 2017, and never recorded a comparable long-lived southern formation. Its absence in the Cassini record suggests the decagon is genuinely young — a feature that developed after the south pole began turning back into sunlight and back into view from Earth. Saturn's tilted axis had kept the southern pole poorly placed for sustained monitoring from about 2012 onward; only as the seasons shifted did astronomers get a proper look at what was happening down there. One reason the decagon went unnoticed for so long may be that it quite literally grew while the region was hardest to see.

A note of scientific honesty accompanies the headline. The available images cannot pin down an exact birth date, and the wave may have been developing out of view. But the trend is unambiguous: an emerging structure becoming more defined, more geometric, more pronounced with each observing season. Strengthening, not fading.

## The Old Twin: A Hexagon at the North Pole

To understand why the decagon matters, you need to know its older sibling. Saturn's north polar hexagon was discovered in images from NASA's Voyager 1 and 2 flybys of 1980 and 1981, then identified as a coherent structure by David Godfrey in 1987 when he pieced the flyby views together. It was revisited in detail by Cassini, which arrived at Saturn in 2004 and watched the hexagon through 2017.

It is enormous. Centered near 78 degrees north latitude, the hexagon spans roughly 29,000 to 30,000 kilometers (about 18,000 to 20,000 miles) across — wider than two Earths. Each side runs about 14,500 kilometers, longer than Earth's 12,742-kilometer diameter. It is a jet stream of atmospheric gases racing at around 320 kilometers per hour (about 200 miles per hour), and it rotates with a period of 10 hours, 39 minutes, 24 seconds — essentially the same period as Saturn's radio emissions from its deep interior, which suggests the pattern is anchored to the planet itself rather than drifting in the winds. It does not shift in longitude the way ordinary cloud features do. Inside the hexagon sits a separate hurricane-like vortex with an eye roughly 2,000 kilometers wide, and Cassini watched the whole structure change color from a delicate blue to a goldenrod hue between 2012 and 2016, a seasonal effect of sunlight and atmospheric chemistry.

For decades, researchers expected to find a matching pattern at the south pole. The physics that could pinch a jet stream into a hexagon — most likely a standing Rossby wave, the same kind of planetary-scale wave that meanders Earth's jet stream — should operate at both poles. Yet year after year, the south showed only a circular hurricane-like vortex and no polygon. Physicists at the University of Oxford even recreated polygon jet streams on a spinning laboratory table, finding hexagonal shapes the most stable outcome of the experiment, which deepened the puzzle: if hexagons form so readily, where was the south's?

The answer, apparently, is that the south's polygon was simply late. It took its time — and arrived with four extra sides.

## Why Ten Sides Is Stranger Than Six

The decagon is not merely the hexagon copied and rotated. The two features differ in the ways that matter most to atmospheric scientists: side count, motion, apparent stability, scale, and known lifetime. The northern hexagon is practically stationary in longitude and has been present at every observation for more than four decades. The decagon migrates eastward at around 10 kilometers per hour and is still strengthening, which means its final form — if it settles into one — is unknown. Its ~167,820-kilometer span makes it dramatically larger than the hexagon's ~29,000-kilometer width, though the two are being measured at different jet latitudes and by different generations of instruments, so the honest comparison is in their dynamics, not just their tape measures.

Those differences are data. A southern pattern that is forming now, under southern-hemisphere seasonal conditions, gives modelers a live test case for the theories developed to explain the northern hexagon. One leading idea holds that deep convective flows generate vortices that "pinch" a zonal eastward jet near the pole, warping it into a stable polygonal waveform. If that mechanism is right, the side count may depend on the jet's speed, width, and latitude — all of which differ between Saturn's poles — and the decagon's continued evolution will either confirm or complicate that picture. The fact that Cassini's thirteen-year stare found no southern polygon while Hubble's survey caught one forming a decade later also feeds into the question of whether these features are permanent fixtures of gas-giant atmospheres or transient patterns that can switch on and off with the seasons.

Saturn's year is long — 29.4 Earth years — so a full seasonal cycle unfolds over timescales that strain a single mission. Hubble has been photographing the outer planets annually for more than a decade, and that patient cadence is precisely what makes the decagon visible as a story rather than a snapshot. Watching a 168,000-kilometer atmospheric structure sharpen its corners season by season is the closest planetary science comes to time-lapse geology on a world with no surface at all.

## The Best Night of the Year to See Saturn

The decagon's announcement lands in the same month as Saturn's closest, brightest approach to Earth of 2026. On October 4, at about 12:21 UTC (8:32 a.m. EDT), Saturn reached opposition — the point where Earth passes directly between the Sun and the planet, placing Saturn exactly opposite the Sun in our sky. Opposition matters for three reasons. The planet is as close to Earth as it gets all year — about 8.43 astronomical units, or 784 million miles (1.26 billion kilometers), a distance light takes 70 minutes to cross — so its disk and rings appear at their largest. Second, a subtle brightening known as the Seeliger effect, or opposition surge, makes the rings glow slightly brighter: with the Sun directly behind the observer, shadows cast by ring particles fall directly behind those particles and vanish. Third, and most practically, a planet at opposition rises as the Sun sets and stays visible all night, climbing to its highest point around local midnight when it sits above the thickest, most turbulent air.

This year's opposition is particularly rewarding because of the rings. In 2025, Saturn's rings were tilted at less than two degrees to our line of sight — nearly edge-on and almost vanishing. By October 2026 the tilt has opened to about 7.5 degrees, roughly a quarter of the maximum, clearly separating the rings from the planet's disk. A telescope of 80 millimeters or more at 100× magnification or above can split out the Cassini Division, the dark gap between the A and B rings, under steady seeing. The planet itself shone near magnitude 0.3 at opposition — brighter than nearly every star in the sky — with a disk about 19.7 arcseconds across and its ring system stretching roughly 44 arcseconds wide.

And the opposition glow lasts. You do not need to have observed on the exact night: any clear night in the first two to three weeks of October gives essentially the same view, and Saturn remains an evening-sky object for months, only slipping into the Sun's glare around March 2027. Last year's opposition fell on September 21; next year's arrives on October 18 — Saturn's 29.4-year orbit means each opposition drifts about two weeks later in the calendar.

Finding it is easy. At opposition Saturn sat in front of the constellation Cetus, rising in the east around sunset as a steady, creamy-gold point of light that does not twinkle the way stars do. To the naked eye it is simply a bright, constant dot. Binoculars on a tripod begin to hint at its slightly oval shape. A small backyard telescope at 100–200× resolves the rings, and patient observers under dark skies may catch the giant moon Titan as a faint star-like point nearby — the same Titan that NASA's Dragonfly mission, planned for launch in July 2028, will one day explore with a rotorcraft.

## An October Packed With Sky

Saturn's opposition anchors one of the year's busiest skywatching months. As NASA notes in its October 2026 "What's Up" guide, Saturn takes center stage while a meteor shower lights up the sky and the Moon visits the Seven Sisters. The Orionids — debris from the most famous comet of all, 1P/Halley — peak on the night of October 21 into the morning of October 22, with about 20 meteors per hour under ideal dark skies. A bright waxing gibbous Moon will wash out the fainter meteors this year, so the best strategy is to look in the few hours before dawn, after the Moon sets, from the darkest location available. On the night of October 27 into the 28th, look east as the Moon passes near the Pleiades star cluster, a slightly fuzzy grouping of six or seven stars visible to the unaided eye and spectacular in binoculars. The Draconids, active around October 7–8, add an early-month encore, and Uranus reaches its own opposition on October 26.

NASA's October skywatching notes add one more detail worth savoring while you look at Saturn: the ringed planet's southern hemisphere, where the decagon now lives, is tipped toward Earth this decade. The rings themselves are opening toward us after the near edge-on crossing of 2025. The geometry that lets you see the rings separating from the disk is the same geometry that let Hubble see a forming polygon at the pole beyond them.

## What Comes Next

The decagon's story is only beginning. Hubble's annual outer-planet survey will keep returning to Saturn, and each year's images will show whether the ten-sided wave continues to sharpen, settles into a stable form like the northern hexagon, or dissolves back into the chaos of the southern jet. Amateur planetary observers — the same community that first noticed the wavy band in 2024 — will keep watching between Hubble's visits. The researchers' paper in *Science Advances* gives modelers new numbers to chew on: a second polygon, a different side count, a live formation event.

There is a poetic symmetry to the timing. Forty-five years ago, the Voyager spacecraft flew past Saturn and, almost as an afterthought, recorded a hexagonal cloud pattern at the north pole that took years to be recognized and decades to be understood. Today, as Saturn makes its brightest appearance of the year in our sky, astronomers announce that they are watching the south pole build its own polygon from scratch — ten sides, still strengthening, still becoming. For once, the universe is not merely showing us a finished puzzle. It is letting us watch the pieces move.

Other eyes will join the watch. The James Webb Space Telescope has been monitoring the northern hexagon's stability and its seasonal color shifts in recent years, and its infrared instruments can probe the layers beneath the visible cloud tops where these jet streams are anchored. Combined with Hubble's annual optical images, that gives scientists a multi-wavelength record of both poles at once — the veteran hexagon and the rookie decagon, side by side, as Saturn's long seasons turn. What Saturn's south pole does next, over the coming observing seasons, may finally answer the question the hexagon has posed since 1981: are these polygons permanent architecture of a gas giant, or weather that comes and goes?

So find a dark spot this October. Look east after sunset for the steady golden point beneath the Great Square of Pegasus, brighter than everything around it. You will not see the decagon itself — it is far too small and far too distant for any telescope on Earth — but you will be looking at the planet where it spins, on the best nights of the year to see it. Somewhere near its south pole, a storm wider than a dozen Earths is tracing out a ten-sided wave, corner by corner, while we watch. The hexagon had forty years alone. It finally has company.
