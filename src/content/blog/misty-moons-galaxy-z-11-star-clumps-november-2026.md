Less than 400 million years after the Big Bang, when the universe was under three percent of its present age, a small galaxy was already building its stars in a way that looks nothing like the orderly spirals and smooth discs astronomers see around them today. Its stars were packed into a handful of giant, dense clumps, each holding roughly ten million times the mass of the Sun, and together those clumps carried at least eighty percent of everything the galaxy had ever made. Now the James Webb Space Telescope has caught that galaxy in the act, and the view may be the closest astronomers have ever come to watching globular clusters — the oldest star swarms in the cosmos — being born.

The galaxy has been nicknamed "Misty Moons", a nod to its appearance in Webb's images: two faint, misty blobs, doubled by the gravity of a massive cluster of galaxies sitting between it and Earth. The discovery was reported on Friday, 6 November 2026, and it comes from a team led by Minami Nakane of the University of Tokyo. Their paper appeared in *The Astrophysical Journal* on 19 October 2026, based on observations from Webb's Near Infrared Camera, known as NIRCam, combined with older images from the Hubble Space Telescope.

What makes Misty Moons remarkable is not its distance, impressive though that is. Astronomers have found galaxies further away. What makes it remarkable is its detail. Thanks to a natural magnifying glass 20 to 30 times stronger than anything a telescope alone could provide, researchers could pick apart the internal structure of a galaxy from the universe's first few hundred million years and measure the masses, ages and share of its stars, clump by clump. No one had resolved a galaxy of this era into its building blocks with this clarity before.

## What Webb Found in the Dark

Misty Moons sits at a redshift of about 11 to 12. Redshift is the measure astronomers use for distance and age in the expanding universe: the higher the number, the further the light has travelled and the earlier the moment it left its source. A redshift of 11 to 12 corresponds to a universe roughly 370 million to 410 million years old, a time when the first stars and galaxies were still assembling and the cosmos was emerging from the long dark age that followed the Big Bang.

Its light has spent more than 13 billion years reaching Earth, stretched by the expansion of space itself from the ultraviolet wavelengths at which it was emitted into the infrared wavelengths that Webb was built to detect. That stretching is exactly why the galaxy vanishes at shorter wavelengths in the images, a tell-tale dropout signature that marks it out as a genuinely distant object rather than a nearer interloper. In Webb's NIRCam images, taken through ten colour filters, Misty Moons appeared as two blobs, labelled ID1 and ID2. They are not two galaxies. They are two views of the same galaxy, its light bent into multiple paths by the gravity of the foreground cluster MACS J0257.1-2325, a massive collection of galaxies, hot gas and dark matter at a redshift of 0.505, roughly halfway between us and the young galaxy.

The team strengthened the case with Hubble images of the same cluster field, taken in three filters as part of the Reionization Lensing Cluster Survey, or RELICS. Combining the two telescopes' views, they built two independent models of how the cluster's gravity bends light, and both models told the same story: Misty Moons should appear as five separate images on the sky. Two of them are bright, magnified roughly 20 to 30 times, and those are the ID1 and ID2 that the team could study in detail. A third candidate image was also found. The remaining two are predicted to be too faint to detect with the current data.

That agreement between two independent lens models matters. Gravitational lensing is powerful, but it is also complicated: to undo the distortion and reconstruct what a lensed galaxy really looks like, astronomers must model the mass of the foreground cluster precisely, and small errors can create false structures. When two different modelling approaches converge on the same prediction, the reconstruction earns real trust.

## A Natural Telescope 20 Times Stronger

The discovery was made in data from the VENUS survey, short for Vast Exploration for Nascent, Unexplored Sources, a large Webb Treasury programme that maps the early universe through the magnifying power of lensing clusters. According to the European Space Agency, VENUS received roughly 300 hours of Webb observing time, led by Seiji Fujimoto of the University of Toronto and Dan Coe of the Space Telescope Science Institute. Its goal is to find hundreds of lensed galaxies from less than 500 million years after the Big Bang.

Seeing anything from the universe's first few hundred million years is hard. The first galaxies were small, faint and far away, and their light has spent more than 13 billion years reaching us. Before Webb began science operations in 2022, astronomers had only a handful of credible candidates from this era. Since then, Webb has found many galaxies from when the universe was only a few hundred million years old, some of them surprisingly bright for their size, hinting at dense star clusters packed into tiny volumes.

Gravitational lensing is the trick that makes the faintest of them visible at all. Predicted by Albert Einstein's general theory of relativity, the effect turns a massive galaxy cluster into a natural telescope: its gravity bends the light of objects behind it, magnifying and sometimes duplicating their images. The magnification is free, requiring no bigger mirror and no longer exposure, and for Misty Moons it was worth a factor of 20 to 30. Without it, the galaxy's internal structure would have remained a single unresolved smudge, if it had been visible at all.

The breakthrough came when the team looked inside those magnified images. The raw images are blurred by the telescope's own optics, so the researchers smoothed the images and subtracted the blur, a technique that sharpens the underlying structure. In ID1 they found four distinct clumps; in ID2 they found three. Once the lensing distortion was removed, both images showed the same structure — the same arrangement of clumps in the same galaxy — confirming that the clumps are real features of Misty Moons and not artefacts of the lens or the telescope.

## The Clumps: Ten Million Suns Each

Each clump in Misty Moons holds about ten million solar masses of young, dust-poor stars, aged only 40 to 70 million years. In the source plane — the galaxy as it would look without the lens — the clumps have effective radii of roughly 10 to 70 parsecs, which is tens to a couple of hundred light-years across. That is small on galactic scales and enormous on the scale of a star cluster: a single clump packs the mass of a dwarf galaxy's worth of stars into a volume not much bigger than a large star-forming cloud in the Milky Way.

Together, the clumps account for at least 80 percent of the galaxy's stellar mass. That number is the heart of the discovery. In galaxies around us today, most stars live in smooth discs or broad haloes, and clusters hold only a small fraction of a galaxy's stellar mass. In Misty Moons, the opposite appears true. The galaxy is, in effect, mostly clump. Early star formation may therefore have been concentrated, bursty and clump-dominated, rather than the steady, spread-out process seen in most galaxies today.

The numbers behind that picture are extreme. The team's analysis found that the galaxy's star formation density is 100 to 10,000 times higher than in local dwarf galaxies, and the researchers say that high rate likely drives the clump-dominated mode of star formation. When gas collapses and forms stars that quickly, it fragments into dense knots instead of settling into a smooth disc. Misty Moons is, by this reading, a galaxy in a hurry — converting its gas into stars in violent, compact bursts within its first few tens of millions of years.

Misty Moons is also a modest galaxy in overall terms. After correcting for the lensing magnification, the team estimates it is a sub-L\* galaxy, with an absolute ultraviolet magnitude of about minus 18, fainter than the characteristic brightness of galaxies at its epoch. Its intrinsic radial surface-brightness profile, once the lensing magnification is removed, is comparable to that of GS-z14-1, another early galaxy, which suggests Misty Moons represents a typical faint galaxy at redshifts above 10 — not a rare monster, but an ordinary citizen of the early universe, caught in unusual detail.

## Could These Be Baby Globular Clusters?

The clumps also look familiar, and that familiarity is what elevates the discovery from a curiosity about one galaxy into a possible answer to a long-standing puzzle. Their sizes and masses resemble local nuclear star clusters and young massive clusters, and the team suggests they may be proto-globular clusters — young versions of the ancient balls of stars that orbit galaxies like our own Milky Way today.

Globular clusters are among the oldest known objects in the universe: dense swarms of hundreds of thousands of stars, most of them more than 10 billion years old. The Milky Way hosts roughly 150 of them, orbiting in a vast halo around the galactic disc. How they formed has long been a puzzle, because their birthplaces are gone — the clusters we see today are survivors, and the galaxies in which they were born have long since merged, dissolved or transformed beyond recognition. Astronomers have debated for decades whether globular clusters were born inside the galaxies they now orbit, in dwarf galaxies later swallowed by bigger ones, or in the dense gas clouds of the very first galactic building blocks.

If the clumps in Misty Moons are young globular clusters caught forming, astronomers finally have a direct view of the process at the right cosmic time. The clumps' ages of 40 to 70 million years fit: they are young enough to still be forming stars, but old enough to have settled into dense, gravitationally bound knots. Their low dust content and young, hot stars fit too, since globular clusters are famously poor in heavy elements — the chemical signature of stars formed from nearly pristine gas, before generations of supernovae had enriched the universe with metals.

Comparing clumpiness across cosmic time supports that picture. "The comparisons of the clumpiness among the observed and simulated galaxies across cosmic time suggest that the galaxies at higher redshift tend to be more clumpy, which is consistent with the observations of many clumpy galaxies at z > 6," the team writes in their paper. The opposite holds for local dwarf galaxies, which are small and faint but comparatively smooth. The team concludes that "high-z clumpy galaxies are likely distinct from the local dwarf galaxies not only in their high SFR surface densities but also in their short, bursty star formation during the early formation phase of the galaxy."

That comparison draws on a growing family of lensed early galaxies that Webb has begun to resolve. The Cosmic Gems arc, a gravitationally lensed galaxy seen when the universe was only about 460 million years old, contains five young massive clusters. The <a href="/blog/jwst-firefly-sparkle-black-hole-seeds-2026/">Firefly Sparkle galaxy</a>, seen about 600 million years after the Big Bang, contains ten. Research published in November 2026 suggests those clusters may also have been the birthplaces of intermediate-mass black holes, the long-sought missing link between stellar-mass black holes and the supermassive giants at galactic centres. Misty Moons now joins that family as the earliest and most clump-dominated example yet, pushing the story back to within 400 million years of the beginning.

## The Black Hole Caveat

There is, however, a caveat that the team states plainly, and it is an important one. The central clumps of Misty Moons are bright and compact enough that the team cannot exclude a contribution from an actively feeding supermassive black hole. Some of the light — and possibly some of the inferred star formation — could come from gas spiralling into a black hole rather than from stars.

If that is the case, the clump masses and the star formation rates derived from the light would need revising downward. But the alternative would be a discovery of a different kind, and arguably just as important: a supermassive black hole already feeding and shining less than 400 million years after the Big Bang. Astronomers already know that black holes of a billion solar masses existed within the first billion years, and how they grew so fast remains one of the biggest open questions in astrophysics. Finding one at redshift 11 or 12, embedded in a clumpy young galaxy, would make Misty Moons a case study in how the first black holes grew alongside the first star clusters.

Either way, the uncertainty is a reason for more observation, not for doubt about the discovery itself. The clumps are real, their masses and ages are measured, and the question is only what fraction of the central light comes from stars and what fraction might come from an accreting black hole. That is precisely the kind of question Webb's spectrographs were built to answer.

## Why One Small Galaxy Matters So Much

Misty Moons matters for galaxy-formation models, which must now reproduce galaxies with most of their stellar mass locked in a few giant clumps within the first 400 million years of cosmic history. Models that produce only smooth, slowly assembling discs would be missing something important about how the first galaxies actually grew. The discovery suggests that the early universe built its galaxies differently — faster, denser and more violently than the calm, gradual assembly often pictured in textbooks.

It also matters because of what it says about the ordinary. Misty Moons is not a record-breaker. The team makes no claim that it is the most distant galaxy ever found, and the news is not the redshift — it is the resolved internal structure. That distinction is worth stating clearly, because the hunt for ever-higher redshifts sometimes obscures the more useful question: not how far away a galaxy is, but what it was actually doing. Misty Moons answers that question for one faint, typical galaxy, and the answer is that it was building stars in giant clusters.

The result also showcases a technique that is only just beginning to pay off. Webb has observed many lensing clusters, and the bright knots now found inside some lensed galaxies — some only a few light-years across, magnified hundreds of times — show how far the method can go. The VENUS survey is still in progress and should identify hundreds of lensed galaxies from the early universe, testing whether clump-dominated star formation was the rule or the exception in the first billion years. Other Webb programmes are also targeting lensing clusters, and each new field adds another chance to catch a galaxy like Misty Moons in the act.

The context is worth remembering. Webb's census of the early universe has repeatedly surprised astronomers: galaxies that are brighter, more massive and more structured than many models predicted for their age. Some of those surprises have been explained by dense star clusters like the ones in Misty Moons, which can make a small galaxy shine far more brightly than its mass alone would suggest. The <a href="/blog/webb-earliest-supernova-grb-250314a-730-million-years/">earliest supernova ever seen</a>, found by Webb in a galaxy just 730 million years after the Big Bang, showed that massive stars were already living fast and dying young in that era. Misty Moons now shows where those stars were being born: not spread thinly across a disc, but packed into dense knots that may still be shining today as globular clusters.

## What Happens Next

Next comes confirmation and closer inspection. The redshift of about 11 to 12 is estimated from how the galaxy's light fades at shorter wavelengths — a photometric redshift, precise enough to place the galaxy in the first 400 million years, but not exact. Spectroscopy could pin down the distance precisely and reveal the chemical fingerprints of its young stars. It could also settle the black hole question by searching for broad emission lines betraying gas spiralling into a supermassive black hole.

If the central clumps prove purely stellar, the proto-globular-cluster case strengthens, and Misty Moons becomes the earliest direct evidence that globular clusters were born in the dense knots of young galaxies. If a black hole is found, Misty Moons becomes a case study in how the first black holes grew alongside the first star clusters — a different answer, but one that would be just as valuable for understanding the early universe. And if spectroscopy revises the redshift downward, the clump-dominated picture would still stand, since the structure itself does not depend on the exact distance.

Either way, the survey that found Misty Moons is far from finished. VENUS aims to map hundreds of lensed galaxies from the first 500 million years, and each one will test whether the clump-dominated mode seen in Misty Moons was common. Astronomers will also want to look for the descendants of these clumps: if the knots in Misty Moons really are proto-globular clusters, their modern counterparts should be orbiting galaxies like the Milky Way today, carrying the chemical signatures of stars born in the universe's first few hundred million years. The star-forming regions Webb has imaged closer to home, such as the <a href="/blog/webb-n159-star-forming-region-large-magellanic-cloud/">N159 region in the Large Magellanic Cloud</a>, show the same physics at work on a smaller scale — dense gas collapsing into clusters — but Misty Moons shows it at the beginning, when the universe itself was young.

For now, Misty Moons stands as a small, misty pair of blobs carrying an outsized message. Less than half a billion years after the Big Bang, one galaxy was already building its stars in giant, dense clusters that may still shine today as globular clusters, orbiting quietly in the haloes of galaxies like our own. Its light left when the universe was a newborn and arrived only now, after a journey of more than 13 billion years, bent and doubled by a cluster of galaxies that served as a telescope no engineer could ever build. And in that doubled, magnified light, astronomers could see something no one had seen before: the first galaxies were not smooth, patient discs. They were fireworks — bursting into being in a handful of brilliant knots, in the dark, before the universe had even learned how to be a galaxy at all.
