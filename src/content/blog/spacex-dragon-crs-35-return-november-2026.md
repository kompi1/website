A spacecraft packed with some of the most closely watched science ever flown to orbit is waiting for the weather to let it come home. NASA and SpaceX stood down from the first planned departure of the SpaceX Dragon cargo capsule from the International Space Station on Wednesday, November 18, after forecasters judged the splashdown zone off the California coast unsafe, and the mission's return has now been retargeted for no earlier than Friday, November 20.

The delay is a matter of hours and days, not a problem with the spacecraft. Dragon is healthy, its cargo is sealed and stowed, and the station crew has already finished loading it. What stands between the capsule and Earth is the Pacific Ocean itself: the wind, the waves and the cloud ceiling over the recovery area, which must be calm enough for four parachutes to do their work and for recovery crews to pull a capsule full of temperature-sensitive biology out of the water safely.

When Dragon does leave, it will close out SpaceX's 35th commercial resupply mission for NASA, a flight that began on October 13, 2026, when a Falcon 9 rocket lifted off from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. Two days later the capsule arrived at the station carrying more than 6,300 pounds of crew supplies, science investigations and equipment. It has been docked to the orbiting laboratory for more than a month, and the return leg is the half of the mission that researchers on the ground have been waiting for, because Dragon is the only cargo vehicle in regular service that can bring large amounts of science back intact instead of burning it up on re-entry.

The manifest reads like a tour of modern medicine's hardest problems. Aboard are a 3D-bioprinted blood vessel construct that scientists used to study vascular aging and cardiovascular disease, frozen tumor organoids that were treated in orbit as researchers hunt for better cancer therapies, frozen microbial samples that may explain how antibiotic-resistant bacteria survive and evolve in space, and a lighting system that has spent more than three years aboard the station testing whether carefully tuned light can protect astronauts' sleep and well-being on long missions. Thousands of pounds of cargo in all are coming home, and the most valuable items are measured in grams, not tons.

## A weather call, not a spacecraft problem

NASA announced the stand-down in the early hours of Wednesday, November 18, in a short update on its space station blog. The agency said NASA and SpaceX were "standing down from Wednesday's Dragon undocking opportunity from the International Space Station due to unfavorable weather conditions at the splashdown sites off the coast of California." No problem with the spacecraft or the station was mentioned, and none has been reported since. The capsule was scheduled to undock from the space-facing port of the Harmony module at 11:05 a.m. EST on Wednesday, with NASA's live coverage due to begin at 10:45 a.m. EST. Instead, the hatch stayed shut and Dragon stayed docked.

The decision followed a weather review at noon EST on Wednesday. Mission teams had been watching the Pacific forecast for days, because the return timeline is built backwards from the ocean. Dragon's splashdown had been targeted for no earlier than 1:50 a.m. EST on Thursday, November 19, about 10:50 p.m. PST on Wednesday evening. A capsule descending under parachutes cannot choose a calmer patch of sea once it has committed to re-entry, and the recovery ships that meet it need workable seas to hoist a capsule weighing several tons out of the water and get its cargo into cold storage quickly. When the forecast for the recovery area failed to meet the criteria, the only safe option was to wait.

That is how Dragon returns are designed to work. Unlike a crewed capsule, which can tolerate a somewhat wider set of conditions because its crew can assist with egress, a cargo Dragon's return is judged almost entirely on the recovery weather and the condition of its payload. Some of the most temperature-sensitive samples ride home in portable freezers and incubators, and every hour of delay is weighed against the risk of a rough recovery. NASA said teams would continue to monitor conditions and identify the next available return opportunity, and within a day the agency published a revised plan.

The updated advisory, released on November 18, moves the departure to no earlier than Friday, November 20. Pending weather conditions, Dragon will undock from Harmony's space-facing port at 12:05 p.m. EST, following a command sent by ground controllers at SpaceX's facility in Hawthorne, California. NASA's live coverage of the undocking and departure will begin at 11:45 a.m. EST. Splashdown off the California coast is now targeted for no earlier than 5:04 p.m. EST on Saturday, November 21, which is 2:04 p.m. PST. NASA will not stream the splashdown itself but will post updates on its space station blog as the recovery unfolds.

For readers in Indonesia, the revised times fall in the small hours and the early morning: undocking at 12:05 a.m. WIB on Saturday, November 21, and splashdown at 5:04 a.m. WIB on Sunday, November 22. The original Wednesday attempt would have undocked at 11:05 p.m. WIB on November 18, with splashdown at 1:50 p.m. WIB on Thursday, November 19. The two-day slip is the price of a Pacific forecast that refused to cooperate, and it is a reminder that even in an era of reusable rockets and autonomous docking, the last step of a space mission is still negotiated with the weather.

## What is coming home, and why it matters

The return cargo is the reason this mission exists. A cargo ship that only delivers supplies is a truck; a cargo ship that brings science home is a laboratory with a return ticket. Dragon's ability to survive re-entry and splash down intact, carrying frozen samples in powered freezers, is what makes experiments like the ones aboard CRS-35 possible at all. Here is what NASA says is in the hold.

The headline item is a 3D-bioprinted blood vessel construct, a laboratory-grown piece of vascular tissue that scientists used to study how blood vessels age and how cardiovascular disease develops, both in space and on Earth. The construct was printed layer by layer from living cells, giving researchers a structure that behaves more like a real blood vessel than a flat layer of cells in a dish ever could. In microgravity, the cells grow and organize differently, and the absence of gravity-driven settling lets scientists see processes that are masked on Earth. The sample is returning so that researchers can examine it in detail, comparing its structure with identical constructs kept on the ground. Cardiovascular disease remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, and anything that clarifies how vessels stiffen and degrade with age has value far beyond spaceflight.

Alongside it are frozen tumor organoids, miniature three-dimensional clusters of tumor tissue that were treated aboard the station. Organoids are one of the most promising tools in modern cancer research because they preserve much of a real tumor's structure and behavior while remaining small enough to experiment on. Treating them in orbit, where cells grow in three dimensions without the flattening effect of gravity, may reveal how cancer cells respond to drugs differently than they do in a laboratory dish. The organoids were frozen for the trip home, and once they reach researchers on Earth, the team will analyze them to identify new ways to improve cancer treatments and advance personalized medicine, matching therapies to the specific biology of a patient's tumor rather than to an average.

A third set of samples carries frozen microbes, part of an investigation into how antibiotic-resistant bacteria survive, adapt and evolve in space. The question is practical as well as scientific: astronauts on long missions will live in closed environments where microbes travel with them, and a bacterial strain that becomes more virulent or more drug-resistant in space is a direct threat to crew health. The same research has a mirror on Earth, where antibiotic resistance is already one of the most serious public health challenges of the century. By studying how these organisms change under the combined stresses of microgravity and space radiation, scientists hope to understand the mechanisms of resistance itself, and that understanding could inform new approaches to fighting infections in hospitals as well as in orbit.

The fourth returning item is not a biological sample at all, but it has been aboard the station far longer than Dragon has. A lighting system that spent more than three years on the International Space Station is coming home after researchers tested its potential to support astronauts' sleep and well-being during long-duration missions. Light is one of the strongest cues that sets the human circadian clock, and on a station that sees sixteen sunrises a day, that clock is under constant assault. The experiment explored whether carefully designed lighting could help crews sleep better and stay healthier, a question that matters enormously for the months-long journeys to the Moon and Mars that NASA is planning. The hardware itself will be studied on Earth, and the data it collected over three years will inform the lighting design of future spacecraft and habitats.

## CRS-35 return timeline

- **October 13, 2026** — Falcon 9 lifts off from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral carrying the CRS-35 Dragon.
- **October 15, 2026** — Dragon docks with the International Space Station, delivering more than 6,300 pounds of supplies, science investigations and equipment.
- **Mid-October to mid-November 2026** — The station crew runs the mission's science, including the blood vessel, tumor organoid and microbial experiments, and loads the return cargo.
- **November 18, 2026, 1:44 a.m. EST** — NASA announces a stand-down from the day's undocking opportunity because of unfavorable weather at the splashdown sites off California.
- **November 18, 2026, 12:00 p.m. EST** — Mission teams hold a scheduled weather review to assess the next opportunity.
- **November 18, 2026** — NASA publishes a revised advisory: departure no earlier than Friday, November 20.
- **November 20, 2026, 11:45 a.m. EST** — NASA's live coverage of undocking begins.
- **November 20, 2026, 12:05 p.m. EST** — Dragon is targeted to undock from Harmony's space-facing port on a command from SpaceX's Hawthorne facility.
- **November 21, 2026, 5:04 p.m. EST** — Splashdown targeted off the California coast, no earlier than this time, weather permitting.

The timeline carries a lesson about how spaceflight schedules actually work. The dates that matter most are rarely the ones announced first. A launch date is a plan; a splashdown date is a negotiation between orbital mechanics and the ocean. Dragon's return was always going to happen in a window measured in days, and the Pacific's refusal to cooperate on Wednesday simply moved the negotiation to Friday.

## Why the ocean gets a vote

Dragon does not land on a runway. It falls out of orbit, brakes through the atmosphere with a heat shield, deploys drogue parachutes and then four main parachutes, and settles into the Pacific at a speed a crew can walk away from and a recovery ship can work with. Every part of that sequence assumes a sea state that lets a ship hold position beside a floating capsule, a crane lift several tons of spacecraft and seawater out of the swell, and technicians open the hatch and transfer freezers to a waiting aircraft without delay.

The splashdown sites off California are chosen for their relatively predictable weather, but the Pacific in November is not a tame place. Swell, wind and low cloud can each independently rule a site out, and NASA and SpaceX maintain multiple recovery zones so that a forecast can be chased from one to another. On Wednesday, none of them met the criteria. The agency has not published the specific numbers behind the call, and it does not need to: the standard is that the recovery must be safe for the capsule, the cargo and the people who meet it, and anything short of that is a no-go.

There is also a quieter reason the weather matters so much on this flight. The return cargo is not inert. Frozen organoids and microbial samples must stay cold, and the freezers that keep them cold have a finite battery life once Dragon's own power is gone. A recovery that is delayed by rough seas is not merely uncomfortable; it is a slow threat to the science itself. That is why the recovery team is staged and ready before the capsule ever leaves the station, and why NASA posts updates on its space station blog rather than streaming the splashdown: the operation is a race against the clock that begins the moment the capsule touches the water.

The delay also illustrates a difference between cargo and crew returns. When astronauts come home, the weather criteria are tighter still, because people must climb out of a bobbing capsule and onto a ship. A cargo Dragon has no crew to protect, but it carries irreplaceable samples, and the recovery team treats those samples with something close to the same care. In both cases, the ocean is the final member of the flight team, and it does not take orders.

## The mission behind the return

CRS-35 is the 35th commercial resupply mission SpaceX has flown for NASA, and the program's longevity is itself part of the story. When the first Dragon cargo ship reached the station in 2012, the idea that a private company would routinely deliver supplies to orbit, and bring science home, was still new. Fourteen years later, the routine is so established that a two-day weather delay is the news, not the flight itself. The capsule that launched on October 13 is part of a fleet that has turned resupply into a scheduled service, and the story of its arrival is told in our report on <a href="/blog/spacex-crs-35-dragon-docking/">SpaceX Dragon Docks at the Space Station With the Final Solar Wings — and a Cargo Hold Full of Medical Promise</a>.

The cargo Dragon delivered on this flight included the final two roll-out solar array panels for the station, completing a multi-year upgrade to the station's power generation that began in 2021. Those arrays were installed during spacewalks that finished the job of modernizing a power system designed decades ago, and they are the reason the station can keep supporting a growing research workload even as it ages. The link between the arrays and this mission is direct: without the power they provide, the freezers, incubators and lighting rigs that make experiments like the ones aboard CRS-35 possible would have nowhere to run. Readers who want the full story of that upgrade can revisit our report on <a href="/blog/iss-final-solar-array-spacewalk-october-29/">the station's final solar array spacewalk</a>, which covered the spacewalkers who finished the installation.

The science delivered alongside those arrays was ambitious in its own right. Hardware for manufacturing artificial retinas in microgravity, 3D-printed heart tissue models, materials for studying how glass forms without gravity, and brain organoids all flew up on CRS-35, joining a research program that treats the station as a national laboratory. Not all of it returns on this flight; some of it will stay aboard for months, and some of it is the work of years. But the samples that are coming home now are the ones whose results cannot wait, and the researchers who prepared them have been counting the days since Dragon docked.

The station itself is the context that makes all of this possible. People have lived and worked continuously aboard the International Space Station for more than 26 years, and the research they conduct there is impossible to replicate on Earth, because gravity is not a variable that can be switched off in a laboratory.

The station helps NASA understand and overcome the challenges of human spaceflight, expands commercial opportunities in low Earth orbit, and builds the foundation for long-duration missions to the Moon and Mars. That is the agency's own description of the station's purpose, and CRS-35 is a working example of it: a commercial spacecraft, a government laboratory, and a set of medical experiments that could matter to anyone who has ever waited for a cancer test result.

The human side of the station's current chapter is worth noting, too. NASA astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney are living and working aboard the station now, and they are the crew who will capture the next cargo ship to arrive, using the Canadarm2 robotic arm. Watkins also commands the station's current crew, a story we covered in our report on <a href="/blog/crew-13-record-flight-iss-watkins-command/">Crew-13's record flight and Watkins taking command</a>. The loading of Dragon's return cargo, the sealing of its freezers and the preparation of its samples were their work, done in the hours between the station's other duties, and the safe return of that cargo is, in a real sense, the completion of their part of the mission.

## What happens after splashdown

If the Friday undocking and Saturday splashdown go ahead as planned, the operation will move fast. A SpaceX recovery team will retrieve the capsule from the Pacific, and the most time-sensitive cargo, the frozen organoids and microbial specimens in particular, will be transferred quickly to researchers. The freezers that kept them cold through re-entry will be plugged into ground power, the samples will be packed for onward transport, and within hours they will be in laboratories where the analysis can begin.

The lighting system will take a different path. After three years in orbit, the hardware itself is an artifact of the experiment, and engineers will examine it for wear, degradation and any surprises that three years of continuous operation in space produced. The data it recorded, about how its light affected the crew's sleep and alertness, will be matched against the crew's own records and health data, and the findings will feed into the design of lighting for the lunar and Martian habitats that NASA is planning under its Artemis and Moon Base programs.

The blood vessel construct and the tumor organoids will go to the scientists who designed them, and the results will arrive in papers and conference presentations over the coming months and years. That is the normal pace of science, and it is worth remembering when a splashdown makes the news: the splashdown is the end of the flight, not the end of the research. The experiments that flew on CRS-35 were designed on Earth, run in orbit, and will be understood on Earth, and the value of the mission will be measured in what those researchers publish, not in how smoothly the capsule landed.

There is also a next mission already on the calendar. NASA, Northrop Grumman and SpaceX are targeting no earlier than 10:47 a.m. EST on Sunday, November 29, for the next launch to the station, the Northrop Grumman CRS-25 mission. A Falcon 9 will carry the Cygnus XL spacecraft, loaded with about 11,000 pounds of cargo, from Space Launch Complex 40 in Florida, and the spacecraft, named the S.S. James "Jim" A. Lovell in honor of the former NASA astronaut who flew Gemini VII, Gemini XII, Apollo 8 and Apollo 13, is scheduled to be captured by Canadarm2 on Tuesday, December 1. The contrast is instructive: Cygnus burns up on re-entry, so everything it carries is one-way, while Dragon is the vehicle that brings the science home. The two spacecraft are complements, not competitors, and the station's research program needs both.

The station's own future frames all of this. The orbiting laboratory will not fly forever, and planning for its eventual retirement and replacement by commercial stations is already under way. For now, though, the station is still working at full capacity, and a cargo ship full of cancer research waiting on the Pacific weather is proof of it.

## A short delay in a long story

It is tempting to read a two-day delay as a setback, but the history of the space station suggests the opposite. The station has been occupied for more than 26 years, and in that time it has absorbed delays far longer than two days: launches scrubbed, dockings postponed, spacewalks rescheduled, and entire missions replanned around weather, hardware and orbital mechanics. The program endures because it is built to absorb exactly this kind of interruption. A capsule that waits an extra two days in orbit loses nothing; a capsule that splashes down into a storm could lose everything it carries.

The researchers waiting for Dragon's cargo will understand that. They have waited months, in some cases years, for their samples to fly, to be treated in orbit, and to come home. Two more days is a small price for a safe recovery, and the revised timeline gives them a firm date to plan around: undocking on Friday, splashdown on Saturday, and samples in the laboratory by early next week.

When Dragon finally leaves the station, NASA will stream the departure live, beginning at 11:45 a.m. EST on Friday. The undocking itself will be a quiet moment, a command sent from Hawthorne, California, and a slow drift apart at a speed a person could walk. Then the capsule will fire its thrusters, drop out of orbit, and fall toward a patch of Pacific Ocean that forecasters have finally declared calm enough. The splashdown will not be televised, but the space station blog will carry the news, and somewhere in a laboratory a freezer will be waiting.

For a mission that began with a rocket launch in October and ends with a splash in November, the most important part was never the flight itself. It was the cargo: blood vessels printed from living cells, tumors grown in miniature, bacteria that might teach us how to fight drug resistance, and a light that spent three years learning how to help astronauts sleep. They went to space to answer questions that cannot be answered on Earth. Now they are coming home, two days late and exactly on time, to give us the answers.
