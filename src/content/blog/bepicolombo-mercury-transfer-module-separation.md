On the afternoon of September 3, in a control room in Darmstadt, Germany, a roomful of engineers watched a signal crawl across their screens and broke into applause. At 15:49 Central European Summer Time, ESA's mission control team received confirmation that BepiColombo — the joint European-Japanese mission to Mercury — had successfully separated from its Mercury Transfer Module, the cruise stage that had carried it across the inner Solar System for nearly eight years. It was, as the agency put it, the first step of the mission's long-awaited arrival at the smallest and least-explored planet in our Solar System.

The moment had been eight years in the making. Since lifting off from Kourou on October 20, 2018, aboard an Ariane 5 rocket, BepiColombo had travelled 9.9 billion kilometres, threading a gravitational obstacle course of nine planetary flybys — one of Earth, two of Venus, six of Mercury — while its ion engines fired for thousands of hours to gradually reshape its orbit around the Sun. The Transfer Module, known as the MTM, provided the solar-electric propulsion and much of the electrical power for that entire voyage. Now, its job done, it was cast off to drift away, and the two science orbiters it had been shepherding were left to complete the final approach on their own.

What follows is one of the most operationally challenging arrival sequences any space agency has ever attempted. Over the coming weeks, Mercury's feeble gravity will slowly capture the remaining spacecraft stack; in November, the mission will be weakly captured into a looping polar orbit around the planet; and in December, the two orbiters — ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, nicknamed Mio — will separate from each other and manoeuvre into their final science orbits. If all goes to plan, BepiColombo will become the first mission in history to place two spacecraft in orbit around Mercury simultaneously, with full science operations beginning in 2027.

## Eight Years, 9.9 Billion Kilometres

To appreciate what was jettisoned on September 3, you have to appreciate the journey. Getting to Mercury is brutally difficult — not because it is far, but because the Sun's gravity well is so deep. A spacecraft falling inward from Earth picks up enormous speed, and shedding that energy to settle into orbit around the innermost planet takes years of careful braking. BepiColombo's answer was a combination of gravity assists and patient, low-thrust ion propulsion: fly past planets to steal or shed momentum, and let efficient electric thrusters do the fine work in between.

The itinerary reads like a grand tour of the inner Solar System. After launch in October 2018, BepiColombo swung past Earth in April 2020, then past Venus twice — in October 2020 and August 2021 — each encounter bending its trajectory closer to the Sun. Then came six flybys of Mercury itself, from October 2021 through January 2025, each one bleeding off a little more energy and lining the spacecraft up for capture. Along the way, the mission turned its cameras and instruments on its targets of opportunity: the fourth Mercury flyby, in September 2024, gave scientists their first clear view of the planet's south pole, imaging craters in the polar region; the fifth, in December 2024, made BepiColombo the first spacecraft ever to observe Mercury in mid-infrared light; and the sixth and final flyby, in January 2025, swooped just 165 kilometres above the surface — lower than the orbit the science mission will eventually use — photographing permanently shadowed craters near the north pole that may harbour water ice.

The cruise was not without drama. In April 2024, during a scheduled manoeuvre, engineers discovered that the spacecraft's thrusters could not operate at full power. The investigation that followed led ESA, in September 2024, to unveil a revised trajectory that compensated for the reduced thrust by adding eleven months to the cruise — pushing the expected arrival from December 2025 to November 2026. It was a sobering reminder that deep-space missions must be designed to absorb failure, and a testament to the trajectory designers in Darmstadt who re-plotted a 9.9-billion-kilometre voyage on the fly.

By June 2026, the long burn was over. On June 15 at 13:24 UTC, BepiColombo switched off its solar-electric propulsion for the final time. The ion engines that had fired, on and off, for the better part of a decade fell silent. All that remained was to discard the stage that had carried them — and to let Mercury do the rest.

## What the Transfer Module Did

The Mercury Transfer Module was never meant to visit Mercury. It was a cruise stage, pure and simple: a box of propulsion and power whose entire purpose was to deliver its precious cargo and then get out of the way.

Its heart was a solar-electric propulsion system — ion thrusters that accelerate xenon propellant to enormous exhaust velocities using electrical power from large solar wings. Ion engines produce only a whisper of thrust, measured in fractions of a newton, but they can fire for years on end, and their fuel efficiency is an order of magnitude better than chemical rockets. For a mission that needed to shed the vast energy of falling toward the Sun, that efficiency was indispensable. The MTM also supplied electrical power to the whole stack during the cruise, its solar arrays drinking in the increasingly intense sunlight as the spacecraft spiralled inward.

The module even gave the mission some of its most memorable imagery. Mounted on the MTM were small monitoring cameras — the M-CAMs — that spent the cruise snapping "selfie" pictures of the spacecraft stack against the blackness of space, footage that ESA later stitched into a time-lapse of the eight-year journey. Those cameras are gone with the module now, but the images remain an enduring record of one of the longest planetary cruises ever flown.

Shedding the MTM was not just symbolic; it was a physical necessity. The transfer stage is dead weight for the orbital phase, and its large solar wings would have been a liability in Mercury's brutal thermal environment, where sunlight is up to eleven times more intense than at Earth. From here on, the two orbiters fly on their own power and their own chemical thrusters.

## The Arrival Sequence: Letting Mercury Take Over

With the transfer module gone, BepiColombo has entered what ESA calls the Mercury arrival phase — and it is unlike any orbit insertion most missions attempt. Rather than a single dramatic braking burn, the spacecraft will be "weakly captured" by Mercury's gravity in November 2026, eased into a vast, looping polar orbit whose farthest point stretches 178,000 kilometres from the planet. From there, a series of small chemical manoeuvres will gradually tighten the orbit, and in December the two orbiters will finally separate from each other and descend toward their dedicated science orbits.

The reason for this gentle approach is physics. Mercury is tiny — barely larger than Earth's Moon — and its gravity is correspondingly weak. Slamming on the brakes with a big burn is not really an option for a spacecraft arriving with this much residual energy; instead, navigators must coax the trajectory into the planet's grasp with exquisite precision. ESA describes the arrival phase as one of the most complex planetary arrival sequences it has ever attempted, and the operations team at ESOC spent much of June running full-scale arrival simulations to rehearse every contingency.

For the scientists who have waited since 2018, the timeline from here is tantalizingly short: capture in November, orbiter separation in December, and the start of routine science operations in early 2027. After nearly a decade of travel, Mercury is suddenly, finally, close.

## Two Orbiters, One Destination

What makes BepiColombo unique is that it is really two missions flying as one. Once the stack breaks apart in December, ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter — known as Mio — will go their separate ways.

The MPO is the geologist. Flying a low, tight orbit, it will map Mercury's surface in detail, probe its composition, measure its topography, and investigate the planet's strangely oversized iron core and its magnetic field — a field that, remarkably for such a small world, is global and Earth-like in structure. Understanding how Mercury generated and sustains that field is one of the mission's central mysteries, with implications for how all rocky planets, including Earth, evolved.

Mio is the space physicist. From a more eccentric orbit that carries it far from the planet and deep into its magnetosphere, it will study the violent interaction between Mercury's magnetic field and the solar wind — a miniature, sped-up version of the processes that drive space weather at Earth. Because Mercury sits so close to the Sun, its magnetosphere is small, dynamic and constantly battered, making it a natural laboratory for plasma physics.

Together, the pair will do something no single spacecraft could: observe Mercury's surface and its space environment simultaneously, linking cause and effect. When the solar wind slams into the magnetosphere, Mio will watch the impact while the MPO watches the surface respond. It is the first time two spacecraft will operate in concert around the innermost planet, and the choreography required — two agencies, two control centres, one tightly coupled science plan — has been years in the making.

## The Instruments Waking Up

Even before the orbiters reach their science orbits, their instruments are stirring. The weeks since the MTM separation have brought a string of "first light" milestones that have the science teams buzzing.

On September 14, the University of Bern team successfully activated the laser of the BELA altimeter for the first time in space — and for the first time since 2017. BELA, the BepiColombo Laser Altimeter, is the heaviest instrument riding on the MPO: a powerful laser rangefinder that will measure Mercury's topography with metre-scale precision, mapping mountains, valleys and crater depths across the entire planet. Firing its laser in the cold of space, after years of dormancy, was a moment years of testing had built toward.

A day later, on September 15, the MPO's multipurpose imaging system SIMBIO-SYS captured its first light, confirming its cameras and spectrometers are healthy and ready. And watching over the surface chemistry is MIXS, the Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer — the only UK-led instrument on the mission, developed by a consortium led by the University of Leicester. MIXS will analyse the composition of Mercury's surface by measuring the fluorescent X-rays that surface rocks emit when stimulated by high-energy solar X-rays, working in tandem with its companion SIXS instrument that monitors the incoming solar flux. It will even observe the planet's nightside, where X-rays generated by magnetospheric particles bombarding the surface reveal the invisible connection between Mercury's space environment and its geology.

Each of these milestones is a small rehearsal for 2027, when the full instrument suite — eleven instruments on the MPO, five on Mio — begins routine operations. The science questions waiting are profound: Why is Mercury so dense, with an iron core that fills most of its interior? How did volatiles like water end up in permanently shadowed polar craters on a world where daytime temperatures top 400°C? What does Mercury's magnetic field reveal about the ancient dynamos of rocky planets? After Mariner 10's brief flybys in the 1970s and NASA's MESSENGER orbiter (2011–2015), BepiColombo is humanity's third act at Mercury — and by far its most ambitious.

## Why Mercury Is Worth the Trouble

It is fair to ask why any space agency would spend eight years and billions of kilometres to reach a scorched rock barely larger than Earth's Moon. The answer is that Mercury is the most extreme and least understood of the rocky planets — and extremes test theories.

Start with its density. Mercury is the second-densest planet in the Solar System after Earth, yet it is far smaller, which means it cannot be compressing itself under its own weight the way Earth does. The only explanation is an enormous iron core that fills most of the planet's interior, wrapped in a thin silicate shell. How a planet ends up like that — whether a giant impact stripped away its outer layers early in its history, or whether it formed that way — is one of planetary science's great unsolved puzzles, and BepiColombo's gravity and composition measurements are designed to settle it.

Then there is the magnetic field. Mercury generates a global, dipole-dominated magnetic field, much like Earth's but far weaker — a surprise, because dynamo theory says such a small core should have frozen solid and gone quiet billions of years ago. Something is keeping Mercury's dynamo alive, and whatever it is will rewrite textbooks about how rocky planets work.

Add the exosphere: Mercury's "atmosphere" is so thin it barely qualifies, a shimmering envelope of atoms knocked off the surface by solar radiation and micrometeorites. Studying how the solar wind sputters material from the surface into space — and how some of it escapes entirely — tells scientists about atmospheric loss on every rocky world, including the early Earth and exoplanets hugging their stars.

And then there are the polar craters. Despite daytime temperatures above 400°C, radar and MESSENGER data showed that permanently shadowed craters near Mercury's poles hold deposits of water ice — delivered by comets and asteroids, preserved for aeons in cold traps that never see the Sun. BepiColombo will map those deposits in detail, investigating how volatiles survive in the most hostile thermal environment in the Solar System.

NASA's MESSENGER orbiter, which operated at Mercury from 2011 to 2015, transformed the planet from a blurry Mariner 10 snapshot into a real world. BepiColombo is built to go much further: two spacecraft, sixteen instruments between them, making simultaneous measurements no single orbiter could attempt. If MESSENGER introduced us to Mercury, BepiColombo intends to explain it.

## An Ending Eight Years in the Making

There is a particular poignancy to discarding a spacecraft stage that worked flawlessly for eight years. The Mercury Transfer Module never got a name that captured the public imagination; it was plumbing and thrust, not science. But without its ion engines patiently pushing for thousands of hours, without its solar wings powering the stack through the inner Solar System, BepiColombo would never have reached this point. Thousands of people followed the separation live on ESA's webcast as the signal confirming the jettison arrived in Darmstadt — a quiet, technical moment that marked the end of the cruise and the beginning of the destination.

What remains is the payoff. In November, Mercury's gravity takes hold. In December, two orbiters go their separate ways around the smallest planet. And in 2027, after one of the longest and most intricate journeys in the history of planetary exploration, BepiColombo finally gets to work — two spacecraft, two space agencies, one scorched and mysterious world, and a decade of questions waiting for answers.
