Staring back at us from some 5,000 light-years away, deep in the constellation Gemini, is a lion. Its face is a bubble of glowing hydrogen gas, hollowed and sculpted from the inside by the corpse of a dying star. Its mane is a thick ring of dust, lit up from within by that same stellar remnant — except in a few places, where clumps of dust have managed to survive, trailing comet-like tails as the wind of the dead star tears past them. At the center, like a button nose, sits the star itself: a white dwarf, a cinder barely larger than Earth, searing the whole scene with invisible ultraviolet radiation.

This is NGC 2392, the Lion Nebula — and NASA's James Webb Space Telescope has just given us the most revealing look at it ever captured. The portrait, released under the title "Lion Nebula Roars to Life With NASA's Webb" and produced from Webb's Near Infrared Camera (NIRCam) and Mid Infrared Instrument (MIRI) working in tandem, shows the planetary nebula as a snapshot in time: a few thousand years of sculpting by a dying star, frozen in a process astronomers say will end in total dispersal in about 10,000 years. In astronomical terms, that is the blink of an eye — a cosmic monument caught mid-roar, already beginning to fade.

The images are remarkable on their own terms. What makes them matter to science is subtler: in infrared light, Webb can separate the dust that is being destroyed from the dust that is surviving, mapping the thermal battlefield inside the nebula clump by clump. That map is, among other things, a glimpse of how the solid building blocks of future planets are made — and destroyed — in the death throes of ordinary stars. Including, one day, our own.

## A Familiar Face, 5,000 Light-Years Away

NGC 2392 — also catalogued as Caldwell 39 — sits at right ascension 07:29:10.76 and declination +20:54:42.49, in the northern constellation Gemini, about 5,000 light-years from Earth. The Webb composite spans roughly 1.8 arcminutes across, which at that distance translates to about 2.6 light-years — a tiny patch of sky containing an object on the order of a light-year across, seen from a distance that light itself takes five millennia to cross.

What the image shows, in NASA's description, is a near-circular nebula at center that reads unmistakably like the head of a male lion. The "face" is an oval of purple-pink cavernous bubbles and shell-like rings, with two small, wide arcs near the top suggesting ears. Radiating outward from the face is a thick, consistent ring of purple-blue material — the mane. Up close, the face looks cloudy where gas churns, while the edges of the mane resolve into clumps of dust with comet-like tails, their tails pointing away from the center like smoke streaming off a fire. And sprinkled behind it all, visible through and around the nebula, are the real background of the universe: distant galaxies, some yellow-orange points of light, some with visible spiral structure, plus foreground stars with Webb's characteristic eight-point diffraction spikes.

It is, to be clear, a physical coincidence that it looks like a lion — the particular way this star shed its outer layers, and the geometry of how its radiation is now reshaping them. But that coincidence has a scientific dividend: the symmetry and clarity of the structure make NGC 2392 one of the best laboratories anywhere for studying how a dying star's wind and radiation shape a planetary nebula.

The name "planetary nebula" is itself a historical accident. These objects have nothing to do with planets. When early observers saw small, round, glowing greenish discs through their telescopes, the objects resembled distant planets, and the name stuck. What a planetary nebula really is: the ejected envelope of a star much like the Sun, sloughed off near the end of its life, now being lit up and carved by the star's exposed core. NGC 2392 is one of the finest examples known — and one of the nearest.

## The Data Behind the Portrait

The new images were built from Webb observations taken on November 19, 2025, under proposal 9548 led by M. Garcia Marin, and released in August 2026. Five filters sampled the nebula across the infrared spectrum: F115W and F335M in the near-infrared, the narrowband filters F187N and F212N that pick out specific glowing gases, and F1000W in the mid-infrared. Image processing was done by Alyssa Pagan of the Space Telescope Science Institute, and the release carries the credit line of NASA, the European Space Agency, the Canadian Space Agency, and STScI.

The color is not what a human eye would see — it is a code. In the composite, blue comes from F115W, cyan from F187N, yellow from F212N, orange from F335M, and red from F1000W. Each color is a different infrared wavelength rendered visible, so the hues trace different temperatures, densities, and chemical compositions. The blue tones mark the hottest, most energetic features; the reds trace the cooler, dusty structures.

That dual-instrument approach is what makes the portrait more than a pretty picture. NIRCam maps the fine structure of the gas in near-infrared light; MIRI adds the mid-infrared view, which is sensitive to thermal emission — the heat glow of dust at different temperatures. By combining them, astronomers can do something no visible-light telescope could: distinguish the dust that is being actively cooked apart from the dust that sits cool enough, in the shadows cast by other dust, to survive.

## The Nose, the Face, and the Mane

At the center of the face, bright and unmistakable with eight diffraction spikes, sits the white dwarf — the stellar corpse doing the sculpting. In the lion metaphor, NASA describes it as the button nose. Physically, it is the exposed core of the star, collapsed down to roughly the size of Earth, and its surface temperature is estimated at around 40,000 to 50,000 Kelvin — staggeringly hot, radiating furious ultraviolet light that floods the surrounding nebula.

"The central dying star is so hot that its radiation is 'cooking' everything from the inside and producing a bubble of ionized hydrogen gas as it does," NASA explains. That cooking is not metaphorical. Ultraviolet photons stream outward from the white dwarf, stripping electrons from hydrogen atoms and heating the gas, while a fast wind of particles — moving at immense speeds — slams into the slower-moving material the star shed earlier. The collision excavates caverns, rings, and tendrils. It is the same basic physics that shapes every planetary nebula, played out here with unusual clarity.

The face itself is the lion's most dynamic feature: a bubble of ionized hydrogen gas filled with cavernous shells and rings, threaded with tendrils that are expanding and destroying dust upon encounter. Where the tendrils sweep through, dust is obliterated; the gas glows purple-pink in the composite's palette, a visible record of the demolition in progress.

Then there is the mane — and the mane is where the story gets interesting. It is not, as it might look, an outer ring of pristine material. It is the inside of a dust shell, illuminated by the central star's core. And within it, not everything is being destroyed. Compact clumps of dust — rendered red and orange in the composite — have managed to survive so far, and they are doing more than surviving: they are protecting the material that lies behind them, casting shadows in the radiation field. Behind those shadows, additional clumps of molecular hydrogen glow yellow. The comet-like tails at the mane's edge are the signature of this standoff — material streaming away downstream of the dense knots, exactly as it should if dense clumps were sheltering the gas in their wake while everything unshielded gets blown apart.

## Cooking From the Inside Out

There is a reason the word "cooking" fits so well. The white dwarf at the center of NGC 2392 is not gently fading. It is pumping out radiation so intense that it dominates every structure around it, and it has been doing so for thousands of years. The nebula around it is thought to be roughly 10,000 years old — it began forming about the time the last ice age was ending on Earth — and in that span, the interaction between the star's radiation, its wind, and the slowly expanding ejecta has built the whole elaborate architecture: an inner bubble with its face-like structure, and an outer shell that we see as the mane.

That interaction is a tug-of-war with a fixed outcome. The fast wind from the white dwarf is always ramming into the older, slower material, and the radiation is always eating through the dust. Right now the mane's shell still holds together, and the surviving clumps still cast their shadows. But every year the nebula expands a little further and thins a little more, and the central star, having exhausted its fuel, will cool and fade. The sculpting is at its peak roughly now — which is, of course, why the portrait matters as a scientific snapshot: it catches the structure at a moment of maximum definition.

This is also why the mid-infrared view is crucial. In visible light, dust is mostly an obstacle — it absorbs and scatters light, hiding what lies behind. In the mid-infrared, dust at temperatures between roughly 30 and 700 degrees Celsius glows with its own heat, and dust at different temperatures glows differently. Webb's MIRI can therefore read the thermal state of the dust directly: which clumps are being irradiated and destroyed, and which are cool and sheltered enough to persist. A visible-light image of NGC 2392 — and the Hubble Space Telescope took a beautiful one in January 2000 — shows the face and the shells. It cannot show the thermal battlefield inside them.

## From Clown Face to Lion

The nebula has been famous for a very long time. William Herschel discovered NGC 2392 in 1787, and it has been a favorite of skygazers ever since — it is bright enough to be visible with a small telescope, which made it one of the objects generations of amateur astronomers could find for themselves.

For most of that history it wore a different face. Through older telescopes and in ground-based photographs, the central bubble with its surrounding structure looked like a head ringed by a fur parka — or, to other eyes, a clown's face — and the "Eskimo Nebula" and "Clown Face Nebula" nicknames stuck. In 2020, at the recommendation of the International Astronomical Union's Working Group on Star Names, NASA discontinued use of the "Eskimo" nickname, noting that certain cosmic nicknames are "not only insensitive, but can be actively harmful." Astronomers increasingly favor the Lion Nebula, a name that describes what the new high-resolution structure actually shows: a lion's face in a magnificent mane.

The object has been observed across the spectrum for decades. Hubble's January 2000 visible-light image showed the same face-and-mane structure that the ground-based observers saw. NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory found unusual X-ray emissions from the region — the central bubble runs at millions of degrees in places, where the fast stellar wind collides with the older ejecta — and the origin of that X-ray emission is still an open question. One long-standing speculation is that the white dwarf is not alone: it may have a binary companion, possibly a red giant, whose interaction with the dying star shaped the nebula's distinctive geometry. That possibility remains under investigation, and it is exactly the kind of question Webb's sharper thermal maps can help address, by revealing how the shells are layered and what shaped them.

## Why Webb's Infrared Eyes Matter Beyond One Nebula

NGC 2392 is not just a curiosity; it is a test case for one of astronomy's biggest questions: where does cosmic dust come from? Dust — solid grains of carbon, silicates, and other compounds — is the raw material from which planets, and eventually the chemistry of life, are built. Yet the universe's supply of dust is poorly understood. Supernova explosions make dust, but perhaps not enough; dying low-mass stars like the Sun's progenitor are another major source, and planetary nebulae are the visible record of that contribution.

By measuring which dust survives the white dwarf's onslaught and which is destroyed, Webb's thermal mapping of the Lion Nebula feeds directly into models of how much dust dying stars actually deliver to the interstellar medium. The surviving clumps in the mane — dense enough to hold together under the star's radiation, sheltering the material behind them — are a small-scale demonstration of a process with galactic consequences. Every red and orange knot in the portrait is a package of solid matter that may, in the far future, seed the formation of new stars and planets. The comet-like tails are the visible exhaust of the destruction that competes with that seeding.

This is why the observation was done jointly in the program of NASA, ESA, and CSA: it turns a beautiful object into a quantitative laboratory for stellar death and material recycling — a process that, in aggregate across billions of stars, built the chemical complexity of the universe we live in.

## A Snapshot With an Expiration Date

The most poignant detail in NASA's description is also the simplest: the Lion Nebula is a snapshot in time. It took several thousand years for the gas and dust to be shaped into the face and mane we see now, and the structure continues to change as the star drives material away. Astronomers estimate NGC 2392 will eventually disperse entirely in about 10,000 years.

Ten thousand years is nothing. The nebula is roughly 10,000 years old now — meaning it has spent about as long being built as it will spend falling apart, and its fully-formed beauty is a transient peak in between. For a human perspective, 10,000 years is the span of recorded civilization, the age of agriculture, the time since the ice age retreated. For a star's life cycle, it is a camera flash. The nebula will thin, fade, and merge into the interstellar medium, leaving only the white dwarf — a cooling cinder drifting through the galaxy, invisible to anyone but a telescope.

That timescale is also what makes the timing of Webb's portrait fortunate rather than merely pretty. A planetary nebula's most structured phase is brief; catch it a few thousand years earlier and it would be a blobby shell, catch it a few thousand years later and it would be a diffuse wisp. NGC 2392, right now, is at its most articulate — face, mane, ears, and all.

## The Sun's Own Future

There is one more reason the Lion Nebula resonates: it is, in the broad strokes, a preview of what will happen to the Sun. The star that built NGC 2392 was, in life, a star much like our own — an ordinary main-sequence star that spent billions of years fusing hydrogen, then swelled, shed its outer layers, and collapsed into a white dwarf. In roughly five billion years, the Sun will do the same: its outer layers will drift away into space, the exposed core will heat up, and for a few tens of thousands of years there will be a glowing nebula in the sky of whatever is left of the solar system.

The Sun's nebula will not look exactly like the Lion Nebula — the details depend on the star's mass, rotation, and companions. But the physics will rhyme: the same cooking radiation, the same fast wind carving the slow ejecta, the same clumps of surviving dust seeding the interstellar medium with the elements that will one day become new worlds. Looking at NGC 2392 is looking at a rehearsal for our star's own finale — and at the same time, at the mechanism that has already recycled countless stars before it, scattering the ingredients of planets across the galaxy.

So the lion is real, in the way cosmic likenesses are real: not a face that someone painted, but a structure built by physics, seen at exactly the right moment. It took several thousand years to sculpt. It has perhaps 10,000 years left before it dissolves into the night. And Webb has given us the clearest view of it anyone will ever have — the dying star's portrait, finished just before the sitting ends.
