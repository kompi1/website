Somewhere in the darkness between the galaxies, a single red dot is shining. It is not a star. It is not a nearby galaxy. It is the glowing heart of a galaxy that existed when the universe was barely five percent of its present age, and its light has been travelling toward us for about 13.1 billion years. On 24 November 2026, the European Space Agency announced that its Euclid space telescope had found this object — formally catalogued as J1504+6252 and nicknamed Dún Aonghasa — and confirmed it as the most distant quasar ever discovered. At a redshift of 8.13, it beats Euclid's own record from only a few months ago by roughly 40 million years, and it carries a black hole of about 360 million solar masses that somehow grew to that enormous size when the cosmos was just 630 million years old.

The discovery matters for much more than the record itself. Every time astronomers push the quasar frontier closer to the Big Bang, they tighten the screws on every theory of how the first supermassive black holes formed. A black hole this large, this early, leaves almost no room for slow, steady growth. It demands either a massive seed at birth, a period of furious feeding far faster than the usual speed limit for accreting gas, or some combination of both. Dún Aonghasa, it turns out, is currently feeding rather slowly — which makes its early history even harder to explain, and even more interesting.

## A Red Dot at the Edge of Time

In Euclid's image, J1504+6252 is almost comically unremarkable: a small red dot near the centre of a field of faint galaxies and scattered points of light, covering an area of sky just 30 arcseconds by 30 arcseconds across. That is the whole point. The most distant objects in the universe do not announce themselves with grandeur. They appear as faint, red smudges, their light stretched by the expansion of the cosmos itself until the blue and ultraviolet light they emitted as young, hot objects arrives at our telescopes shifted deep into the infrared.

That stretching is what astronomers call redshift, and it is the yardstick for cosmic distance. The higher the redshift, the earlier the epoch we are seeing. Dún Aonghasa sits at a redshift of 8.13 — precisely, a systemic redshift of 8.129 plus or minus 0.005, measured by the James Webb Space Telescope. It is the first quasar ever identified beyond a redshift of 8, which means we are seeing the universe as it was roughly 13.1 billion years ago, when it was about five percent of its current age of roughly 13.8 billion years.

A quasar is the intensely bright core of an active galaxy, powered by gas falling into a supermassive black hole. As the gas spirals inward it forms a turbulent, glowing disk that heats until it outshines the rest of its host galaxy entirely. Quasars represent a brief phase in a galaxy's life, but during that phase they are the most luminous persistent sources in the universe — bright enough to be studied in detail even from within the first billion years of cosmic history. That is why they are such powerful tools: each one is a lighthouse at the edge of the observable universe, and each one carries information about the black hole at its centre and the young galaxy around it.

Euclid identified J1504+6252 in about 18 months of data from its Euclid Wide Survey, picking the candidate out from among millions of objects. The telescope was not built to hunt quasars — it was launched in July 2023 on a mission to reveal the secrets of the dark universe, mapping the geometry of the cosmos and the distribution of dark matter and dark energy. But its combination of highly sensitive infrared imaging with exceptionally wide sky coverage turns out to be exactly what a quasar hunt needs. Rare objects can only be found by searching enormous volumes of sky, and Euclid searches more sky, more deeply, in the infrared than any survey before it.

## How Euclid Found a Needle in a Cosmic Haystack

Finding a quasar at redshift 8 is an exercise in sifting. Euclid's wide survey images contain millions of sources: stars in our own Milky Way, ordinary galaxies at modest distances, and a thin sprinkling of genuinely ancient objects. The candidates for the most distant quasars are selected by their colours — specifically, by the way their light drops off sharply at short wavelengths, a signature of the hydrogen in the early universe absorbing everything bluer than a certain threshold. Only a handful of objects in millions survive that filter, and most of those turn out to be something else: cool brown dwarf stars in our own galaxy, or dusty galaxies at much lower redshifts masquerading as distant ones.

That is why Euclid's discovery is only the beginning of the story. The telescope finds the candidates; a worldwide campaign of follow-up observations has to confirm them. For Dún Aonghasa, that campaign involved some of the most powerful telescopes on the ground and in space. The W.M. Keck Observatory in Hawaii and the NASA/ESA Hubble Space Telescope provided the first hints that the object was genuinely distant. The Large Binocular Telescope in Arizona, with follow-up observations led by MPIA scientist Silvia Belladitta, confirmed the source's high redshift. Finally, the NASA/ESA/CSA James Webb Space Telescope delivered the decisive measurement: a spectrum that pinned the redshift to 8.129 and revealed the mass of the black hole powering the quasar.

The result was published in the journal Astronomy and Astrophysics on 24 November 2026, in a paper led by Julien Wolf, a postdoctoral researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy in Heidelberg, Germany, who has since moved to the European Southern Observatory in Garching, Germany. The paper's title is direct: "Euclid: Discovery of a quasar at z = 8.13 hosting a 360-million-solar-mass black hole."

The nickname, Dún Aonghasa, comes from the team itself. It is named after a prehistoric cliff-top stone fort on Inis Mór, the largest of the Aran Islands off the west coast of Ireland, where the land gives way to a vast Atlantic horizon. The parallel is deliberate: J1504+6252 stands at the current observational frontier of quasars, illuminating the threshold to even earlier cosmic times waiting to be explored. The name is pronounced roughly "doon AYNG-guh-suh."

## Breaking Its Own Record — Again

What makes this discovery striking is how quickly Euclid is moving the frontier. In July 2026, the same team announced the first 31 quasars discovered in the Euclid Wide Survey from the universe's first billion years — the largest haul of ancient quasars ever collected in a single survey sweep, roughly doubling the number known from that epoch. Among those 31 was the previous record-holder, at a redshift of 7.77. Four months later, Euclid has broken its own record by another 40 million years.

The numbers tell the story of a survey hitting its stride. In just a year and a half of operations, Euclid has announced 31 ancient quasars. It has already found 12 new quasars beyond redshift 7 — more than doubling what was known previously at that distance. That threshold, redshift 7, took 15 years to reach with earlier surveys. Euclid crossed it in months. And at redshift 8, quasars are so rare that at most a few tens are expected in Euclid's entire survey, which will eventually cover roughly a third of the sky. Dún Aonghasa may be one of the most distant quasars Euclid ever finds — or it may be the first of a small, precious handful.

That rarity is part of the science. The epoch Dún Aonghasa comes from — about 630 million years after the Big Bang — sits inside the Epoch of Reionization, the era when the first stars and galaxies were transforming a dark universe into one filled with light, stripping electrons from the hydrogen gas that filled space. Quasars are among the brightest beacons from that era, and each one found is a probe of the conditions in which the first galaxies assembled.

The table makes the scale of the achievement plain. A redshift of 7 took the astronomical community a decade and a half to reach. Euclid has now pushed past redshift 8 in its first 18 months of survey data — and the object it found there is not a marginal case but a fully formed, 360-million-solar-mass giant.

## The Growth Problem: Too Big, Too Soon

The real puzzle of Dún Aonghasa is not its distance but its mass. How does a black hole reach 360 million times the mass of the Sun when the universe has only existed for 630 million years? Stars need time to live and die; the first stars had only just appeared. A black hole that grows by steadily swallowing gas faces a speed limit: as infalling gas heats up, its radiation pushes back against the incoming material, a self-regulating process known as the Eddington limit. Grow too fast, and the light from the feeding itself chokes off the food supply.

"Finding quasars at ever-increasing distances, at the edge of the observable universe, teaches us something about how the black holes in their centres grew," said Julien Wolf. "The closer to the Big Bang we find them, the faster their black holes must have grown or the more massive they must have been at birth — or both."

Dún Aonghasa sharpens the problem in a surprising way. Follow-up observations show that it is currently consuming material at a relatively slow rate, far below the Eddington limit. That is unusual: other distant quasars tend to be feeding at or near the limit. If this black hole had always grown as slowly as it is growing now, it would have needed an extremely massive initial seed — a starting black hole so large that current formation models struggle to explain it. Instead, the team concludes, the black hole must have gone through episodes of extremely rapid growth very early in its history, perhaps even accreting faster than the theoretical Eddington limit allows.

Wolf and his colleagues tested the proposed formation scenarios against the numbers. Under reasonable growth rates, the latest discoveries are consistent with a scenario called direct collapse, in which a massive cloud of gas condenses under its own gravity and forms a black hole directly, skipping the intermediate step of forming a star first. A direct-collapse seed would be born heavy — tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of solar masses — giving the black hole a head start that ordinary stellar-mass seeds cannot match. The conditions for direct collapse must have been rare in the early universe, which would neatly explain why so few quasars have been found at redshifts beyond 7.5 so far.

The evidence points in the same direction from other telescopes, too. Webb's mysterious "little red dots" — tiny, red, compact sources in the infant universe — are increasingly interpreted as newborn supermassive black holes hidden inside the gas disks that formed them, and the <a href="/blog/jwst-little-red-dots-november-2026-direct-collapse-black-holes/">JWST Little Red Dots November 2026: The First Supermassive Black Holes Explained</a> research connects them to the same direct-collapse picture. Dún Aonghasa adds a crucial data point at the extreme end: a confirmed, mature quasar at redshift 8 whose slow present-day appetite rules out the simplest slow-and-steady growth story.

## Timeline: Euclid's Quasar Hunt

## July 2023 — Euclid launches

ESA's Euclid space telescope launches on a mission to map the dark universe, carrying a visible-light camera and a near-infrared spectrometer and photometer built to survey roughly a third of the sky.

## 2024–2025 — The wide survey begins

Euclid starts its systematic sweep of the sky. The Euclid Wide Survey accumulates images of millions of objects, and the consortium's quasar hunters begin sifting the data for the reddest, most distant candidates.

## July 2026 — 31 ancient quasars announced

The Wolf-led team publishes the first 31 quasars from the universe's first billion years found in Euclid data — the largest such haul in a single survey — including the then-record-holder at redshift 7.77. The announcement roughly doubles the known population from the cosmic dawn.

## 24 November 2026 — The redshift 8 barrier falls

Euclid announces J1504+6252, Dún Aonghasa, at redshift 8.13, confirmed by Keck, Hubble, the Large Binocular Telescope and Webb. The paper by Wolf and colleagues appears in Astronomy and Astrophysics the same day. It is the first quasar ever found beyond redshift 8, and it breaks Euclid's July record by about 40 million years.

## 2026 and beyond — The census continues

Euclid's survey is still in its early stages. With at most a few tens of redshift-8 quasars expected across the whole survey, each new find will further pin down when the first supermassive black holes appeared — and whether even the direct-collapse scenario can survive what Euclid finds next.

## Why a Slow Eater Is the Most Telling Clue

It is worth pausing on the oddest detail of this discovery: Dún Aonghasa is not, right now, a particularly greedy black hole. Most quasars found at these distances are caught in the act of gorging, feeding near the Eddington limit, their accretion disks blazing. This one is different. Its measured accretion rate sits far below that limit, which means its present-day feeding cannot account for its mass.

That leaves two options, and both are informative. Either the black hole was born unusually massive — a direct-collapse seed far heavier than most models comfortably produce — or it grew in bursts, feeding at extreme rates in the past that have since subsided. The team's modelling favours the second picture: an early phase of very rapid growth, possibly exceeding the Eddington limit, followed by the comparatively quiet state we see today. It is a reminder that a quasar is a snapshot, not a biography. The black hole we observe at 630 million years after the Big Bang has already lived a dramatic life, and its calm present conceals a violent past.

This pattern — black holes racing ahead of expectations in the early universe — is becoming a theme of the Webb and Euclid era. Just days before the Dún Aonghasa announcement, astronomers reported on MARTA-3887, a black hole that had already grown to about 3.3 million solar masses inside a galaxy holding only about 145 million Suns' worth of stars, a ratio roughly a hundred times above the relation seen in nearby galaxies. Read more in <a href="/blog/marta-3887-black-hole-grew-faster-than-galaxy-november-2026/">MARTA-3887 Black Hole Grew Faster Than Its Galaxy 12 Billion Years Ago</a>. Dún Aonghasa is the same story written at a far grander scale and a far earlier time: the black holes came first, or at least they grew first, and the galaxies around them are still catching up in our understanding.

The census behind this discovery puts it in context. The July announcement of <a href="/blog/euclid-31-oldest-quasars/">A Census of Dawn: Euclid Spots 31 of the Universe's Oldest Quasars</a> showed that Euclid could find ancient quasars in bulk; Dún Aonghasa shows it can also reach the extreme edge, where each individual object strains the models. Together, the two announcements trace a survey that is doing exactly what it was designed to do — except that it was designed to study dark energy, and the quasar haul is a spectacular bonus.

## What Comes Next

The obvious question is how much farther back Euclid can go. The telescope's survey will continue for years, and the consortium's quasar census is still growing. "And this may happen sooner than we think," said Silvia Belladitta, the MPIA scientist responsible for the critical Large Binocular Telescope follow-up that confirmed the source's high redshift. "This highlights how quickly Euclid is expanding our census of the most distant quasars. Each new discovery brings us closer to understanding how supermassive black holes formed and grew in the early universe."

If Euclid finds quasars at considerably higher redshifts — approaching a cosmic age of just a few hundred million years — even the direct-collapse scenario may become hard to sustain. In that case, astronomers may have to consider a more exotic alternative: primordial black holes, hypothetical objects formed in the extreme conditions right after the Big Bang, which would have been born massive and served as ready-made seeds for gas to accumulate around. Such objects would give black holes a head start without requiring extreme growth rates at all. Only future observations — and perhaps even more distant quasars — will settle the question.

"It is unclear how far back in time these supermassive black holes powering luminous quasars exist, but Euclid has a good chance of finding out what that limit is," concluded Eduardo Bañados, the MPIA research group leader involved in the study.

That is the real promise of Dún Aonghasa. It is not merely a record, and records in astronomy are made to be broken — Euclid broke its own within four months. It is a calibration point: the most distant quasar known, with a measured mass and a measured feeding rate, sitting at a moment in cosmic history when the first galaxies were still assembling. Every model of black hole formation must now pass through this single red dot. If the models can explain how 360 million Suns gathered in 630 million years, they are on the right track. If they cannot, the universe is telling us that the first black holes were born in a way we have not yet imagined — and Euclid, still early in its survey, is exactly the telescope to find out.
