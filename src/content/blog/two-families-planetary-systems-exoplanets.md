Look up at the night sky and every point of light tells a story about how it was born. For more than three decades, astronomers have been piecing together the family trees of the thousands of planets discovered around other stars, and the picture that emerged was a bewildering menagerie: hot Jupiters hugging their stars, tightly packed packs of super-Earths, lonely gas giants on eccentric orbits, and our own solar system sitting off to one side like an outlier nobody could quite explain. Now, in a study that amounts to the first "cosmic genealogy" of alien worlds, an international team led by Italy's National Institute for Astrophysics (INAF) has found a deep order hidden in that chaos. The multiplanet systems of the Milky Way, it turns out, belong to two distinct families — and the star at the center is the parent that decided everything.

The research, published on October 13 in *Nature Astronomy* by a team co-led by Diego Turrini of INAF and Camilla Danielski of the Universitat de València, examined 300 of the best-characterized multiplanet systems among the more than 1,000 known in our galaxy. It is one of the most ambitious comparative studies of planetary architecture ever attempted, and its conclusion is as elegant as it is far-reaching: there is a true "genetic link" between a host star and its planets, one that shapes not only how the system is born but how it evolves dynamically over billions of years — and even whether it survives.

## A census of alien solar systems

When the first planet was confirmed around a Sun-like star in 1995 — 51 Pegasi b, a gas giant in a scorching four-day orbit — it shattered the expectation that other systems would look like ours. NASA's Kepler mission, which launched in 2009, then flooded the catalog with thousands of exoplanets and revealed that compact systems of small planets are astonishingly common. A landmark Kepler-era finding was the "peas-in-a-pod" pattern: planets within the same system tend to be similar in size and regularly spaced, like siblings sharing a family resemblance. Astronomers also learned that massive, metal-rich stars — stars whose spectra show higher abundances of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium — are more likely to host giant planets, a correlation established in the early 2000s and confirmed many times since.

But the big unanswered question lingered, and the INAF-led team put it bluntly: what determines the characteristics of planetary systems as we see them today? Are those characteristics established at birth, in the dusty protoplanetary disk, or sculpted later by billions of years of gravitational evolution? Previous studies had examined pieces of the puzzle — planet masses here, orbital spacings there, stellar metallicities somewhere else — but no one had combined architecture and stellar properties across such a large, well-measured sample.

The team assembled the 300 best-characterized systems available, meaning systems where both the planets' masses and radii and the host stars' fundamental properties were measured with enough precision to compare apples to apples. They then constructed a set of metrics that let them compare wildly different systems on equal footing: measures of the total planetary mass of each system, the orbital spacing of the planets, the planets' orbital eccentricities and inclinations, and crucially, a parameter the researchers call "dynamical temperature." Just as physical temperature describes the average agitation of molecules in a gas, dynamical temperature quantifies how much a planetary system deviates from a calm, unperturbed configuration in which every planet travels on a circular orbit in the same plane. A hot system is a disturbed system; a cold one is an orderly one.

The result was not a smooth continuum but two clusters — two families with sharply different properties, separated by a statistical analysis robust enough to survive rigorous testing.

## Family one: the heavyweights

The first family is the clan of the heavyweights. Its members are wide, massive planetary systems whose combined planetary mass often runs from twice to ten times the total mass of our own solar system's planets. They orbit stars that are more massive than the Sun and richer in metals — astronomers' catch-all term for every element other than hydrogen and helium. Their orbits sprawl across a larger region of space, and many of their members are gas giants.

What really sets this family apart, though, is its temperament. These systems run hot — dynamically speaking. Their high dynamical temperatures betray a past marked by violence: gravitational instabilities that wrenched planets out of their original orbits, giant collisions between worlds, and close encounters that ejected planets from their systems entirely. The researchers describe a history of chaos that reads like a demolition derby played out over hundreds of millions of years.

Numerical simulations carried out by the team, exploring how planetary systems with different starting conditions evolve, reinforced that picture. "The scenarios that best reproduce the dynamical properties of the family of wide and massive systems are those in which the systems are unstable from birth," explains Danae Polychroni, an INAF researcher who supervised the simulation work. The logic is almost brutally simple: around heavier, metal-rich stars, the protoplanetary disk starts out with a greater abundance of the solid material from which planets assemble. That bounty is a double-edged sword. The planets that form are born too large and too close together, and the system simply cannot stay stable. Sooner or later, gravity's bill comes due.

It is a striking inversion of the old intuition that more material should make for a more impressive, more orderly system. Instead, abundance breeds instability. The massive planets jostle one another, orbits cross, and the system rearranges itself through collisions and ejections until it settles into a configuration that can survive — often with fewer planets than it started with, on wider and more eccentric orbits.

## Family two: the peas in a pod

The second family is, in many ways, the first family's mirror image — and it happens to be the family that feels most familiar. Its members are compact, low-mass systems whose planets resemble one another in mass and size: the famous peas-in-a-pod architecture. They orbit stars with masses and metallicities equal to or lower than the Sun's, and their planets crowd astonishingly close to their stars — in most cases at distances much smaller than Mercury's orbit around the Sun. A world at Mercury's distance circles our star every 88 days; many of these planets complete an orbit in a matter of days or even hours.

These systems run cool. Their dynamical temperatures are low, indicating comparatively gentle histories — no catastrophic instabilities, no giant planetary billiards. They look, in essence, much like our solar system in its overall temperament: orderly, stable, long-lived. That resemblance matters, because it suggests that the solar system is not a freak anomaly but a fairly typical member of the calm family — albeit a distinctive one, since our planets are spread over much wider orbits than the tight knots that dominate this group.

That observation itself raises an intriguing question the researchers are eager to explore. Our solar system kept the calm temperament of the second family but built itself at the grand scale more typical of the first family's architectures. Why the solar system never produced a compact pack of inner super-Earths — the most common planetary configuration in the galaxy — remains one of exoplanet science's most delicious mysteries. One leading idea is that Jupiter, by growing quickly and carving a gap in the young solar nebula, choked off the inward flow of planet-building material and starved the inner solar system. The new two-family framework gives that debate fresh context: the Sun's moderate mass and near-solar metallicity placed our system squarely in the calm family's nursery, but Jupiter's early growth may have pushed our architecture toward a wider, grander layout.

## Real families or a trick of the telescope?

Any astronomer hearing about a clean new classification of exoplanets will ask the same skeptical question: is this real, or is it just a trick of our telescopes? Observational bias haunts exoplanet science. The transit method, which detects planets by the tiny dip in starlight when a planet crosses its star's face, is most sensitive to large planets on tight orbits. The radial-velocity method, which detects the stellar wobble induced by an orbiting planet, favors massive planets. A sample of the "best-characterized" systems is, almost by definition, a sample of the systems our instruments find easiest to study — and those selection effects could easily carve an artificial divide out of what is really a continuous distribution.

The team confronted that problem head-on, and it was, in their own telling, the hardest part of the work. "The main difficulty during the data analysis was determining whether the two families of planetary systems were real or just an illusion created by the technological limits of current observational techniques," says co-first author Camilla Danielski, a researcher at the Universitat de València and former INAF postdoctoral fellow.

Their answer came from statistics. The researchers fit the distribution of their architectural metrics with a Gaussian mixture model — a technique that tests whether a dataset is better described by one population or two — and then ran two-sample statistical tests (Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests with multiple-testing corrections, and Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney tests where the data demanded them) comparing every architectural and stellar property between the candidate families. The differences held up at high statistical significance across multiple independent subsamples of the data. In the end, Danielski says, "it became clear that selection effects acted as the tip of the iceberg, revealing the existence of much deeper structural differences between these systems." The biases are real, in other words — but beneath them lies a genuine dichotomy.

The simulations provided the second pillar of the argument. Models of planet formation and long-term dynamical evolution, run with different starting masses and disk compositions, naturally produced the two families: abundant disks gave rise to systems that were unstable from birth and settled into wide, massive, dynamically excited configurations, while sparser disks produced compact, calm peas-in-a-pod systems. The models reproduced not just the existence of two groups but their observed dynamical temperatures — a match between theory and data that is difficult to dismiss as coincidence.

## The gene that decides a system's fate

The study's most provocative claim is philosophical as much as scientific. "The fundamental question we started from was: what determines the characteristics of planetary systems as we see them today? Are they established at birth or sculpted by evolution over time? The truth, in the end, turned out to lie somewhere in the middle," explains Diego Turrini, an INAF researcher and co-first author of the paper. "Our results show that there is a genetic link between planetary systems and their stars, and that, depending on the star around which they are born, this link also determines their fate."

The word "genetic" is doing deliberate work here. Just as a child's genome — inherited from its parents — constrains the range of bodies it can grow into, the star's mass and metallicity constrain the range of planetary systems its disk can produce. Even modest variations in the planet-formation environment, the team found, can radically change the nature of the forming system and its future evolution, determining whether it will pass through phases of marked instability and giant impacts or glide through the eons in relative peace.

This is a subtle but important shift from how astronomers have usually thought about exoplanet diversity. For years the dominant narrative emphasized chaos and contingency: planetary systems are diverse because formation is a messy, stochastic process, and small differences in starting conditions get amplified into wildly different outcomes. The new work does not deny that messiness — the violent histories of the massive family are a testament to it — but it argues that the messiness itself is heritable, channeled by the parent star into two broad outcomes. Nature, it seems, deals from a stacked deck, and the star is holding the cards.

## What it means for the search for life

Perhaps the most consequential implication of the two-family picture concerns habitability — the hunt for worlds that could support life, which remains the field's grand prize. The calm family, with its gentle dynamical histories, is the natural place to look for stable, long-lived environments. A planet that has endured billions of years of gravitational turmoil, or one whose siblings were ejected into interstellar space, is a poor bet for the sustained clement conditions life presumably requires. Systems that run cool dynamically are far more likely to preserve temperate, stable orbits over the geological timescales on which biology can take hold.

That insight could sharpen the targeting strategies of the great observatories now coming online and those on the drawing board. Europe's PLATO mission, scheduled to launch in the coming years, is designed to find Earth-size planets in the habitable zones of Sun-like stars and measure their radii and ages with unprecedented precision. NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will survey vast numbers of exoplanets by microlensing and direct imaging, while the European Space Agency's Ariel mission will characterize the atmospheres of hundreds of known exoplanets. Knowing that a star's mass and metallicity predict not just what kinds of planets it hosts but how violently those planets have been treated gives mission planners a powerful new prior: calm stars make calm systems, and calm systems are where Earth-like worlds are most likely to have survived.

The discovery also reframes our understanding of the solar system's place in the galaxy. We have long known that the solar system is unusual in some respects — its lack of close-in super-Earths, its widely spaced giants — and the new framework helps explain why: the Sun's properties set our system on the calm-family track, but the specific history of Jupiter's growth and the Grand Tack-like migrations proposed by theorists gave us an architecture all our own. We are a member of the family, but with our own story — which is, perhaps, exactly what a genealogy should reveal.

## The long road to a family tree

To appreciate the weight of this result, it helps to remember how recently the entire field of exoplanetary genealogy was impossible. In 1992, the first confirmed exoplanets were found orbiting a pulsar — the scorched remnants of worlds circling a dead star. In 1995 came 51 Pegasi b. By 2009, when Kepler launched, the confirmed count stood in the low hundreds; Kepler alone would eventually push it past 5,000, with thousands more candidates awaiting confirmation. Each wave of discovery added statistical power, and each new method — transits, radial velocities, microlensing, direct imaging, astrometry — filled in a different slice of the population.

The peas-in-a-pod pattern, identified in Kepler data by researchers including Lauren Weiss and Erik Petigura in a widely cited 2018 analysis, was an early hint that intra-system uniformity was a real phenomenon rather than a selection effect. The correlation between stellar metallicity and giant-planet occurrence, established by Fischer and Valenti in 2005, was an early hint of the star–planet link. What Turrini, Danielski, Polychroni and their colleagues have done is fuse those threads — and many others — into a single coherent picture: the star's mass and chemistry set the disk's budget, the disk's budget sets the planets' birth masses, and those masses determine whether the system lives fast and dies young, gravitationally speaking, or settles into a long, quiet middle age.

It is worth noting what the study does not claim. Two families is almost certainly a simplification — the "uncertain membership region" the authors identify, where systems fall between the two clusters, hints at transitional cases and possibly substructure yet to be resolved. And the sample, for all its quality, is still just 300 systems: the best-characterized tip of a catalog that will grow by orders of magnitude in the coming decade. Future surveys will test whether the dichotomy sharpens or dissolves as the sample grows, whether the boundaries between the families shift, and whether additional families emerge from the noise.

## A new lens on a crowded galaxy

Standing back, what the INAF-led team has offered is not just a classification scheme but a new lens. For a generation, astronomers classified exoplanets one by one — hot Jupiters, super-Earths, mini-Neptunes, cold giants — like naturalists cataloging beetles. The two-family framework instead classifies systems the way a genealogist classifies people: by lineage. Ask not just what a planet is, the study suggests, but who its parent is, and what family it was born into. The answer will tell you much of what you need to know about the life that planet has lived.

There is something quietly profound in that. The universe builds planets the way families raise children: not from a blueprint, but from inherited tendencies, material circumstances, and the accidents of a shared history. Some families are born into abundance and tear themselves apart; others are born into scarcity and hold together. The stars, it turns out, are not just the lamps that light their planets — they are the parents that shaped them. And now, for the first time, we can read the family resemblance in the sky.
