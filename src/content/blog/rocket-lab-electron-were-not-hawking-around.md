Later today, from the southern tip of New Zealand's Mahia Peninsula, a slender 18-meter rocket will climb into the afternoon sky carrying three small satellites designed to do something deceptively powerful: listen. Rocket Lab's Electron is scheduled to launch the mission whimsically named "We're Not Hawking Around" during a window that opens at 05:00 UTC — 12:00 noon Western Indonesia Time, 6:00 p.m. local time in New Zealand — carrying the three-satellite HawkEye 360 Cluster 16 into a sun-synchronous orbit about 520 kilometers above Earth. The mission will be Rocket Lab's 98th Electron launch overall and its 19th flight of 2026, a cadence that has quietly made the little rocket one of the busiest launch vehicles on the planet.

## A three-and-three-quarter-hour window for the triplets

The launch is scheduled from Rocket Lab Launch Complex 1A at Mahia, with a window running from 05:00 to 08:45 UTC on Wednesday, October 21 — a three-hour-and-forty-five-minute span that gives the launch team flexibility to work around weather or minor technical holds. Electron will head south from the pad on a southern trajectory, threading its way over the open ocean toward a sun-synchronous orbit, the type of orbit in which the satellite passes over the Earth at roughly the same local solar time on each pass. Sun-synchronous orbits are the standard choice for Earth-observation satellites, and they are equally valuable for satellites that watch the planet's radio-frequency spectrum, because consistent lighting and ground-track conditions make the data easier to compare from one pass to the next.

Launch viewers can expect a brief window of on-air coverage before liftoff, with the live stream typically beginning 15 to 30 minutes before the attempt. If the team needs to stand down, backup opportunities generally follow within the same window period on subsequent days.

For Rocket Lab, the flight continues a 2026 launch tempo that has the company knocking on the door of a milestone few in the industry thought a small-lift rocket could reach: the 100th Electron flight. After today, the countdown to triple digits will be down to two.

## The payload: three ears in the sky

The three satellites aboard Electron belong to HawkEye 360, a Virginia-based company that operates a space-based intelligence network built around radio frequency (RF) technology. Rather than looking at Earth with cameras, HawkEye 360's satellites listen to it. They detect, geolocate, and characterize radio-frequency signals from across the spectrum — everything from a ship's automatic identification system broadcasts to radar emissions, communications signals, and the telltale fingerprints of GNSS jamming.

HawkEye 360 flies its satellites in clusters of three, and Cluster 16 continues that design. The reason is physics as much as strategy: by measuring the same signal arriving at three spacecraft separated in orbit, the company can triangulate where on the ground that signal originated. One satellite hears a blip; three satellites turn it into a location on a map.

The data the constellation produces serves both government and commercial customers, with applications the company describes across five areas: maritime tracking, the detection of GNSS jamming, radar monitoring, communications mapping, and space defense. In practice, that means spotting vessels that have gone "dark" by switching off their transponders, flagging interference that degrades GPS-style navigation, cataloging radar activity in sensitive regions, and mapping who is broadcasting what, where, and how powerfully.

## Why signals intelligence matters right now

Space-based radio-frequency collection has become one of the fastest-growing corners of the commercial space economy, and its relevance has only sharpened in recent years. At sea, maritime tracking from orbit fills gaps that shipboard transponders leave behind — including the deliberate gaps left by vessels engaged in sanctions evasion, illegal fishing, or smuggling that silence their Automatic Identification System beacons. A satellite listening for a radar or radio transmission can still find them.

On land and in the air, the detection of GNSS jamming has taken on new urgency as interference with satellite navigation has become more common in conflict zones and busy air corridors. HawkEye 360's constellation can pick up the telltale emissions of jammers and pinpoint their source, information that matters to aviation safety, military planners, and civilian infrastructure operators alike.

Radar monitoring and communications mapping add a third and fourth dimension: understanding which radar systems are active where, and how communications activity shifts across a region. For defense and security customers, that picture of the electromagnetic environment is as valuable as a photograph — sometimes more so, because radio emissions continue through cloud cover and darkness where optical imagery fails.

That persistence is a key part of the company's pitch. Clouds, night, and weather degrade optical and even some infrared sensors, but radio waves pass through unimpeded. A radio-frequency constellation sees through the things that blind cameras.

## The little rocket that flies everything

Electron has earned its place in the industry the hard way: by flying often and flying reliably. The two-stage vehicle is powered by nine Rutherford engines on its first stage and a single vacuum-optimized Rutherford on the second, burning kerosene and liquid oxygen. The engines are famous for their 3D-printed components, a manufacturing approach that let Rocket Lab iterate quickly during development and scale production at its facilities in the United States and New Zealand.

At 18 meters tall, Electron can carry about 320 kilograms to low Earth orbit — a modest capacity by the standards of a Falcon 9 or an Ariane 6, but precisely the point. Electron exists for missions that don't need a giant rocket: small satellites that need a dedicated ride to an exact orbit on an exact schedule, rather than a seat on a rideshare mission that launches when someone else's payload is ready.

The numbers behind the vehicle tell the story of its maturation. According to launch tracking data, Electron has flown 97 times, with 93 successes and a streak of 56 consecutive successful missions — a run of reliability that has made it the workhorse of the small-launch market. Today's mission, the 98th, leaves the company two launches shy of a century, a milestone that would make Electron one of only a handful of rockets in history to reach 100 flights.

That tempo is not an accident. Rocket Lab has steadily increased Electron's launch rate year after year, and 2026 is on track to be another record campaign. The company has also developed booster reusability for Electron, recovering first stages from the ocean for refurbishment and reflight — a discipline it pioneered on the small rocket before applying similar lessons to its larger Neutron vehicle, currently in development.

## A week packed with launches

"We're Not Hawking Around" is just one flight in a remarkably busy launch week. On October 19, Japan's H3 rocket carried the JAXA Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) spacecraft toward Phobos on the first leg of a mission that aims to return samples of the Martian moon to Earth in 2031. On October 20, SpaceX launched its Bandwagon-5 dedicated rideshare mission from Cape Canaveral and, separately, flew Starlink Group 15-15 from Vandenberg on the 36th flight of veteran booster B1063.

Today, October 21, is itself crowded: China's Galactic Energy is scheduled to launch a Ceres 1S rocket from a converted barge off the coast of Haiyang — the sea-launched variant of its Ceres 1 small-lift vehicle — while Falcon 9 has another Starlink batch queued from Vandenberg later in the day. Looking ahead, the calendar shows more Chinese launches with Long March vehicles, a Long March 8A carrying connectivity satellites, and another Starlink mission later in the week.

That density is the new normal. The global launch industry is on pace for another record year in 2026, and small rockets like Electron and Ceres 1 are part of the engine driving it — flying the smaller payloads that the big rockets can't schedule efficiently on their own.

## The Hawking joke, explained

Rocket Lab names its missions with puns that have become a running signature of the program — and "We're Not Hawking Around" may be one of the best. It's a nod to Stephen Hawking, the late theoretical physicist whose work on black holes and cosmology made him one of the most recognized scientists in history. The triplets aboard, appropriately enough for a mission honoring one of physics' great minds, will spend their working lives doing applied physics: turning the arrival times of faint radio waves into precise positions on the surface of the Earth.

The joke lands twice, though, because this mission isn't messing around. Each of the three satellites in Cluster 16 must reach its designated orbital slot in formation, power up, and begin contributing to a constellation that sells its data to governments and security agencies. The business of listening from space is serious business — the name is just the part that makes you smile on the way to the pad.

## What to watch for

When Electron lifts off from Mahia today, the sequence will follow the familiar choreography. The nine Rutherford engines will light, the rocket will pitch southward, and roughly two and a half minutes into flight the first stage will shut down and separate. The second stage will burn to orbit, and then the mission's third stage — the Kick Stage, a small propulsion module that gives Electron its precise-orbit capability — will carry the three HawkEye 360 satellites to their 520-kilometer destination and release them.

The deployment of three satellites on a single mission is routine for Electron, and the Kick Stage's ability to place them precisely is one reason dedicated small-launch missions command a premium over rideshares. On a rideshare flight, satellites ride to whatever orbit the primary customer needs and maneuver — or accept — the difference. On a dedicated Electron mission, the orbit is the customer's.

For HawkEye 360, that precision matters. The three satellites of Cluster 16 need to be positioned relative to one another so that their geolocation math works; the exact spacing and orbital plane are mission parameters, not suggestions. Getting them delivered exactly where they need to be is the service Rocket Lab is selling.

## Building the constellation, three satellites at a time

Cluster 16 is the latest addition to a constellation strategy that HawkEye 360 has pursued since its founding: grow the network in threes. Each cluster of three satellites adds another set of "ears" to the system, and each new cluster sharpens the constellation's coverage — more frequent revisits over any given spot on Earth, and more satellites available to participate in a geolocation solution.

The logic of the triple formation deserves a moment of appreciation. Radio-frequency geolocation from space works on the same principle your brain uses to figure out where a sound is coming from: the signal reaches one ear slightly before the other, and the difference tells you the direction. HawkEye 360's satellites measure the tiny differences in arrival time — and in frequency, caused by the Doppler shift of satellites moving at orbital speed — of the same radio emission reaching each spacecraft. Feed those measurements into the orbital geometry of the cluster, and the result is a fix on the emitter's position.

That technique is why the company launches in threes and why the precise placement of each satellite matters so much. It is also why a dedicated Electron mission is worth the premium: on a rideshare, the three satellites might end up in different orbital planes or altitudes, degrading the triangulation math. Delivered together by the same Kick Stage to the same 520-kilometer sun-synchronous orbit, they start their working lives in the formation the constellation model assumes.

The applications of that data keep multiplying. Beyond defense and security work, commercial maritime analytics firms fuse RF-derived vessel positions with shipping records to understand trade flows; insurers use signal data to verify where vessels actually went; and humanitarian organizations have used radio-frequency mapping to track activity in disaster zones where ground infrastructure has failed. The same three satellites that help a navy find a jammer can help a shipping company find its own fleet.

## Mahia's rise as a small-launch capital

The Mahia Peninsula is an unlikely rocket capital. A remote headland on the east coast of New Zealand's North Island, it hosts a launch site that became the first private spaceport in the world to attempt an orbital launch, when an Electron lifted off on May 25, 2017 — and the first to succeed, with the January 21, 2018 flight that put Rocket Lab on the map. Since then, Launch Complex 1 has become one of the busiest small-satellite launch sites on Earth, dispatching Electrons for customers ranging from NASA and national security agencies to commercial startups on every continent.

The site's appeal is geography. A launch site near the coast with clear ocean corridors to the south and east can serve the full range of inclinations — polar, sun-synchronous, and everything in between — without overflying populated land. Rocket Lab's two pads at Mahia, LC-1A and LC-1B, let the company run parallel campaigns, turning around one pad while the other prepares the next mission. Today's flight from LC-1A is part of a machine that has settled into a rhythm of roughly two launches a month.

That rhythm is about to get busier. Rocket Lab operates additional launch capacity at the Mid-Atlantic Regional Spaceport on Wallops Island, Virginia, for customers that need to fly from the United States — particularly government missions with domestic-launch requirements. Between the two sites, the company now offers something no other small-launch provider can match: a flight-proven vehicle, two continents of launch sites, and a manifest deep enough to keep both busy.

## What happens after the drop-off

Getting to orbit is only the first test. Once the Kick Stage releases the three HawkEye 360 satellites, the constellation operator takes over: each spacecraft must deploy its solar panels and antennas, establish contact with ground stations, and run through a commissioning campaign — checking out power, thermal, attitude control, and payload systems before beginning operational collection.

For RF satellites, commissioning includes calibration passes against known signal sources: radar stations, broadcast towers, and reference beacons whose positions and frequencies are well documented. Those calibrations tune the geolocation algorithms to each spacecraft's actual performance in orbit, which inevitably differs slightly from its performance on the ground. Only after calibration does a new cluster start contributing data to the operational product.

The process typically takes weeks to a few months, after which Cluster 16 will join the rest of the fleet in routine collection: sweeping the spectrum as it circles the planet every 90-odd minutes, downlinking raw signal captures to ground stations, and feeding the analytics pipeline that turns waveforms into the maps and alerts customers pay for. Three more listeners, added to a chorus that never sleeps.

## The bigger picture

There is a larger story tucked inside this launch, and it has to do with how the space industry now does business. A decade ago, a company like HawkEye 360 would have waited years for a launch opportunity on a large government-class rocket, sharing space with a primary payload whose schedule and destination dictated everything. Today it books a dedicated small rocket, flies from a private spaceport on the other side of the world from its headquarters, and places its satellites exactly where its constellation model demands — then does it again the next time a new cluster is ready.

Rocket Lab's launch complex at Mahia, on the east coast of New Zealand's North Island, embodies that transformation. It was the first private spaceport to host an orbital launch attempt, and it has grown into one of the most active launch sites in the world for small satellites. That a Virginia signals-intelligence company, a California-founded rocket company, and a New Zealand spaceport can come together on a regular commercial contract — no government procurement program required — is the market working the way its architects hoped.

And the tempo is accelerating. With 19 launches in 2026 and counting, Rocket Lab is now the most frequent launcher of small payloads in the Western market, and the 100th Electron flight is close enough that the company can start planning the celebration. In an industry where development programs routinely slip by years, Rocket Lab's achievement is almost embarrassingly straightforward: it built a small rocket, and then it flew it — again, and again, and again.

## Why it matters

Space headlines tend to belong to the giants: the moon missions, the space stations, the heavy-lift rockets. But the quiet revolution of the last decade has been the industrialization of small launch — the emergence of a logistics system that moves small satellites to orbit the way cargo planes move parcels. HawkEye 360's growing constellation, listening to the planet's radio heartbeat from 520 kilometers up, is what that logistics system is for: not flags and footprints, but the daily, operational business of seeing — or in this case, hearing — what the world is doing.

When Electron's engines ignite above Mahia today, they will carry three more listeners into that system. The mission name says the company isn't fooling around. The manifest says the industry agrees.
