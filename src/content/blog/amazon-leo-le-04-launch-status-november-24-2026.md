Europe's most powerful rocket finally left the ground on Tuesday evening, carrying 36 of Amazon's internet satellites toward low Earth orbit and giving the battered constellation programme its first good news in nine days. The Ariane 64 lifted off from Europe's Spaceport in French Guiana at 18:14 WIB, at the very end of its 29-minute launch window, a day after a technical problem in the launcher's ground support systems stopped the first countdown before it could reach zero.

The mission, designated VA271 by Arianespace and LE-04 by Amazon, is the tenth flight of the Ariane 6 rocket and the fourth to carry satellites for Amazon Leo, the broadband constellation formerly known as Project Kuiper. The flight was previewed in detail in <a href="/blog/amazon-leo-le-04-launch-november-24-2026-final-preview/">Amazon Leo LE-04 launch November 24 2026 final preview</a>. If the flight completes as planned, with the last of the 36 satellites separating roughly two hours after liftoff, around 20:18 WIB, it will bring the number of Amazon Leo satellites launched by Arianespace to 136 and the total number of Amazon Leo satellites in orbit to 432.

That total matters more than it might look. Nine days earlier, on November 15, United Launch Alliance's Vulcan rocket failed on its return-to-flight mission and destroyed 40 Amazon Leo satellites, the first full mission failure for ULA since the company was founded in 2006 — see <a href="/blog/vulcan-lv-01-launch-failure-november-15-2026/">Vulcan LV-01 launch failure November 15 2026</a>. The loss froze Amazon's deployment count at 396 satellites — a figure that had not moved since early July — and left the company depending on the two rockets still flying for it: Ariane 6 and SpaceX's Falcon 9. Tuesday's flight was therefore not just another routine batch delivery. It was the first test of whether Amazon's constellation could start climbing again after the worst fortnight in its short history.

## A liftoff at the edge of the window

The day had not started smoothly. The launch window opened at 17:45 WIB and was due to close at 18:14 WIB. Tracking data recorded during the countdown showed the target time slipping: an initial target of 17:45 WIB moved to 17:46 WIB, then the countdown was placed on hold, then a new target of 18:14 WIB — the last second of the window — was set. Arianespace's webcast and live trackers confirmed liftoff at 11:14 UTC, which is 18:14 WIB, at 08:14 local time in Kourou.

The long wait was the second delay in two days. The original attempt on Monday, November 23, never got close to liftoff. Arianespace halted the countdown hours before the planned window after what it described as a technical event in the launcher's ground support systems, as covered in <a href="/blog/amazon-leo-le-04-scrubbed-november-23-2026/">Amazon Leo LE-04 scrubbed November 23 2026</a>, and the chronology stopped before the mobile gantry was even removed from the rocket. The rocket and its 36 satellites were declared safe and stable, and Arianespace announced a new window for Tuesday, November 24, from 17:45 to 18:14 WIB — 24 minutes earlier than the Monday window, not the same one.

That Monday scrub was itself the tail end of a much longer delay. Arianespace first announced the mission on October 14, targeting November 23 at the earliest. The original guide date had been October 7, so by the time the rocket finally flew, the mission had slipped by about seven weeks. Reporting during that period traced part of the delay to the Ariane 6 programme's engine troubles: in September, the European Space Agency acknowledged awareness of delays after reports of a turbopump anomaly, and French press reports described an anomaly in a liquid-hydrogen turbopump found during an engine acceptance test shortly before the summer. ESA's Director of Space Transportation, Geraldine Naja, told reporters in October that the problem was not something ESA was worried about, while also acknowledging that it was delaying a launch planned for 2026 into the following year. Neither Arianespace nor ESA has publicly confirmed which pump was affected, what caused it, or what the repair involved, and any claim that the turbopump problem is fully resolved should be treated as unconfirmed until Arianespace says so at launch.

The countdown that finally worked was therefore carrying more than 36 satellites. It was carrying the credibility of a launcher that has flown only nine times before, in a year when its programme has already slipped from a target of up to eight flights to at most six, and a constellation that has spent 2026 watching two of its three launch providers fall over.

## Two hours to deployment

A launch is not a deployment, and for Amazon Leo the difference matters. The Ariane 6 flight plan for VA271 is a long one: the mission was expected to last approximately two hours and four minutes from liftoff to the deployment of the final satellites into low Earth orbit. That is because the satellites are not released where they will operate. Amazon's mission plan puts the release altitude at approximately 465 kilometres, and the spacecraft then have to raise themselves to their 630-kilometre operating orbit over the days and weeks that follow, using their own propulsion, before they can be checked out and brought into service.

The flight profile, as published by ESA ahead of the launch, runs like this: the four P160C solid rocket boosters separate about two minutes and 23 seconds after liftoff, the payload fairing comes off at about three minutes and 12 seconds, and the core stage burns through its fuel and shuts down its Vulcain 2.1 main engine at about seven minutes and 45 seconds. The upper stage, powered by the restartable Vinci engine, then takes over for a long coast phase before a second burn circularises the orbit. The satellite deployment sequence begins about one hour and 39 minutes after liftoff and ends a little over two hours after liftoff, with the end of the broadcast scheduled at about two hours and nine minutes. On Tuesday's timeline, that puts the start of deployment at about 19:53 WIB and the final separation at about 20:18 WIB.

Spaceflight Now's live coverage, updated at 11:44 UTC, reported that Arianespace had launched its rocket and was in the midst of its first coast phase — the long, quiet stretch of the flight in which the upper stage coasts toward the point where it can fire again. At the time this article was published, the deployment sequence had not yet been confirmed, and the result of the mission — whether all 36 satellites separated cleanly — was still to be established. The safe result, with deployment confirmed, is scheduled for the Wednesday 07:53 edition.

The long flight is a feature of the Ariane 6 design for this mission, not a sign of trouble. The Vinci upper-stage engine can restart multiple times, and the profile includes a third burn after deployment. What it means for anyone watching is that a clean liftoff, however satisfying, is only the first chapter. The Vulcan failure nine days ago is a reminder of why: that rocket also lifted off cleanly, and the anomaly came about ten minutes later, during the Centaur upper stage's first burn.

## The rocket that had to fly

The vehicle that carried LE-04 was an Ariane 64, the four-booster version of Ariane 6, flying with the upgraded P160C solid rocket motors for the second time. The P160C is one metre longer than the P120C booster it replaces, carries 14 tonnes more solid propellant — up to 156 tonnes — and provides a 10 to 15 percent increase in the launcher's performance. According to ArianeGroup's launch kit for the previous Amazon mission, the Ariane 64 with P160C boosters can carry about 22 metric tons to low Earth orbit, roughly 48,500 pounds, and that extra lift is why a single flight can carry 36 satellites.

VA271 was the 363rd launch performed by Arianespace and its fifth launch of 2026. It was also a milestone for the Ariane 6 programme in a narrower sense: the tenth flight of the rocket overall, and the fourth flight of the Ariane 64 configuration. The programme has been ramping up slowly. Four Ariane 6 flights had flown in 2026 before this one, and the year is expected to end with six flights at most — five if the December flight, carrying the EUMETSAT MetOp-SG B1 weather satellite, slips into January.

The numbers tell a story about a launcher still finding its rhythm. Arianespace was chasing up to eight Ariane 6 flights in 2026. It will finish the year with five or six. That is not a failure — the rocket has now flown ten times and has not lost a mission — but it is a reminder that Europe's heavy-lift workhorse is still early in its operational life, and that Amazon's constellation, which needs hundreds of satellites a year, cannot be carried by a launcher flying five times a year on its own.

## Nine days after Vulcan

To understand why Tuesday's flight mattered so much to Amazon, you have to go back nine days. On November 15, ULA's Vulcan rocket lifted off from Florida on mission LV-01, its return to flight after a grounding that had lasted most of 2026. The grounding followed an anomaly with a Northrop Grumman-built GEM-63XL solid rocket motor during the February launch of USSF-87, which kept Vulcan on the ground for the majority of the year. The November flight was also the first flight of Vulcan's six-booster configuration, and it was carrying 40 Amazon Leo satellites.

Liftoff was clean. About ten minutes later, during the Centaur upper stage's first burn, something went wrong. ULA confirmed around 01:30 WIB that the mission had failed to deliver its payload to orbit. All 40 satellites were lost. It was the first total mission failure for ULA since 2006, and it was Amazon's second launch disaster of the year in a different sense: the satellites were gone, and the rocket they had been riding — the one that was supposed to carry 37 more Amazon launches, the largest single block of the constellation's manifest — was grounded pending an investigation, with no return date.

Amazon's public response was measured. "There is risk with any launch, and that's especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01," the company said in a statement after the anomaly. "ULA has been a trusted partner for Amazon, and we believe they'll learn from this mission and deliver on their long-term plans for Vulcan." The company also noted, in the same breath, that its factory in Kirkland, Washington, can build up to 30 satellites a week — meaning the 40 lost spacecraft could be replaced in roughly nine days of production. The hardware, in other words, was not the problem. The rockets were.

Vulcan was not the only provider in trouble. Blue Origin's fourth New Glenn rocket, which had been intended to launch 48 Amazon Leo satellites, was destroyed along with some launch infrastructure during an explosion on the pad during a static fire test of its BE-4 engines. The satellites were not on board when the rocket blew up, but the pad was out of action, and Blue Origin, which had been working toward returning to flight by the end of the year, is now expecting its first flight in early 2027. New Glenn carries 24 Amazon Leo launches on the manifest, and it has not flown a Leo mission at all.

That left Amazon with two rockets that were actually flying: Ariane 6 and Falcon 9. The arithmetic is stark. Before LE-04, Amazon had launched 396 Leo satellites across 14 successful missions since April 2025, roughly 12 percent of the 3,232-satellite first-generation constellation its FCC licence requires. The two rockets still flying, Ariane 6 and Falcon 9, could lift about 996 more satellites between them if every booked mission goes ahead — which would bring the network to roughly 1,392 satellites. The halfway point of the constellation is 1,616. Even in a perfect run, Amazon would end up about 224 satellites short of halfway without Vulcan and New Glenn.

## The deadline that does not move

The constellation's schedule is governed by a licence condition that has already been missed once. Amazon was originally required by the Federal Communications Commission to deploy half of its constellation — 1,616 satellites — by July 30, 2026. It did not come close. At the time it filed for relief in January, Amazon reported that it had launched 180 satellites and estimated that it would have deployed approximately 700 by July 30, 2026. In June, the FCC's Space Bureau issued a conditional waiver of the interim milestone, but the final deadline did not move: all 3,232 first-generation satellites must be in orbit by July 30, 2029.

The waiver came with a price. Satellites launched after July 30, 2026, temporarily lose the spectrum priority Amazon won in earlier FCC processing rounds, and that loss lasts until March 30, 2028, or until half the constellation is operating, whichever comes first. Amazon can cut the period to 15 months — ending in October 2027 — if it proves it has built half of its satellites and fully secured the launches for them. Spectrum priority decides who has to give way when two networks' signals could interfere, so without it the burden falls on Amazon to show its newer satellites will not disrupt operators such as SpaceX.

The numbers behind the deadline are unforgiving. After LE-04, Amazon needs about 2,800 more satellites in roughly 32 months — roughly 87 satellites a month, or more than two full Vulcan loads every month until mid-2029. That is why the company has been buying launches wherever it can find them. In April 2022 it signed contracts for 18 Arianespace launches, 38 Vulcan launches, and 12 New Glenn launches. It later added three Falcon 9 launches, all flown in 2025, converted an option to fly 24 batches on New Glenn, and bought 10 more Falcon 9 rockets. In September 2026 it announced six additional Ariane 64 launches, taking its Arianespace agreement from 18 to 24 launches, with the new flights beginning in 2029 and the package running to 2031.

It is also why the factory matters. Amazon announced in October that it had built its 1,000th satellite at its facilities in Kirkland, Washington — which means roughly 600 finished spacecraft are already waiting on the ground for rockets that do not yet exist in sufficient numbers. The constraint on Amazon Leo is not satellite production. It is launch cadence.

## What 432 satellites buy

A successful LE-04 does not solve any of that. It moves the total from 396 to 432, about 13 percent of the first-generation constellation, and it brings the Arianespace total to 136. But it does three things that matter. First, it restores forward motion after nine days in which the count had not moved at all. Second, it proves that the Ariane 6 line, the only European route to orbit for the constellation, is working after its own delays. Third, it keeps Amazon on track for the one milestone the company has set for itself this year: beginning initial commercial service in late 2026.

That service, when it starts, will be limited. Amazon has said it had enough satellites in orbit by early July to begin initial service, and it repeated on its third-quarter 2026 earnings call that the network remains on track to begin service this year. The customer hardware is ready in outline: three terminals cover consumers and enterprises, with the Leo Nano rated at up to 100 Mbps, the Leo Pro at up to 400 Mbps, and the Leo Ultra at up to 1 Gbps down, with direct links into Amazon Web Services. What has been missing is not the terminals or the satellites, but the density of coverage that only a much larger constellation can provide — and the launches to build it.

The comparison with the incumbent is unavoidable. SpaceX's Starlink is already up and running with thousands of satellites, and it launches on its own rockets at a pace Amazon cannot match by buying rides. Amazon's answer has been to spread its bets across five rocket families precisely to avoid a single point of failure, and in 2026 that strategy has been tested to destruction: two of its providers grounded, one rocket lost, and the remaining two carrying the whole programme. The strategy has not failed — the constellation is still growing — but the year has shown its cost. When Vulcan and New Glenn both go down, the manifest math stops adding up, and a single Ariane 6 flight becomes the most important launch of the quarter.

## A crowded Tuesday in orbit

LE-04 was not the only launch of the day. Early on Tuesday morning, Roscosmos sent the Progress MS-36 cargo ship to the International Space Station on a Soyuz 2.1b from Baikonur, and that mission was confirmed successful. Later on Tuesday night, SpaceX is scheduled to launch USSF-266, a classified Space Force mission on a Falcon 9 from Vandenberg, with the window opening at 22:55 WIB and the target liftoff at 23:08 WIB — the fifth government mission SpaceX has flown in November. Four launches were on the public schedule for Tuesday, and it was, as one launch tracker put it, a good example of how global the launch business has become.

For Amazon, though, the day's arithmetic is simple. One rocket flew, 36 satellites went up, and the deployment sequence will decide whether the count moves to 432 or stays at 396. The spacecraft still have to separate, raise their orbits from about 465 kilometres to 630 kilometres, pass their health checks, and be commissioned before they count as part of a working network. None of that happens tonight. What happens tonight is the first step, and after nine days of bad news, the first step was the one that mattered most.

The result — deployment confirmed or not — will be covered in the Wednesday 07:53 edition. If the satellites separate cleanly, LE-04 will stand as the flight that got Amazon Leo moving again. If they do not, the constellation's worst fortnight will have a third chapter, and the launch crunch that Spaceflight Now's live coverage named in its headline will have tightened further.

## Why the rocket crunch bites

Amazon's launch problem is not a shortage of rockets in the world. It is a shortage of rockets it has actually booked, that are actually flying, and that can carry its satellites in the numbers it needs. The manifest, as counted from figures published by Amazon, Ars Technica, and Spaceflight Now, shows the scale of the gap. Ariane 6 has 21 of its 24 contracted Amazon launches left, each carrying 36 satellites — 756 satellites in total. Falcon 9 has 10 launches left at 24 satellites each, or 240. Vulcan has 37 of 38 launches left at 40 or more satellites each, more than 1,480 — but it is grounded pending an investigation. New Glenn has 24 launches at 48 or more satellites each, more than 1,152 — but its pad has been out of action since the May explosion. Atlas V, which flew all eight of its Leo missions, is used up.

Add it together and the two grounded rockets account for about 61 of the 92 launches still left on the manifest. That is the rocket crunch in one number. Amazon spread its bets across four rocket families to avoid a single point of failure, and in 2026 it discovered that two failures can arrive at once, and that the providers left standing cannot carry the load alone. Even if every Ariane 6 and Falcon 9 flight on the books goes ahead, the network would reach only about 1,392 satellites — still short of the 1,616 halfway mark that the FCC waived in June, and a long way from 3,232.

The company has responded the only way it can: by buying more of what is flying. The six extra Ariane 64 launches announced in September take the Arianespace agreement to 24 flights, with the new ones beginning in 2029 and running to 2031. The 10 extra Falcon 9 rockets bought in late 2025 add 240 more satellites of capacity. And the factory keeps building — up to 30 satellites a week, with roughly 600 finished spacecraft already waiting on the ground. None of that changes the near-term math, but it does change the shape of the problem: Amazon's bottleneck is no longer manufacturing, and it is no longer money. It is the launch manifest, and the manifest depends on rockets that are, tonight, either flying or not.

Tuesday night, one of them flew. The Ariane 64 climbed away from Kourou at the last second of its window, its four P160C boosters burning, its upper stage settling into the long coast that will decide whether 36 more satellites join the constellation. For a programme that has spent nine days counting what it lost, that was enough to call the day a success — pending the deployment sequence, pending the orbit raising, pending the health checks, and pending the launches still to come.

## What to watch next

Three dates will show how deep the hole is, and how fast Amazon can climb out of it. The first is tonight, when the deployment sequence ends and Arianespace confirms whether all 36 satellites separated — the Wednesday 07:53 edition will carry that result. The second is the return of Vulcan, whenever ULA completes its investigation into the LV-01 failure and clears the rocket to fly again; until that happens, the largest single block of Amazon's manifest is frozen. The third is New Glenn's return to flight, now expected in early 2027 after the pad explosion, which will decide whether the second-largest block of the manifest can start moving at all.

In the meantime, the flights that are actually flying will have to carry the programme. Ariane 6 has one more flight scheduled this year, carrying the MetOp-SG B1 weather satellite in December, and Amazon's next Ariane 6 batch after LE-04 is not yet on the public schedule. Falcon 9 continues to fly Leo batches alongside its other work. And SpaceX's own government manifest rolls on tonight with USSF-266, a classified Space Force mission lifting off from Vandenberg at 23:08 WIB on a Falcon 9 booster flying for the 29th time.

For Amazon Leo, the story of 2026 has been a story of rockets: the ones that flew, the ones that failed, and the ones that have not flown at all. LE-04 was a reminder that the rockets that do fly still work. Whether that is enough to meet a deadline of 3,232 satellites by July 30, 2029, is a question that will not be answered by one flight, or by ten. It will be answered by the manifest — and by whether the grounded rockets come back.
