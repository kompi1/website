On a patch of simulated lunar ground inside a research hall in Tokyo, a four-legged machine crawled forward at three centimetres per second, its caterpillar tracks grinding over rocks and the rim of a crater-like depression. It was not much to look at — a squat test model, still years from flight — but the small crowd of reporters watching it on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, was looking at the first public steps of one of the most consequential lunar missions now in development.

The machine is the test model of the LUPEX rover, the centrepiece of the Lunar Polar Exploration mission, a joint project of the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) and the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO). If the schedule holds, the flight rover will land near the Moon's south pole in fiscal 2028, drill a metre or more into the polar soil, and answer a question that will shape the next half-century of space exploration: how much water is really there, what form it takes, and whether astronauts can one day turn it into rocket fuel.

"We ultimately want to use lunar water for rocket fuel," the mission's project manager, Takeshi Hoshino, told reporters at the unveiling. "That's why a key objective is to produce fuel on the moon for future missions, whether returning to Earth or heading onward to Mars."

The unveiling at JAXA's Chofu Aerospace Center was a deliberately modest affair — a driving demonstration, a briefing, a chance for reporters to see hardware that has so far existed mostly in diagrams and project documents. But the modesty of the event belied the scale of what is at stake. Water ice at the lunar poles is the single most valuable known resource beyond Earth. Split it by electrolysis and you get hydrogen and oxygen: the two ingredients of rocket propellant, and the two ingredients of breathable air and drinkable water. A reliable supply would mean spacecraft leaving the Moon would not have to carry all their fuel up from Earth, and crews could stay far longer than the few days Apollo allowed.

Nobody yet knows whether that supply exists in a form anyone can actually use. Orbital sensors have found hydrogen-rich deposits near the poles, and neutron spectroscopy suggests water ice lies close to the surface in permanently shadowed craters, where temperatures never rise high enough for ice to sublimate away. But neutron data cannot say how much water is mixed into the soil, how deep it goes, or whether it sits in pure, mineable layers or is scattered thinly through dry regolith. LUPEX exists to settle those questions on the ground — or rather, in the ground.

## The rover that walks on caterpillar tracks

The first thing that stands out about the LUPEX rover is its legs. Most planetary rovers roll on wheels: six of them, in the case of NASA's Perseverance and Curiosity on Mars, or the rocker-bogie designs that have carried rovers across the Martian surface for decades. LUPEX takes a different approach. The test model shown at Chofu has four legs, each ending in a caterpillar-track assembly, a combination of wheels and belts designed to spread the rover's weight and keep it moving across the loose, rubble-strewn ground of the lunar south pole.

The demonstration was designed to show off exactly that mobility. The test vehicle drove over simulated lunar rubble and crater slopes, moving at about three centimetres per second — a slow crawl by Earth standards, but a deliberate pace for a machine that must pick its way across terrain no one has mapped at the scale of its own wheels. It climbed obstacles roughly 15 to 20 centimetres high, a meaningful test in a landscape where a single boulder can end a mission.

Hoshino told reporters that scientists have been testing the design's performance under a variety of conditions, including "climbing slopes using a single leg, continuous driving using two legs, and mobility trials under vacuum conditions." The vacuum trials matter: a mechanism that works smoothly in a Tokyo laboratory can seize up in the airless cold of the lunar surface, where lubricants freeze and dust — fine, sharp, electrostatically charged lunar dust — works its way into every joint. Testing in vacuum chambers is how engineers find out whether a leg will still bend when the temperature drops far below anything it will ever meet on Earth.

The machine shown at Chofu is a ground test model, not the rover that will fly. That distinction matters, and JAXA was careful to make it: the flight vehicle is still under development, and the prototype's job is to prove the mobility concept before the real rover is built. According to reports from the unveiling, the prototype is due to go to India in 2027, where it will be integrated with the lander that ISRO is building — the first time the two halves of the mission will physically come together.

The rover itself is expected to weigh about 350 kilograms with its instruments, according to reports from the unveiling, although JAXA's mission page lists the figure as 420 kilograms including instruments. The difference is a reminder that LUPEX is still a moving target: mass budgets shift as instruments are finalised and structures are refined. Either way, it is a substantial machine — heavier than the rovers India has landed on the Moon before, and built to carry a serious scientific payload across some of the most difficult terrain in the Solar System.

## Why the south pole, and why water

The lunar south pole is not an obvious place to send a rover. The terrain is rugged, the sunlight comes in at a low, raking angle, and the most interesting places — the permanently shadowed regions inside deep craters — are places sunlight never reaches at all. But those shadowed craters are precisely the point. The Moon's axis is tilted only about 1.5 degrees, so the floors of craters near the poles can remain in permanent darkness, and in that darkness temperatures plunge low enough that water ice delivered by comet impacts over billions of years could survive indefinitely, frozen into the soil.

Scientists believe it is highly likely that water exists near the surface in these polar shadows. The LUPEX team hopes the rover will gather data on the quantity and quality of that water, helping to determine whether it could one day make space exploration more sustainable. The distinction between quantity and quality is important: water mixed thinly through dry soil at a few percent by weight is a scientific curiosity; water concentrated in rich, accessible deposits is a resource. LUPEX is designed to measure both.

The economics of the idea are stark. On the International Space Station, more than 90 percent of water is recycled. But rockets fly by burning oxygen and hydrogen, and the water produced by that combustion cannot be recovered for reuse, as Hoshino explained at the unveiling. Every kilogram of propellant a spacecraft needs for the journey home must be launched from Earth at enormous cost, or made where it is needed. If lunar water can be mined, heated, and split into hydrogen and oxygen, a spacecraft departing the Moon could refuel there — and the cost of going anywhere else in the Solar System falls dramatically.

That is why the south pole has become the most contested piece of real estate in space. NASA's Artemis programme is aimed at the same region, and <a href="/blog/artemis-program-moon-update-2026/">Six Months After Artemis II: Inside NASA's Reworked Plan to Put Astronauts Back on the Moon</a> describes how the agency's reworked lunar plan still centres on landing crews near the south pole. The water LUPEX measures would be the same water future Artemis astronauts might one day drink, breathe, and burn.

## A drill, eleven instruments, and a 100-day shift

Once it lands, the rover's work will be methodical rather than dramatic. Its first task will be to examine the surface and the layers beneath it, using its instruments to look for signs of hydrogen in the soil. If its instruments detect hydrogen, it will collect soil samples — drilling down to a metre or deeper — and heat them to vaporise the volatile substances they contain. Analysing the released gases will reveal the chemical composition of the samples, the amount of water present, and its isotopic characteristics, which in turn can tell scientists where the water came from.

The rover carries eleven instruments in all, according to reports from the unveiling. One of them, called REIWA, heats samples to measure the water they contain by weight. The name is a small piece of poetry: Reiwa is the name of Japan's current imperial era, and the instrument's job is to weigh the most precious substance the mission will ever handle.

The surface mission is expected to last about 100 days, with a maximum duration of one year. That is a long time to survive at the lunar south pole, where temperatures swing wildly between sunlit ridges and shadowed crater floors, and where a rover must manage its power carefully through the long lunar night. The rover's builders at Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, which is constructing the vehicle, have had to design for a world where the ground under the tracks may be as cold as anywhere in the Solar System, and where the dust is so fine and so sharp that it can wear through seals and coatings.

The drill is the mission's most important tool and its greatest risk. Drilling on the Moon is not like drilling on Earth: there is no atmosphere to carry away cuttings, no easy way to cool a bit, and no human hand to free a stuck auger. The drill must work the first time, in soil whose properties no one has measured directly. JAXA's engineers have tested the design extensively, but the moment the bit bites into the polar regolith will still be the most nerve-wracking few minutes of the mission.

If it works, the payoff is a ground-truth measurement that orbital data can never provide: not an inference from neutron counts, but an actual sample of polar soil, heated and weighed and analysed on the spot. That single measurement — how many grams of water per kilogram of soil, and in what form — will tell space agencies around the world whether the lunar south pole is a fuel station waiting to be built, or a scientific curiosity that will stay out of reach for another generation.

## Two countries, one landing

LUPEX is a genuinely joint mission, and the division of labour is clean. Japan is responsible for the rover and the launch: the H3 rocket that will carry the mission from the Tanegashima Space Center, and the rover itself, built by Mitsubishi Heavy Industries. India is responsible for the lander that will carry the rover down to the surface. Japan and India agreed in 2025 to jointly pursue the mission, and in India the flight is known as Chandrayaan-5 — the fifth mission in the Chandrayaan lunar series.

The partnership builds on a record both countries have earned the hard way. India landed Chandrayaan-3 near the lunar south pole — the first landing in that region by any country — proving it could put a spacecraft down precisely where the science is. Japan's SLIM lander touched down on the Moon in 2024, making Japan only the fifth country to achieve a soft lunar landing, even though the probe came to rest at a crooked angle. And Tokyo-based firm ispace failed in 2025 in its attempt to become the first private firm outside the United States to achieve a controlled arrival on the Moon, a reminder that landing is still the hardest part of any lunar mission.

For India, LUPEX is the next step in a lunar programme that has grown steadily more ambitious, from orbiters to landers to rovers. For Japan, it is a chance to put its robotics expertise — the same expertise that let its Hayabusa missions touch asteroids and bring samples home — to work on the surface of another world. For both, it is a mission neither could easily do alone: India has the landing experience, Japan has the rover and the rocket, and the science belongs to both.

## From agreement to the south pole: LUPEX timeline

- **2025** — Japan and India agree to jointly pursue the LUPEX mission, with India developing the lander and Japan supplying the rover.
- **November 25, 2026** — JAXA unveils the rover test model at its Chofu Aerospace Center in Tokyo and demonstrates it driving over simulated lunar terrain.
- **2027** — The prototype is due to go to India for integration with the ISRO-built lander.
- **Fiscal 2028 (April 2028 – March 2029)** — Planned launch aboard Japan's H3 rocket from the Tanegashima Space Center.
- **2028–2029** — Landing in the lunar south polar region; the rover begins its surface mission.
- **About 100 days after landing** — Planned end of the primary surface mission, with operations able to continue for up to one year.

## The wider race to the lunar south pole

LUPEX is not arriving in a vacuum. The lunar south pole is the destination of choice for almost every serious lunar programme now running, and the rover will land in a landscape that is rapidly becoming the busiest frontier in space. NASA's Artemis programme flew four astronauts around the Moon in April 2026, and the agency's plans still point toward a crewed landing near the south pole later this decade. <a href="/blog/artemis-iii-blue-moon-first-launch-2027/">Artemis III Blue Moon First Launch 2027: NASA Will Not Commit SLS Until the Lander Is in Orbit</a> covers the lander that is supposed to carry those astronauts down — a vehicle whose schedule, like so many in this field, is still being negotiated in public.

The science LUPEX does will feed directly into those plans. A crewed landing needs to know exactly where the water is and how hard it is to extract, because a crew that lands near a rich deposit has options a crew that lands on dry ground does not. The radiation environment matters too: <a href="/blog/artemis-i-crystal-detectors-2026-moon-radiation/">Artemis I Crystal Detectors 2026: Tiny Crystals Reveal a Moon Radiation Surprise</a> reports on measurements from the uncrewed Artemis I flight that are still reshaping how mission planners think about protecting astronauts on the surface. LUPEX's measurements of the polar soil will sit alongside that radiation data in the planning documents for every south-pole landing to come.

There is also a quieter, longer-term significance. The missions now heading for the Moon are no longer purely scientific expeditions; they are surveys of resources, and the countries that map those resources first will have a say in how they are used. Japan and India, by measuring the south pole's water together, are positioning themselves as stakeholders in whatever comes next — not just visitors to the Moon, but partners in deciding what a permanent presence there looks like.

That context also explains why the unveiling was a public event rather than a closed engineering review. A rover crawling across a simulated lunar landscape at three centimetres per second does not make dramatic television. But it is a signal — to the public, to the partner nation, and to the rest of the world's space agencies — that the hardware is real, the partnership is working, and the mission is on track for a launch window that is now less than three years away.

## What success would look like

The most important number LUPEX will ever produce is not a distance or a date. It is a percentage: the water content, by weight, of the soil a metre beneath the lunar south pole. If that number comes back high — if the drill pulls up soil rich in ice, and the REIWA instrument confirms it — then the conversation about the Moon changes overnight. Fuel depots, longer stays, and missions to Mars that refuel on the way stop being viewgraphs and start being engineering problems with known inputs.

If the number comes back low, the mission will still have done something no one else has done: measured the ground truth. Orbital sensors can suggest; only a drill can confirm. Either way, the answer will be a fact rather than an estimate, and the next generation of lunar missions will be planned around it.

For now, the rover that will deliver that answer is a test model in Tokyo, learning to climb slopes on a single leg and drive on two, grinding its caterpillar tracks over simulated rubble at three centimetres per second. It has a long way to go — to India, to Tanegashima, to the top of an H3 rocket, and finally to the coldest, darkest ground in the inner Solar System. On Wednesday, it took its first public steps. The next ones will be on the Moon.
