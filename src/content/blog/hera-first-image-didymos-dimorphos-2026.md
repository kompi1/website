For two years, the European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft chased a single point of light across the Solar System. On 27 October 2026, ESA announced that the point of light had finally split in two. Hera's Asteroid Framing Camera has resolved the Didymos system into its two separate members for the first time: the 780-metre asteroid Didymos and its 151-metre moonlet Dimorphos, seen as distinct bodies from a distance of 1,280 kilometres, while the pair sat some 197 million kilometres from Earth.

It is, by any ordinary photographic standard, a modest picture. Dimorphos occupies only around one pixel. But that single pixel carries the weight of an entire field of science. Until this image, Hera had seen its destination only as one unresolved dot. Now, in the words of mission scientist Michael Kueppers, "the smaller Dimorphos asteroid becomes visible for the first time." The spacecraft is no longer flying toward a smudge. It is flying toward a place it can see, in detail that will sharpen with every kilometre it closes.

## A picture four years in the making

To understand why one pixel matters, it helps to rewind to 26 September 2022. On that night, NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test, or DART, slammed a spacecraft into Dimorphos at roughly 22,500 kilometres per hour. The impact shortened the moonlet's orbit around Didymos by about 32 minutes, from 11 hours 55 minutes to 11 hours 23 minutes, give or take two minutes. It was the first time humans had deliberately changed the motion of a celestial object, and it proved the core idea of kinetic impact deflection: hit an asteroid hard enough, early enough, and you can nudge it off a collision course with Earth.

But DART was only half the experiment. Telescopes on Earth could measure the change in Dimorphos's orbit, yet they could not see the crater, weigh the moonlet, or tell scientists what the impact had actually done to its shape and structure. Ground-based observations are estimated to carry roughly 10 percent residual uncertainty, and computer models still cannot calculate Dimorphos's mass and makeup from Earth alone. That missing information is the difference between a stunt and a strategy. A planetary defence technique is only useful if engineers can size a deflection mission against a real threat with confidence, and confidence requires knowing exactly what happened at Didymos.

Hera exists to supply that missing half. Launched on 7 October 2024, the spacecraft spent two years crossing the inner Solar System, and its arrival has been choreographed with unusual care. Because Didymos's gravity is roughly 40,000 times weaker than Earth's, Hera cannot simply fall into orbit around the pair. Instead, it must slow down gently and fly a series of slow arcs past the asteroids, adjusting its path as it goes, like a ship easing alongside a harbour rather than docking at speed.

## The long, slow approach

The approach began to get interesting in early October 2026. On 7 October, exactly two years after launch, Hera imaged its destination for the first time, from approximately 236,500 kilometres away. In that first view, the entire binary system was a single pixel of light, bright enough for the camera to detect but far too small to resolve. It was a proof of direction more than a portrait: the spacecraft was looking at the right place, and the right place was looking back.

Then came the braking. On 15 October, Hera fired its hydrazine thrusters for 93 minutes in its first major braking manoeuvre, cutting its speed relative to the asteroids. A second burn followed on 22 October, when the spacecraft was about 2,300 kilometres from Didymos, trimming its relative velocity by a further 70 metres per second. Together, the pair of firings, a week apart, slowed Hera to just 20 metres per second relative to its targets, roughly 45 miles per hour, slow enough to approach gradually and safely. SpaceNews covered those braking burns as they happened, in <a href="/blog/esa-hera-braking-burns-didymos-arrival/">Hera Is Braking for History: ESA's Asteroid Detective Arrives at the Scene of NASA's DART Crash</a>.

It was in the window between those burns and the final approach that the new image was taken. On the evening of 18 October, with Hera about 1,280 kilometres from the pair, the monochromatic Asteroid Framing Camera captured the frame that ESA released on 27 October. Dimorphos, previously blended into Didymos's glare, stood clearly apart from its parent body. Kueppers explained the significance plainly: "Before this acquisition the spacecraft was still resolving the Didymos system as a single dot. But now, even though Dimorphos is made up of only around one pixel in this initial image, it is clearly well separated from its parent body."

Mission manager Ian Carnelli put it more poetically. "For the very first time we are seeing through Hera's own eyes the very double asteroid system that the mission was designed and built for," he said. "Our views will much grow sharper as Hera comes closer."

## Two rocks in a slow dance

The numbers that describe the Didymos system sound almost gentle, and that gentleness is precisely what makes the mission so demanding. Didymos measures about 780 metres across, a little smaller than the Burj Khalifa, the tallest building in the world, is tall. Dimorphos, at 151 metres, is roughly the size of the Great Pyramid of Giza. The two orbit each other at an average distance of just 1.15 kilometres, and Dimorphos circles its parent at about 18 centimetres per second, a walking pace. It is a slow dance in near weightlessness, and Hera must join it without disturbing it.

Binary asteroids like this pair are far from rare. They make up roughly 15 percent of all known asteroids, yet none has ever been studied at close range before. That statistic alone makes Hera a landmark mission for asteroid science, independent of its planetary defence role. Every close-up measurement of Didymos and Dimorphos, their shapes, their densities, their surface textures, their internal structures, becomes the first entry in a new catalogue of how double asteroids actually work.

The stakes of that catalogue extend well beyond academic curiosity. Asteroids do not arrive in standard sizes or compositions. Some are solid rock, others are loosely bound piles of rubble held together by their own feeble gravity. Computer models suggest Dimorphos may be the latter, a rubble pile, which would mean the DART impact deformed the entire moonlet rather than simply digging a crater in it. Whether that is true is exactly what Hera's close-up observations are designed to test, and the answer changes how engineers would design a deflection mission for a different asteroid. Push a solid rock and you get one result; push a rubble pile and the energy spreads, the shape shifts, and the debris cloud itself adds to the push. How much extra push the ejected debris gave DART's deflection depends on what Dimorphos is made of, and no one has measured that directly.

## What Hera will do next

The first resolved image is a doorway, not a destination. Hera's early characterisation phase begins on 4 November, when the spacecraft starts its close work at a distance of about 20 to 30 kilometres from the asteroids. During this six-week phase, the whole pair will fit inside the framing camera's field of view, and the mission will produce its first maps of both asteroids, along with its first close look at the crater DART left behind. ESA expects to unveil the first detailed images of Dimorphos on 25 November, an event planned for the dinosaur hall of Berlin's natural history museum, a fitting stage for a story about extinction and survival.

That phase is only the beginning. A closest pass of about 10 kilometres follows later in December, and in January 2027 Hera will release its two shoebox-sized CubeSats, Milani and Juventas. Milani will study the surface materials of Dimorphos by analysing reflected light and survey the dust around the pair, flying lower than Hera itself, from about 10 kilometres down to 2 kilometres. Juventas will go further still, sending radar into Dimorphos to probe beneath its surface, and eventually attempting to land on the moonlet to measure its gravity from the ground. If that landing succeeds, it will be the first time a radar instrument has sounded an asteroid's interior from within its own gravitational environment.

After the CubeSat deployments comes a close observation phase taking Hera down to about 4 kilometres from the asteroids, and then an experimental phase aiming for a single kilometre from Dimorphos. The mothership itself may end its mission by touching down on one of Didymos's poles, an option still under consideration. In total, Hera is expected to spend at least six months surveying the Didymos system, turning the aftermath of the DART impact into the best-measured collision in Solar System history.

## Why a deflection needs a second opinion

It is worth pausing on a question that the excitement of the new image can obscure: why does planetary defence need Hera at all, when DART already worked? The answer is that DART proved a concept, not a capability. A concept says that a spacecraft can change an asteroid's orbit. A capability says how big a spacecraft you need, how fast it must fly, and how much warning time you need, for an asteroid of a given size, mass, and composition. DART gave scientists one data point. Hera turns that data point into a calibration curve.

Consider the chain of unknowns. DART's impact shortened Dimorphos's orbital period by about 32 minutes, but the size of that change depends on the momentum carried away by the debris thrown off the surface, a quantity scientists call the momentum enhancement factor. Without knowing Dimorphos's mass, that factor cannot be pinned down. Without the factor, predictions for the next asteroid carry a wide error bar. And the next asteroid, if one ever threatens Earth, will not be Dimorphos. It will have its own mass, its own structure, and its own orbit, and the only way to predict the outcome of hitting it is to have a model validated against a case where every variable was measured. Hera is that validation.

There is also a subtler point. Kinetic impact is not the only deflection technique, but it is the most mature, and the one most likely to be available on short notice. Gravity tractors, which use a spacecraft's own gravitational pull to tug an asteroid over years, require decades of warning. Nuclear deflection carries political and technical complications that make it a last resort. A kinetic impactor can be built, launched, and flown in a few years. For that option to be credible, the physics must be understood, and Hera is the mission that finishes understanding it.

None of this means Didymos itself threatens Earth. It does not. The system was chosen precisely because it is safe, a laboratory where humanity could practise deflection without consequence. The method only works for threats found years or decades in advance, which is why the survey telescopes that find asteroids matter as much as any interceptor. Missions like NASA's NEO Surveyor, whose sunshade tests recently passed a key milestone as covered in <a href="/blog/nasa-neo-surveyor-sunshade-tests-asteroid-hunter/">NASA NEO Surveyor Sunshade Tests Complete: Asteroid Hunter's 20-Foot Solar Shield Ready</a>, are the other half of the planetary defence equation: you cannot deflect what you have not found.

## A mission built on autonomy

Hera's approach also showcases a quieter technological story: autonomy. Compared with ESA's Rosetta comet mission, which returned its data to Earth and waited for ground teams to perform all orbital calculations, Hera operates with far greater onboard independence, and its degree of autonomy increases as it draws nearer to the asteroids. That matters because of the distances involved. With the Didymos system 197 million kilometres from Earth, radio signals take many minutes to travel each way, far too long for real-time piloting around two tumbling rocks in near-zero gravity. Hera must sense, decide, and adjust on its own, with six different teams on the ground independently checking the data it sends back.

The braking manoeuvres themselves illustrate the discipline required. Slowing to 20 metres per second relative to the targets sounds slow, but it is the product of precisely timed thruster firings weeks apart, each one correcting for the imperfections of the last. The second burn was designed in part to compensate for any misperformance in the first. Around bodies whose gravity is tens of thousands of times weaker than Earth's, there is no margin for a heavy hand: too fast, and the spacecraft overshoots; too slow, and the approach stalls. The one-pixel image of Dimorphos is, among other things, evidence that the navigation worked.

## The long view: from DART to a defended planet

Hera's story sits inside a larger narrative about how humanity learned to take asteroid impacts seriously. For most of history, asteroid strikes were a geological curiosity, a theory to explain dinosaur bones. The discovery that a single impact could end a dominant form of life on Earth changed that, and the decades since have produced a slow, deliberate programme: find the asteroids, track them, and, since 2022, learn to move them. Japan's asteroid missions belong to the same lineage. Hayabusa2's daring flyby of asteroid Torifune, which revealed a two-lobed contact binary, as described in <a href="/blog/hayabusa2-torifune-flyby/">Two Rocks, One Snowman: Hayabusa2's Daring Flyby Reveals Asteroid Torifune's Secret</a>, showed how much a close encounter can teach that telescopes cannot.

What makes Hera different from those missions is that it arrives after the experiment, not before it. Every other asteroid mission has studied a pristine world. Hera studies a crime scene, and its instruments are forensic tools: cameras to map the crater, a laser altimeter to measure the shape, radio science to weigh the body, and radar to see inside it. The question is not just what Dimorphos is, but what DART did to it, and how the two answers combine into a recipe for the next time.

The timeline ahead is brisk. Early characterisation begins on 4 November, the first detailed images arrive on 25 November, and the CubeSats deploy in January 2027. Each milestone will sharpen the picture that began as a single pixel on 18 October. By the time Hera finishes its six-month survey, Dimorphos will be the most thoroughly examined small body in the Solar System, and the DART impact will be the best-understood collision humans have ever staged.

That is the real significance of the image ESA released on 27 October. A single pixel, separated from its parent by a few pixels of black, does not look like much. But it is the moment a mission stopped chasing a dot and started investigating a world. Four years after humanity first moved an asteroid, the spacecraft sent to understand what happened has finally seen its target clearly, and the work of turning a spectacular demonstration into a dependable planetary defence technique can begin in earnest.

The next time Hera looks at Dimorphos, the moonlet will fill far more than one pixel. The crater will come into view, then the boulders, then the texture of a surface that was rearranged by human hands. On 25 November, the world gets its first real look at the scar left by the first act of planetary defence. This one-pixel portrait is the promise that the close-up is coming, and that when it arrives, it will be worth the wait.
