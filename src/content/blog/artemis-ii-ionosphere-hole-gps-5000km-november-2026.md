Seven months after four astronauts rode NASA's Space Launch System around the Moon and back, the most powerful rocket NASA has ever flown is still making news — not for what it carried, but for what it did to the sky it passed through. A new preprint study reports that the Artemis II launch carved temporary holes in the ionosphere, the electrically charged layer of Earth's upper atmosphere, stretching roughly 5,000 kilometres across North America and disturbing GPS signals enough to shift ground-based position measurements by several metres.

The finding, reported by Space.com on November 4, 2026, does not describe a disaster. Nobody lost navigation. No aircraft went astray, no self-driving car swerved, and no drone fell from the sky. What the researchers found was subtler and, in its own way, more important: the biggest crewed rocket launch of the decade left a measurable scar on the layer of sky that every satellite navigation signal must cross, and the scar was larger — by some measures up to ten times larger — than anything left by the heavy rockets that came before it.

As launch rates climb and ever-bigger rockets fly ever more often, that scar is a warning written in the upper atmosphere. The sky, it turns out, remembers every launch.

## The flight that shook the sky

Artemis II lifted off from Launch Complex 39B at Kennedy Space Center in Florida at 6:35 p.m. EDT on April 1, 2026. Aboard the Orion spacecraft, named Integrity, were NASA astronauts Reid Wiseman, the commander; Victor Glover, the pilot; and Christina Koch, a mission specialist — joined by Canadian Space Agency astronaut Jeremy Hansen, the second mission specialist. It was the first crewed flight of NASA's Artemis program, the first human journey to the vicinity of the Moon since Apollo 17 in 1972, and the first time any spacecraft had carried people beyond low Earth orbit in more than half a century.

The mission lasted nine days, one hour and 32 minutes. On April 6, Orion swept around the far side of the Moon, passing within about 4,067 miles of the lunar surface, and the crew lost radio contact with Earth for roughly 40 minutes as the Moon blocked their signal — an expected blackout, and a test in itself. At their farthest, the astronauts reached about 252,756 miles from Earth, breaking the distance record set by Apollo 13 in 1970. They splashed down in the Pacific Ocean off San Diego on April 10, at 5:07 p.m. PDT, landing about 2.9 miles from their target after a flight of 695,081 miles.

For the crew, the flight was a triumph. The heat shield that had shed chunks of charred material on the uncrewed Artemis I flight in 2022 held. The spacecraft's systems performed. The four astronauts flew Orion by hand around the spent upper stage on the first day, a proximity-operations test that matters for every future docking with a lunar lander. By every measure NASA set for the mission, Artemis II worked.

But the rocket that carried them left a mark that no one on board could see. The Space Launch System is the most powerful rocket NASA has ever built, and its core stage — 212 feet, or about 65 metres, tall — burned through the ionosphere on its way to orbit, dumping exhaust gases into a layer of the atmosphere where free electrons carry radio signals. Where the exhaust went, the electrons thinned. And the upper stage, firing again and again as it pushed Orion first into a higher orbit and then toward the Moon, did the same thing twice more.

## Two holes, three burns, 5,000 kilometres

The new study comes from researchers at National Cheng Kung University in Taiwan, led in part by Earth science professor Charles Lin. It is a preprint, posted for the journal Geophysical Research Letters and available through the ESS Open Archive, which means it has not yet completed peer review. Its conclusions should be read with that caveat in mind — but the data behind them are unusually solid, because the researchers did not need a new instrument to see the effect. They used the GPS network itself.

About 3,500 ground-based GPS receivers across North America record, every second, how long satellite signals take to reach them. Because those signals slow down in proportion to the number of free electrons they pass through, the network doubles as a giant sensor array for the ionosphere. When Artemis II flew, the researchers could watch the electron density change in near real time, station by station, across a continent.

What they found, according to Space.com's report, was two broad disturbances. The first trailed the SLS core stage as it climbed through the ionosphere after liftoff. The second was associated with two burns of the rocket's upper stage, at an altitude of about 185 kilometres, that sent Orion on its way. Together, the disturbed zone stretched about 3,100 miles — roughly 5,000 kilometres — from east to west across North America.

The BBC's Sky at Night magazine, reading the same preprint, counts the events slightly differently: three distinct disturbances — the core-stage ascent, an apogee-raise burn about an hour after launch that lifted Orion's orbit, and the trans-lunar injection burn about 25 hours after liftoff that committed the spacecraft to the Moon. The magazine quotes the study's own summary: this is the first time a single space mission produced three instances of ionospheric disturbance over the same region during successive orbital manoeuvres. Whether counted as two regions or three events, the scale is the same, and it is the scale that startled the researchers.

The apogee-raise burn, in particular, "triggered shock waves and unprecedented large-scale disturbances that elongated 50 degrees of longitude across North America," the study reports. The disturbance from the upper-stage burns was stronger than the one left by the core stage. And the whole effect was up to ten times more intense than the disturbances measured after SpaceX Starship test launches — and, according to the study, larger than the effect of any rocket launch recorded to date.

## What a hole in the ionosphere actually is

The word "hole" is doing a lot of work in the headlines, and it deserves unpacking. The ionosphere is not a shell with a surface you can puncture. It is a region of the upper atmosphere, roughly 80 to 640 kilometres above Earth's surface, where sunlight strips electrons from atoms and molecules, leaving a thin plasma — a gas of free electrons and ions. Radio signals, including the GPS signals that rain down from satellites 20,000 kilometres overhead, must pass through that plasma to reach the ground, and the plasma slows them by an amount that depends on how many free electrons are in the way.

A rocket punching through the ionosphere does two things. Its exhaust — rich in water vapour and other molecules — reacts with the plasma and speeds up the process by which free electrons recombine with ions, thinning the electron population along the rocket's path. And the sheer energy of the burn can launch acoustic and gravity waves through the thin upper air, rippling outward like the wake of a ship. The result is a moving region of depleted electron density: a "hole" in the sense that there are temporarily fewer electrons there, not in the sense that the atmosphere has been torn open.

The effect is temporary. The ionosphere is constantly rebuilt by sunlight, and the depleted region refills as electrons are freed again — a process the researchers and commentators describe as the layer healing itself. Severe solar storms, by contrast, can disturb GPS signals for days. A launch's mark on the ionosphere is much shorter-lived, measured in hours rather than days.

That is why the headlines saying Artemis II "disrupted GPS" need careful reading. The study found changes in the ionosphere equivalent to GPS positioning errors of several metres at ground stations. It did not record navigation failures, and it did not document any user experiencing an outage. Solar storms remain the bigger GPS threat — NASA's new <a href="/blog/nasa-solar-flare-alert-system-november-2026/">NASA solar flare alert system November 2026</a> was built precisely because flares can disrupt signals for days, far longer than any launch effect. Space.com's report is explicit on this point: the measurements point to changes over North America, but the report does not document user outages or navigation failures. The tech press that has covered the story carefully has said the same — the disturbances are real, the positioning errors are real, but no one has shown that a vehicle or a drone was actually affected.

And yet the "several metres" matters. Co-author Charles Lin told Space.com that an error of just a couple of metres is enough to nudge a self-driving car into another lane, and that drones could be affected too. The United States National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's Space Weather Prediction Center explains the mechanism plainly: a highly disturbed ionosphere can prevent a receiver from locking on to a satellite signal, or cause it to calculate an inaccurate position. For a phone user finding a coffee shop, a few metres of error is a shrug. For a combine harvester steering itself down a field row, a drone landing on a rooftop pad, or a car deciding which lane it occupies, a few metres is the difference between right and wrong.

## A known effect, at an unknown scale

Scientists have known since the 1970s that rockets disturb the ionosphere. The launch of the Skylab space station in 1973 produced one of the first well-documented cases: a large, sudden depletion of ionospheric electrons above the launch site, detected by ground stations and studied for years afterward. In the decades since, researchers have tracked similar effects from Space Shuttle launches, from Falcon 9 flights, and from Starship test launches, building a picture of how exhaust chemistry and shock waves interact with the thin plasma overhead.

What is new about the Artemis II result is not the existence of the effect but its size. The SLS is a far bigger rocket than a Falcon 9, and it flew a far more complex departure sequence — a core-stage ascent followed by two substantial upper-stage burns, each inside or just above the ionosphere, spread over more than a day. The combination produced a disturbance that stretched 50 degrees of longitude and, by the study's measure, ran up to ten times stronger than the effects seen after Starship tests. The previous record-holder for launch-induced ionospheric disturbance, whatever it was, has been left behind.

That scale is the story. Spaceflight is entering its heavy-lift era. SpaceX's Starship is flying regularly. Blue Origin's New Glenn, which launched NASA's ESCAPADE mission toward Mars in November 2025, is in service. The SLS itself is scheduled to fly again — and NASA's plans for the Artemis program call for a cadence of crewed flights that would have been unimaginable a decade ago. If each heavy launch leaves a temporary scar on the ionosphere, the question is no longer whether the effect exists, but how often it will happen, how big it will get, and what happens when several large launches occur close together in time.

## Why Artemis II was the perfect experiment

There is an irony in the timing of this study. Artemis II was, by design, a test flight — a shakedown of Orion and the SLS with a crew aboard, flying a free-return trajectory around the Moon and coming home. Its departure sequence, with the apogee-raise burn and the trans-lunar injection burn separated by a full day, gave the Taiwan team something no previous mission had offered: three energetic events from a single launch, all passing through the same patch of sky, all recorded by the same dense network of GPS stations.

The apogee-raise burn, about an hour after launch, raised Orion's orbit so the crew could check out the spacecraft before committing to the Moon. The trans-lunar injection burn, about 25 hours after liftoff, was the big one — the push that sent Orion out of Earth orbit and onto its lunar trajectory. Each burn dumped exhaust into the ionosphere at a different time and a different place, and each left its own signature in the GPS data. The researchers could separate the three events, measure each one, and compare them with the effects of earlier launches.

The result is a dataset that does double duty. It tells scientists how a super-heavy rocket interacts with the upper atmosphere, and it gives launch planners a baseline for what to expect from the flights that follow. That baseline matters because the flights that follow are already being planned. Our <a href="/blog/artemis-program-moon-update-2026/">Artemis program Moon update 2026</a> has the full picture of how the campaign has been reshaped, and our <a href="/blog/artemis-iii-blue-moon-first-launch-2027/">Artemis III Blue Moon first launch 2027</a> report covers the lander side of the 2027 test flight. NASA's October 23, 2026, Artemis acceleration update reshaped the program: Artemis III, now planned for 2027, will be a crewed test flight in low Earth orbit, practising docking with the commercial lunar landers — Blue Origin's Blue Moon and SpaceX's Starship — before the first landing attempt moves to Artemis IV in 2028. The Artemis III crew was named on June 9, 2026: commander Randy Bresnik, pilot Luca Parmitano of the European Space Agency, and mission specialists Andre Douglas and Frank Rubio, with Bob Hines as backup. Every one of those flights will begin with an SLS launch through the same layer of sky.

That is the context in which a 5,000-kilometre disturbance stops being a curiosity and becomes an operational question. Launch weather criteria already cover winds, lightning, and clouds. The ionosphere is not yet on that list — but the Artemis II data suggest that, for the heaviest rockets, it may need to be.

## GPS, drones, and the machines that trust the sky

To understand why a few metres of GPS error is worth a scientific paper, it helps to understand how much of modern life now depends on satellite navigation being right to within a metre or two — and how quietly that dependence has grown.

A phone's GPS, on its own, is typically accurate to a few metres, and a few more metres of error barely registers. But the machines that increasingly share the roads and the skies do not use a phone's GPS. They use augmented, high-precision positioning: signals corrected by ground stations, fused with inertial sensors, and trusted to place a vehicle within a lane, a drone within a landing pad, a tractor within a crop row. Those systems assume the ionosphere is behaving normally, and they correct for its ordinary, slow variations. A sudden, launch-driven disturbance is not ordinary. It is a sharp, moving gradient in electron density — exactly the kind of anomaly that correction models handle worst.

The study's authors are careful not to overclaim. They report what the ground stations measured and what those measurements imply for positioning error. They do not claim that any autonomous vehicle was misled, any drone mislanded, or any aircraft misnavigated during Artemis II. No such incident has been reported, and the researchers do not suggest otherwise. But the implication they do draw is plain: as heavy launches become routine, the margin between "measurable in the data" and "felt by a user" will narrow.

There is a second audience for this research, and it is not on the ground at all. The ionosphere is also the medium for high-frequency radio communications, the band that long-distance aviation, maritime traffic, and military systems still rely on when satellites are unavailable. A depleted region can bend, absorb, or scatter those signals. The same physics that shifts a GPS fix can garble a radio call. For now, the effect is local and brief. For a future in which multiple heavy rockets launch in a single week from the same coast, "local and brief" is a description that deserves a second look.

## What happens next

The preprint's next step is peer review, and the numbers may be refined as reviewers probe the analysis. The core claim — that Artemis II produced the largest launch-linked ionospheric disturbance yet measured — will be tested against other datasets, other ground stations, and other models of how exhaust chemistry and shock waves propagate through the upper atmosphere. That is how science works, and the Taiwan team has made its data and methods available for exactly that scrutiny.

But the finding is already reshaping the conversation in two ways. First, it gives atmospheric scientists a new tool: the GPS network, built for navigation, is now also a real-time monitor of what rockets do to the sky. Every future heavy launch will be watched the same way Artemis II was watched, and the database of launch effects will grow with each flight. Second, it gives the launch industry a new variable to manage. Rocket exhaust is already regulated for its effects on the ozone layer and the lower atmosphere. The ionosphere, high above the weather, has largely escaped that attention. The Artemis II study suggests it should not.

There is also a broader lesson, and it is one that the Artemis program itself embodies. Artemis II was a test flight, and test flights exist to find the things nobody predicted. The mission's headline results were the ones NASA planned for: a heat shield that held, a spacecraft that performed, a crew that flew Orion by hand and came home safe. The ionosphere result was not on that list. It was found after the fact, in data collected for an entirely different purpose, by researchers on the other side of the world who were watching the sky with instruments built for navigation. That is the nature of exploration: you go to test one thing, and you learn something else entirely.

The Moon is still there, and the astronauts who flew around it are already part of history. The sky they passed through on the way is still recovering, one electron at a time — and the next time a rocket that big climbs through it, the whole world will be watching, in real time, from 3,500 ground stations and counting.

## The bottom line

Artemis II did not break the sky. It bruised it, briefly and measurably, across a stretch of North America 5,000 kilometres wide. The GPS network felt it as positioning errors of several metres. No user lost navigation, no system failed, and the ionosphere healed itself within hours. But the disturbance was the largest ever linked to a rocket launch — up to ten times stronger than the effects of Starship test flights — and it arrived just as the heavy-lift era is getting underway.

The next SLS launch will fly through the same layer of sky. So will Starship, New Glenn, and every other giant rocket now on the drawing board. The question the Artemis II study leaves behind is not whether rockets disturb the ionosphere — they do, and scientists have known it since the 1970s — but how the industry will reckon with an effect that grows with every tonne of thrust. The sky has always been the road to space. It turns out the road keeps a record of every vehicle that drives on it.
