Imagine a planet as big as Jupiter — a world so wide that more than a thousand Earths could fit inside it — and then imagine that it weighs almost nothing. Pick it up, and it would feel lighter than a bag of cotton candy. That is not a thought experiment. It is a real measurement, made twice, in the same star system, and today it is back in the spotlight as StarDate, the long-running astronomy programme from the University of Texas at Austin, features the system in its November 10 broadcast.

The two worlds are called TOI-791 b and TOI-791 c. They orbit a Sun-like star about 1,110 light-years from Earth in the southern constellation Volans, and they belong to one of the strangest classes of planet astronomers know: the super-puffs. Both are roughly the size of Jupiter. Both have densities lower than cotton candy. And both were found the hard way — flagged by volunteers, chased for eight years by telescopes on several continents, and finally weighed by the way they tug on each other as they circle their star.

The discovery, led by the University of Oxford and published in the *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society* on June 25, 2026, gives astronomers something they have almost never had before: two super-puffs in a single system, born from the same disk of gas and dust, locked in a gravitational dance that lets scientists measure them precisely. Only four other known planetary systems contain more than one super-puff. Fewer than 40 super-puffs have been confirmed among the nearly 6,300 exoplanets known to date. TOI-791 is, in the words of the team, a unique laboratory — and the James Webb Space Telescope is next in line to peer inside it.

## Two Worlds Made of Almost Nothing

The numbers are what make TOI-791 b and c so hard to believe. TOI-791 b is about the same size as Jupiter but carries just three percent of Jupiter's mass. Its density works out to only 0.038 grams per cubic centimetre. TOI-791 c is even larger than Jupiter, yet holds only 5.9 percent of Jupiter's mass, giving it a density of 0.047 grams per cubic centimetre.

To put that in context, cotton candy — the spun-sugar confection sold at fairgrounds — typically has a density of about 0.05 grams per cubic centimetre. Both planets are less dense than that. Jupiter itself, the puffiest planet in our own solar system, averages 1.33 grams per cubic centimetre, making it roughly 28 to 35 times denser than the two newcomers. Earth, by comparison, packs 5.5 grams into every cubic centimetre. If you could somehow scoop up a cubic centimetre of TOI-791 b, it would weigh less than a pinch of sugar floss.

"Only a handful of these super-puffy planets are known, and it is even rarer to find two in the same system," said lead author Dr. Georgina Dransfield of the University of Oxford's Department of Physics, who also presents for BBC Sky at Night. "Their extremely low densities make them fascinating targets for understanding how planetary systems form and evolve."

The pair are, by the team's assessment, the largest known exoplanets of their kind and the lightest planets ever measured for their size. That combination — enormous and almost weightless at the same time — is precisely what makes them so valuable. A planet's density is one of the few things astronomers can measure from light-years away, and it is a direct clue to what the world is made of. A dense planet must be mostly rock or metal. A planet as light as TOI-791 b can only be mostly gas: a vast, bloated envelope of hydrogen and helium wrapped around a comparatively small solid core.

That much is agreed. How such a world comes to exist at all is still an open question — and it is the question TOI-791 may finally help answer.

## A Discovery That Took Eight Years

TOI-791 b and c were not found in a single triumphant night. They were assembled, piece by piece, over eight years, by a collaboration that spanned space telescopes, mountaintop observatories and one of the coldest places on Earth.

The story began with NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite, TESS, which launched in 2018 aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket and now scans the sky from a highly elliptical 13.7-day lunar resonant orbit. TESS watches for transits: the tiny, regular dips in a star's brightness that happen when a planet passes in front of it. The depth of the dip reveals the planet's size. TESS has found around 6,000 exoplanet candidates to date, of which about 700 had been confirmed by September 2025, and in May 2026 NASA released the first complete sky map of all of them.

But TOI-791 b and c were difficult targets from the start. Their orbits are long — 139 days for the inner planet and 232 days for the outer one — so transits are rare, and each one lasts more than 11 hours. Catching a full transit means watching a single star without interruption for half a day, something a telescope in one place on Earth cannot always do. In total, TESS accumulated 1,122 days of observations of the system, spread over seven years, before the picture came together.

The first clues came not from professionals but from volunteers. TOI-791 b was first flagged as a possible planet in 2019, and TOI-791 c in 2023, by participants in the Planet Hunters TESS citizen-science project, who examine TESS data in search of worlds the automated pipelines miss. It is a reminder of how much of modern exoplanet science still depends on human eyes: the volunteers spotted the dips, and the professionals then spent years proving the dips were planets.

Size alone was not enough. To calculate a density, astronomers need both a planet's size and its mass, and mass is far harder to measure. The team could not rely on the usual method — the tiny wobble a planet induces in its star — because the planets are so light that their gravitational pull on the star is vanishingly small. Instead, they used the planets against each other.

As TOI-791 b and c orbit, each one's gravity tugs at the other, pulling it slightly ahead or behind schedule. The transits therefore do not occur at perfectly regular intervals; they arrive early or late by small but measurable amounts. By tracking those transit timing variations across years of observations, the team could weigh both worlds. It is the same technique that revealed the masses of the first super-puffs a decade ago, and in TOI-791 it worked beautifully — because there are two planets to measure, each one betraying the other's mass.

## Winter at the Bottom of the World

The hardest observations in the whole campaign were made in Antarctica. The ASTEP telescope — the Antarctic Search for Transiting ExoPlanets — sits at Concordia Station, a French-Italian research base on the Antarctic plateau, jointly operated by researchers from Université Côte d'Azur and the Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur with international collaborators. During the Antarctic winter, the region experiences months of uninterrupted darkness, and that darkness is an astronomer's gift: it allows a telescope to watch a single star cross the sky without daylight ever breaking up the observation.

That was exactly what TOI-791 needed. Each transit lasts more than 11 hours, and the ASTEP observations captured them whole — the longest continuous planetary transits ever fully recorded from the ground. Dr. Dransfield herself spent the 2021–2022 summer service mission at Concordia working with the telescope, and the photographs from that season show the small dome against a landscape of ice and unbroken night.

In all, the confirmation drew on almost a dozen ground-based telescopes on several continents, stitched together with the space-based TESS data. "These multi-planetary systems are complex, with gravitational interactions between the planets that evolve over very long periods, tens of years or more," said Professor Tristan Guillot of Université Côte d'Azur, principal investigator of ASTEP and a co-author of the study. "This discovery highlights the importance of continued international collaboration in astronomy. Bringing together observations from Antarctica, space telescopes and observatories across several continents was essential to revealing the true nature of these extraordinary planets."

## Locked in a 5:3 Dance

One detail of the system has attracted particular attention: the two planets are locked in what astronomers call a 5:3 mean-motion resonance. For every five trips the inner planet makes around the star, the outer planet completes almost exactly three. Their orbits are synchronised like the gears of a clock, and that synchronisation is no accident. Resonances like this are the fossil record of a planetary system's history — they form when planets migrate through their birth disk and their gravity catches hold of each other.

That history matters because it may explain the puffiness itself. One leading idea is that TOI-791 b and c were born much farther from their star, in the cold outer reaches of the protoplanetary disk, where gas could cool and pile up quickly around a solid core, building an immense, lightweight envelope of hydrogen and helium. The planets then drifted inward to their present orbits, where they fell into resonance with each other. StarDate's account of the system notes the same scenario: the planets might have been born farther out and migrated inward, a journey that could explain why they are so inflated.

The resonance also makes the system unusually stable and unusually measurable. Because the planets keep tugging on each other in the same rhythm, orbit after orbit, the timing variations in their transits build up into a clear signal rather than washing out into noise. That is why the team could weigh worlds that would otherwise be almost unweighable — and why TOI-791, rather than some other super-puff system, yielded such precise densities.

TESS has found other long-period planets before, and the difficulty of the TOI-791 campaign illustrates why they are rare in the catalogues. A planet that takes 232 days to orbit its star transits only about once and a half times a year, and each transit demands half a day of unbroken watching. Most surveys are built for planets that transit every few days. TOI-791 rewarded patience on a scale that few projects can afford — eight years, a dozen telescopes, and a winter at the bottom of the world.

## How Does a Planet Get This Puffy?

Scientists have not yet agreed on how super-puff planets become so large while remaining so light, and TOI-791 sharpens the puzzle rather than solving it. The leading explanation is the one sketched above: an enormous atmosphere of hydrogen and helium, gathered quickly in the cold outer disk, wrapped around a modest core, and then carried inward. But there are rival ideas, and the history of the class shows why none of them has won yet.

Super-puffs were first recognised in data from NASA's Kepler Space Telescope. The three planets of the Kepler-51 system, discovered in 2012, are all roughly Jupiter-sized, yet their low densities were not recognised until 2014 — and they came as a shock. The Kepler-51 planets are young, orbiting a Sun-like star only about 500 million years old, roughly 2,600 light-years away in the constellation Cygnus. They are, in the original descriptions, about a hundred times lighter than Jupiter for their size, with densities below 0.1 grams per cubic centimetre.

In 2019, a team led by Jessica Libby-Roberts of the University of Colorado Boulder used the Hubble Space Telescope to study the atmospheres of Kepler-51 b and d, finding a mixture of hydrogen and helium with a high-altitude layer of methane. The results suggested that the planets' youth is part of the answer: young planets are still warm from formation, and warm gas expands. The models indicated the Kepler-51 planets probably formed beyond the system's frost line — the boundary past which volatile substances freeze — and then migrated inward. Over the next few billion years, their low-density atmospheres should evaporate into space; Kepler-51 b may one day look like a smaller, hotter version of Neptune.

That story fits TOI-791 in broad outline, but the details differ in ways that matter. The Kepler-51 planets are infants, still puffed up by the heat of their birth. Whether TOI-791's star is similarly young is not yet established, and the two systems' architectures are different: Kepler-51's three super-puffs huddle close to their star, while TOI-791 b and c take 139 and 232 days to complete an orbit, circling at distances comparable to the inner solar system. If youth alone explained super-puffs, the two systems should look more alike than they do. If migration is the key, the 5:3 resonance in TOI-791 is a clue worth following — it is the kind of gravitational fingerprint migration leaves behind.

Another hypothesis, raised for planets like Gliese 3470 b, is that a super-puff's apparent size is inflated by dust lofted to the top of its atmosphere, so that what telescopes measure as the planet's surface is really a haze layer floating far above the solid body. And a 2026 study has theorised that super-Earths and sub-Neptunes are puffy when young and shrink over time, citing the V1298 Tauri and Kepler-51 systems as examples. Each idea explains part of the picture; none explains all of it. That is why a system with two super-puffs, born together and measurable together, is so prized: it lets astronomers hold one variable — the birth environment — steady while they test the rest.

## What Webb Will Look For

The next chapter belongs to the James Webb Space Telescope. The team proposes to use Webb to study the atmospheres of TOI-791 b and c, and the reason is simple: a planet that is mostly atmosphere is an open book. When starlight filters through that vast envelope during a transit, it picks up the chemical fingerprints of whatever gases are present, and Webb's infrared instruments are the most sensitive ever flown for reading them.

"This system offers a unique laboratory for understanding how super-puff planets form and evolve," said Professor Amaury Triaud of the University of Birmingham, the UK principal investigator of ASTEP and a co-author of the study. "We propose to carry out space-based observations using the James Webb Space Telescope to assess if the puffy atmosphere contains carbon-, nitrogen-, and oxygen-bearing species, revealing new insight into how these unusual planets formed."

Those three elements — carbon, nitrogen and oxygen — are the telltales of formation history. Where a planet was born in its disk determines which ices and gases it could gather, and the ratios of those elements in its atmosphere today preserve a record of that birthplace, billions of years later. If TOI-791 b and c formed far out and migrated in, their atmospheres should carry the chemical signature of the cold outer disk. If they formed where they are now, the signature should look different. Webb can, in principle, read that record.

The team also wants to learn how the planets' spin affects their shape, and how the tilt of the host star compares with the planets' orbits — measurements that can reveal whether the system was disturbed by other, unseen planets in its past. Further study could even show how the orbits themselves were shaped by interactions with other worlds. For now, though, the chemistry comes first: it is the most direct test of the migration story.

It is worth noting how far the field has come. When the Kepler-51 super-puffs were recognised in 2014, there was no telescope capable of reading their atmospheres in detail; Hubble's 2019 observations were a first, partial glimpse. Webb, launched in 2021, was designed for exactly this kind of work, and TOI-791 — big, bright, and bloated — is close to an ideal target. Its transits last more than 11 hours, which is a long time to stare, but it also means a long time to collect light.

## Why Two Super-Puffs in One System Matters

The rarity of TOI-791 is easy to understate. Super-puffs are already scarce — fewer than 40 confirmed out of nearly 6,300 known exoplanets — and systems with two of them are scarcer still. Before TOI-791, only four other systems were known to host more than one. That scarcity is itself a clue: whatever makes a super-puff is not something that happens easily or often, and when it happens twice around the same star, the shared circumstances must matter.

A pair of sibling planets offers something a lone super-puff cannot: a controlled comparison. TOI-791 b and c formed from the same disk, around the same star, at the same time. They differ in size, mass, density and orbital period — b is smaller, lighter and closer in; c is larger, heavier and farther out — but they share a birthplace. If the two planets turn out to have similar atmospheric chemistry, that points to a shared formation story. If they differ, the difference must come from something that acted on one and not the other: their distance from the star, their mass, or their history of migration. Either way, the comparison narrows the field of explanations in a way a single planet never could.

The system also sharpens a broader question about how common our own solar system really is. There is nothing like a super-puff among the Sun's eight planets. Jupiter, the closest analogue, is 28 to 35 times denser than TOI-791 b and c. Saturn, the least dense planet we have, still floats at about 0.69 grams per cubic centimetre — more than ten times denser than cotton candy. For a long time, astronomers wondered whether super-puffs were a curiosity of youth, destined to shrink into ordinary Neptune-like worlds as they aged. TOI-791, with its long orbits and resonant pair, suggests the story is more complicated — and more interesting — than that.

There is also a quieter lesson in the discovery, about how astronomy gets done. The planets were first spotted by volunteers. They were confirmed by a telescope that only works because Antarctica spends months in darkness. They were weighed not by staring at the star but by watching two planets pull on each other. And the paper that announced them lists more than fifty authors from institutions on several continents. It is the kind of result that no single telescope, no single country and no single method could have produced alone.

That collaborative spirit is visible across exoplanet science right now. TESS continues to find new worlds — including, in recent SpaceNews coverage, a <a href="/blog/toi-2535-b-disappearing-planet-november-2026/">hot Jupiter whose transits drifted and then vanished altogether</a>, a <a href="/blog/toi-2431b-hubble-secondary-eclipse-lava-world-november-2026/">lava world glowing at 2,524 K under Hubble's gaze</a>, and a <a href="/blog/toi-1355-b-transit-window-closes-2033-nasa-13-exoplanets/">planet whose transit window is closing in 2033</a> — and each one adds a piece to the puzzle of how planetary systems are built. TOI-791 adds a piece of a different shape: a system that should not, by the old rules, exist at all.

## The Puffiest Worlds Yet — and the Questions They Leave

TOI-791 b and c will not stay in the headlines for long; astronomy moves quickly, and there is always another discovery. But the questions they raise will linger. How does a planet grow to the size of Jupiter on a few percent of Jupiter's mass? Why did it happen twice, around the same star, in lockstep resonance? And what will Webb find when it finally looks through those enormous skies?

The answers, when they come, will say something about more than two odd worlds 1,110 light-years away. They will say something about how gas giants are born — including, perhaps, the one that dominates our own solar system. Jupiter may look solid and settled now, but it too was once young, warm and swollen with gas, assembling itself from the same kind of disk that made TOI-791 b and c. The difference is that Jupiter kept its mass and lost its puffiness, while TOI-791's pair kept their puffiness and never gained the mass. Understanding why two siblings took one path and our giant took another is, in the end, a way of understanding our own beginnings.

For now, the cotton-candy planets keep circling their star in the southern sky, too far south for most of the United States to see, tugging on each other every 139 and 232 days, and waiting for the most powerful telescope humanity has ever built to read their atmospheres. It took eight years, a dozen telescopes, an Antarctic winter and a small army of volunteers to find them. Finding out what they are made of may take a little longer — but for the first time, astronomers know exactly where to look.
