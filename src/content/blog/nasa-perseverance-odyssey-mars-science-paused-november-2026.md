Mars has gone quiet. Not silent — both spacecraft are still talking to Earth — but the flow of science from the Red Planet has stopped, twice over, in the space of a few days. NASA announced on November 19, 2026, that its Perseverance rover has paused all science operations while engineers investigate faults in both of its ultra-high-frequency radios, and that the 2001 Mars Odyssey orbiter has taken its own science instruments offline while its team works on a malfunctioning reaction wheel. Two missions, two unrelated failures, one shared outcome: for now, the most productive pair of machines ever sent to Mars is doing no science at all.

It is a strange and unsettling moment for a programme that has spent a quarter of a century making Mars exploration look routine. Perseverance is the rover that has been collecting rock cores in Jezero Crater since February 2021, building the sample cache that NASA still hopes to bring home. Odyssey is the orbiter that has circled Mars longer than any other mission has orbited any planet but Earth, mapping the surface, finding buried ice, and relaying data from every rover that has landed since it arrived. Between them they represent the two halves of Mars exploration — the machine on the ground and the machine overhead — and both halves have now stopped working at the same time.

The good news, such as it is, is that neither spacecraft is in danger. Perseverance is stable and communicating with its team. Odyssey is out of safe mode and responding to commands. NASA says both teams hope to return their missions to science by the end of November. But the details of what went wrong — a radio that stopped answering its own computer, a backup radio running at half power, a flywheel that stopped spinning after a quarter of a century — are a reminder of how much of Mars exploration depends on hardware that was never meant to last this long, and how thin the margins become when it finally starts to fail.

## The announcement

The news came in a post on NASA's Mars exploration blog on November 19, 2026, written by the NASA Science Editorial Team. In two short sections — one for each spacecraft — the agency laid out what had happened, what its engineers had found, and what happens next. The tone was calm and precise, the way NASA writes when it wants to be clear that a problem is serious but under control. "NASA teams supporting the agency's Perseverance Mars rover and 2001 Mars Odyssey orbiter missions are investigating separate technical issues affecting science operations on the two missions," the post began. "Out of an abundance of caution, science activities are temporarily paused for the Perseverance rover on Mars while ground teams work on issues with its two UHF radios."

That word, "both," is what makes the Perseverance half of the story alarming. Spacecraft carry redundant systems precisely so that one failure does not end a mission. Perseverance has two UHF radios — a primary and an identical backup — and the trouble began when the primary failed and the backup turned out to be faulty too. The orbiter's problem is different in kind but similar in shape: Odyssey has four reaction wheels, one of which already failed in 2013, and now a second has stopped spinning, leaving the spacecraft with no margin at all. In both cases, the redundancy that was supposed to protect the mission has been used up, and the teams are now working with the hardware they have left.

## A missing heartbeat on Perseverance

The trouble for Perseverance began on October 30, 2026, in the most ordinary way a spacecraft problem can begin: the rover powered on for the day's operations, and something did not happen. The rover's primary downlink radio is supposed to send a regular series of electronic pings — NASA calls them "heartbeats" — to the rover's main computer. On October 30, the heartbeats did not come.

"The rover's main computer continually listens for that ping," said Perseverance project manager Steven Lee at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California. "Every time it hears the ping, it resets a counter. However, if the radio stops sending the heartbeat due to a fault, after a certain number of missing heartbeats in a row, the computer swaps to the redundant one." In this case, there were 25 missed heartbeats, or about 100 seconds of silence.

That silence did exactly what it was designed to do. The rover's onboard fault protection correctly identified the missing heartbeats as a fault, powered off the unresponsive radio, swapped to the redundant duplicate radio that serves as a backup, and placed the rover into a protective safe mode, in which all non-essential systems are shut down. Safe mode is the spacecraft equivalent of sitting down and waiting for instructions: nothing breaks, nothing is lost, but nothing gets done either.

Engineers at JPL recovered the rover from safe mode and began working through the diagnostic data. That is when they found the second problem. The backup UHF radio — the one that had just been switched on to save the mission — was transmitting at about half of its expected power output. A radio at half power is not a dead radio, but it is not a radio the team is willing to trust with the rover's data either. The team has stopped using both radios until it can evaluate the potential causes of the issues and judge one or both safe to resume operating.

The consequence is a communications bottleneck. Perseverance's UHF radios are how the rover sends large volumes of data to orbiters passing overhead, which then relay the data to Earth. Without them, the rover is talking directly to Earth instead, using X-band communications over its high-gain antenna to NASA's Deep Space Network. That direct link works — the rover is stable and in contact with its team — but it provides a significantly lower data rate than the UHF relay path. So science and instrument operations have been paused to conserve bandwidth for diagnostic data. During the pause, the rover is not capturing new imagery from its suite of cameras, which means the stream of unprocessed, or "raw," images that appears on the rover's mission website has stopped updating. For the thousands of people who follow that feed every day, the silence is visible: the last images are from before October 30, and nothing new has arrived since.

NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, which manages the mission for Caltech, has assigned a team to investigate the performance of the backup radio and the options for returning it to operational status. Once that work is done, the team will turn its focus to recovering the primary radio. The agency says it is hopeful the rover could return to science by the end of November, and that it will provide further updates as the team works the issue.

## Odyssey loses a wheel

While the rover team was working through its radio problem, the orbiter team was dealing with its own crisis, 400 kilometres above the surface. Between November 4 and 6, 2026, the Mars Odyssey orbiter went into safe mode — the same protective shutdown that Perseverance had entered days earlier. When engineers investigated, they found that one of its reaction wheels had stopped spinning.

Reaction wheels are the quiet workhorses of spacecraft attitude control. They are spinning flywheels that speed up or slow down to change a spacecraft's angular momentum, and changing that momentum generates a torque that rotates the spacecraft — turning it to point its solar arrays toward the Sun, or its instruments toward the ground, without firing a thruster. Odyssey has four of them. One failed in 2013. At least three are needed to orient the spacecraft properly, and now a second has stopped.

Without three working reaction wheels, Odyssey must rely solely on its hydrazine thrusters to remain oriented. That is possible — thrusters can do the job — but it is expensive in a currency the spacecraft can no longer afford. After 25 years of operation in orbit around Mars, the mission team estimates that Odyssey has only enough hydrazine to orient itself without reaction wheels for one to two months. Efforts to refine those fuel estimates continue, alongside the investigation into the malfunctioning wheel.

There is a glimmer of hope. Although Odyssey is no longer in safe mode, its science instruments remain offline while the teams at NASA JPL, which leads the mission, and Lockheed Martin, which built and operates the spacecraft, continue investigating. They are running tests on the malfunctioning reaction wheel, and during one test the wheel was able to move after being commanded. Further testing is being conducted to confirm whether the wheel is recoverable. The goal, NASA says, is for Odyssey to return to science and relay operations at the conclusion of these tests and analysis during the last week of November, with further updates to come as the team learns more.

The stakes for Odyssey are higher than they might appear, because the orbiter is not only a science mission. It is also a communications relay, an essential part of NASA's Mars Relay Network, and it has been relaying data from NASA's surface missions for a quarter of a century — including the first post-landing images from the Phoenix lander and the Spirit, Opportunity, and Curiosity rovers. If Odyssey cannot be recovered, the relay network loses one of its oldest and most reliable links, and every rover on the surface loses a path home for its data.

## What Perseverance was doing when it stopped

The pause comes at a busy moment in Perseverance's career. The rover landed in Jezero Crater, a 45-kilometre-wide impact crater, in February 2021, and it has spent the years since climbing the crater's rim, drilling rock cores, and sealing them in titanium tubes for a future mission to collect. Earlier this year it set the all-time off-world driving record, covering 45 kilometres across the Martian surface — a story we covered in <a href="/blog/perseverance-mars-driving-record-45km/">Perseverance Just Broke the All-Time Off-World Driving Record: 45 Kilometers on Mars</a> — and it has kept finding new science on the rim, including corundum, the mineral better known as ruby, in three centimetre-sized rocks, as we reported in <a href="/blog/perseverance-finds-corundum-mars-rubies-jezero-crater/">Perseverance Finds Corundum on Mars: Rubies Hiding in Jezero Crater</a>.

The samples are the reason the pause matters beyond the rover itself. Perseverance has been collecting and sealing rock cores since it landed, leaving some in a depot on the Martian surface and storing others aboard the rover. The original plan to fetch them grew to a cost near 11 billion dollars, with Earth return not happening until 2040, and NASA cancelled it in 2024 as too expensive and too slow. This month, the agency began a fresh planning effort called the Perseverance Sample Retrieval Architecture, or PSRA, disclosed by Tiffany Morgan, director of NASA's Mars Exploration Program, during a virtual meeting of the Mars Exploration Program Analysis Group on November 9, 2026 — a plan we covered in <a href="/blog/nasa-mars-sample-return-psra-november-2026/">NASA Mars Sample Return PSRA November 2026: A Fresh Plan to Bring Perseverance's Rocks Home</a>. A PSRA strategy team will formally begin its work in January 2027, with a final report expected by the winter of 2027–2028.

That makes the radio fault more than an inconvenience. Every day Perseverance spends with its science paused is a day it is not adding to the cache, not imaging new outcrops, not filling in the geological map that the sample-return planners will use to decide which rocks matter most. The rover's samples are already sealed and safe, and the pause is measured in weeks, not years — but the mission's schedule is built on the assumption that the rover keeps working, and the sample-return architecture now being drawn up depends on a rover that can still drive, drill, and talk.

There is also a quieter consequence. Perseverance's raw-image feed is one of the most followed public windows into space exploration — a daily stream of unprocessed photographs from another planet, watched by scientists, students, and enthusiasts around the world. While the science pause lasts, that window is dark. The rover is not taking new pictures, and the feed has stopped. It is a small thing next to the engineering problem, but it is the part of the story most people will actually notice.

## What Odyssey was doing when it stopped

If Perseverance's story is about a rover at the height of its powers, Odyssey's is about a spacecraft that has already outlived every expectation placed on it — and is now running into the hard limits of age. Odyssey launched in April 2001 and entered Mars orbit in October 2001, for a mission designed to last just over two and a half years. It has operated around the Red Planet longer than any other mission has operated around any planet but Earth, and it currently holds the record for the longest continually active mission in orbit around a planet other than Earth.

In those 25 years it did foundational work. Odyssey created the first global maps detailing the composition of the Martian surface, which have become the bedrock of Mars geology. Its surface maps played a key role in identifying hazards at landing sites, spotting locations of scientific interest for landers and rovers to explore, and mapping water molecules in the top 3 feet — about 1 metre — of the surface, including buried ice that could prove useful to the first astronauts on Mars. That ice map, more than any single discovery, is Odyssey's signature contribution: it turned the idea of water on Mars from a geological inference into a mapped resource.

But for most of the last decade, Odyssey's most important job has been the one nobody sees. As a relay in the Mars Relay Network, it has carried data from the surface to Earth for mission after mission — the first images after landing from Phoenix, Spirit, Opportunity, and Curiosity among them. A rover's UHF radio can only reach an orbiter passing overhead, and the orbiter's job is to catch that data and throw it across interplanetary space to the Deep Space Network. Odyssey has done that job for longer than any other spacecraft, and it has done it while its own science instruments kept mapping the planet below.

That is what makes the reaction-wheel failure so consequential, and what makes the timing so painful. Perseverance's radios are down at precisely the moment when the relay network matters most — and one of the network's oldest orbiters is itself struggling to stay pointed in the right direction. The two failures are unrelated in cause, but they are connected in effect: the system that moves data from the surface of Mars to Earth is only as strong as its weakest link, and right now two of its links are being repaired at once.

There is a further wrinkle. Odyssey's hydrazine clock — one to two months of orientation without reaction wheels — is a hard deadline in a way that Perseverance's radio investigation is not. The rover can wait; its power comes from its nuclear generator, and its communications, though slow, still work. Odyssey cannot wait indefinitely. If the wheel cannot be recovered, the team will have to choose between spending its remaining fuel to keep the spacecraft pointed, or accepting that the mission's 25-year run is coming to an end. The fact that the wheel moved when commanded in one test is genuinely encouraging — a seized wheel does not move at all — but one movement is not a recovery, and the tests now under way will determine whether the spacecraft has months or years left.

## Why both at once matters

It is tempting to treat two simultaneous failures as a coincidence, and in the strict sense they are: a radio fault on the surface and a reaction-wheel fault in orbit share no common cause. But they share a common condition, which is age. Perseverance is more than five years into a mission whose prime phase was measured in a couple of years. Odyssey is 25 years into a mission designed for two and a half. Both spacecraft are operating far beyond their design lives, in an environment — radiation, thermal cycling, dust, vacuum — that wears down every component a little more with each passing year.

That is the real story of this week at Mars. It is not that two spacecraft broke; it is that two spacecraft that were never supposed to still be working have kept working for so long that their failures now arrive together, in the same month, in the same announcement. The redundancy that saved Perseverance on October 30 — the backup radio that took over when the primary went quiet — is the same redundancy that is now exhausted, because the backup turned out to be sick too. The four reaction wheels that gave Odyssey its margin for a quarter of a century are the same four wheels of which one failed in 2013 and a second has now stopped. Every layer of protection these missions were built with has been peeled away, one failure at a time, over years — and what remains is the engineering itself, and the teams on Earth who know these spacecraft better than anyone else alive.

Those teams have a good record. Perseverance's fault protection worked exactly as designed: it detected the missing heartbeats, shut down the faulty radio, switched to the backup, and put the rover somewhere safe. Odyssey's fault protection worked too: it detected the problem and put the orbiter into safe mode before anything worse could happen. In both cases the spacecraft did the right thing without being told. The question now is whether the ground teams can do the same — diagnose the faults, find a way to work around them, and bring two aging machines back to work before their margins run out.

The comparison with other veteran missions is instructive. Earlier this week, NASA's Terra satellite, which watched Earth for 27 years, was scheduled to end its science operations on December 1, 2026 — a retirement, chosen and planned, after a career that outlasted its six-year design life more than four times over. Odyssey, at 25 years, is in similar territory: a spacecraft that has already given far more than it was asked for, and whose end, whenever it comes, will be the end of a mission that succeeded beyond measure. Perseverance, by contrast, is a young spacecraft with its most important work still ahead of it — the sample cache it carries is the reason NASA is drawing up a new plan to bring those rocks home — and its fault is a problem to be solved, not a retirement to be managed.

## What happens next

For Perseverance, the path back is a sequence of investigations. The JPL team is studying the backup radio first — its half-power transmission is the most immediate problem, because a working backup would restore the UHF relay path even if the primary radio never comes back. Only after that will the team turn to the primary radio itself, trying to understand why it stopped sending heartbeats in the first place. The rover will stay on its direct X-band link in the meantime, sending diagnostic data home at the lower rate, and science will stay paused until the team is confident that one or both radios can be used safely.

For Odyssey, the path back runs through the test programme now under way. The wheel moved once when commanded; the team needs to know whether it can move reliably, hold its speed, and take its share of the attitude-control load. If it can, Odyssey returns to science and relay work in the last week of November, and the hydrazine clock stops mattering. If it cannot, the team faces harder choices about how to spend the fuel it has left, and how long the mission can continue on thrusters alone.

NASA has promised further updates as both teams learn more, and both missions are expected to be back at work — or to have a clear answer about why they cannot — by the end of the month. That is a short timeline, and it reflects the agency's confidence that these are recoverable faults rather than terminal ones. It also reflects the stakes: Mars science is a relay race, and right now two of the runners are standing still.

There is a larger lesson in the way this week has unfolded, and it is one that applies far beyond Mars. The spacecraft that explore the solar system are built to last years and end up lasting decades, and the longer they last, the more the mission becomes a negotiation between what the hardware can still do and what the science still needs. Perseverance and Odyssey have both won that negotiation for years — the rover by driving further than any machine has ever driven on another world, the orbiter by mapping a planet and carrying other missions' data home for a quarter of a century. Their simultaneous pause is not a failure of that bargain. It is the bargain reaching its limit, in public, in the same week, on the same planet — and a reminder that every day these machines keep working is a day they were never promised.
