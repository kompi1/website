For nearly 22 years, one small satellite played the fastest game in astronomy. It waited, patient and alert, for the universe to flash — and when it did, it spun around in about a minute to stare at the dying light. Now the game is over. NASA announced on October 23, 2026, that the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has conducted its final observation, and within weeks the spacecraft will tumble back into Earth's atmosphere and burn up.

Swift's end was not a surprise, but it was not the ending anyone had planned either. The observatory was supposed to die years ago, or to be rescued by a robot. Instead, an unusually active Sun dragged it down faster than forecast, and a daring commercial rescue mission came within kilometres of saving it before its own hardware failed. What remains is a legacy that astronomers say will shape the field for decades: more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts caught, more than 1,400 supernovae studied, stars seen being shredded by black holes, and an open archive of some 12 terabytes of data that anyone can still mine.

## The last observation

Swift's final day came on October 20, 2026. By then the spacecraft was orbiting less than 275 kilometres (171 miles) above Earth — well below the roughly 400 kilometres where the International Space Station flies, and far below the roughly 600-kilometre orbit where Swift began its mission in 2004. At that height, the thin wisps of the upper atmosphere dragged on the spacecraft too hard for its telescopes to hold steady on their targets. The observatory slipped into safe mode, and the operations team at Penn State University in University Park sent a command that effectively ended Swift's operations and prepared it for re-entry.

"Swift delivered an incredible, lasting return on investment for the nation, designed for a two-year primary mission but productive for more than 10 times as long," said Shawn Domagal-Goldman, director of the Astrophysics Division at NASA Headquarters in Washington. "In that time, Swift played a major role in advancing our understanding of the changing universe and was part of a revolution in astrophysics that showed how critical rapid-response observations are to the entire community."

NASA expects Swift to re-enter the atmosphere in the next few weeks. The exact day and place depend on solar activity, which changes how dense the upper atmosphere becomes, so no precise re-entry forecast is possible yet. The spacecraft weighs about 1,450 kilograms, and while most of it should burn up, some hardware could survive the fall. The space-tracking company LeoLabs told CNN that the probability of debris causing significant property damage or physical harm to anyone is very small, and that surviving debris most likely lands in the ocean or on uninhabited land.

## A telescope built to be fast

To understand why Swift's loss matters, it helps to understand what gamma-ray bursts are — and why they were, for decades, almost impossible to study. A gamma-ray burst is a brief flash of high-energy radiation, the most powerful kind of explosion known in the universe. Some last only a fraction of a second; others shine for a few minutes. Long bursts come from the collapse of massive stars. Short bursts, lasting under two seconds, are linked to collisions between neutron stars, the city-sized remains of exploded stars.

Before Swift, these flashes usually faded before large telescopes could find them. A burst might blaze for seconds and then vanish, leaving astronomers with little more than a rough direction in the sky and no fading afterglow to study. "Swift was designed as a satellite dedicated to studying gamma-ray bursts, outbursts that came and went so quickly that no one could target them with large telescopes to learn more," said S. Bradley Cenko, the mission's principal investigator at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center.

Swift solved the problem with speed and choreography. A wide-field gamma-ray detector, the Burst Alert Telescope, watched a large patch of sky for flashes. The moment it spotted one, Swift broadcast the burst's position to observatories on the ground and swung its own X-ray telescope and ultraviolet-optical telescope around to catch the fading afterglow. "This typically took Swift about a minute to do," Cenko said. That minute changed everything.

Swift launched on November 20, 2004, and NASA renamed the observatory in 2018 after its first principal investigator, Neil Gehrels, who died in 2017. It was built for a two-year primary mission. It kept working for nearly 22 years — more than ten times as long — and in doing so became something larger than its designers had planned: a general-purpose emergency telescope for the whole sky.

## The thirty-millisecond burst that settled a debate

Swift's most consequential early catch came in May 2005, barely six months after launch. The burst, catalogued as GRB 050509B, lasted just three hundredths of a second — 0.03 seconds — and Swift detected only 11 X-ray photons from its afterglow. It was a whisper of a signal, but it was enough.

Until then, short gamma-ray bursts had never been located precisely enough for telescopes to see their afterglows. Astronomers suspected they came from merging neutron stars, but they could not prove it. GRB 050509B gave them their first real foothold: a short burst pinned down well enough to study, and the result backed the idea that short bursts come from collisions of dead stars. It was a landmark in the young field of time-domain astronomy, the study of a universe that changes on timescales of seconds, days, and years rather than the aeons of traditional astronomy.

The confirmation would come in spectacular fashion more than a decade later, when the gravitational-wave detectors LIGO and Virgo caught the ripples from a neutron-star merger in August 2017 and telescopes around the world — Swift among them — watched the aftermath glow across the electromagnetic spectrum. But the first step on that road was Swift's thirty-millisecond burst in 2005, a signal so faint that only a telescope built to react in a minute could have caught it at all.

## The brightest of all time

If GRB 050509B showed Swift's delicacy, another burst showed its range. On October 9, 2022, Swift joined NASA's Fermi telescope in detecting GRB 221009A, nicknamed the BOAT — the "brightest of all time." It was the most energetic gamma-ray burst ever recorded, erupting from a galaxy about 2.4 billion light-years away, and it sent astronomers scrambling to telescopes on every continent.

The BOAT became one of the most studied explosions in history, and Swift's rapid X-ray and ultraviolet observations of its fading afterglow were central to that effort. The burst also underlined a deeper truth about the mission: Swift was not merely a burst detector. By 2022 it had become an indispensable part of the global rapid-response network, the instrument other observatories counted on to find a target fast and watch it fade.

That role extended far beyond gamma-ray bursts. Astronomers could request urgent observations that were commanded from the ground within minutes, and Swift's daily schedule could juggle up to 75 separate targets. It studied more than 1,400 supernova explosions, watched stars being torn apart by the gravity of black holes, monitored active galaxies powered by supermassive black holes, conducted an all-sky X-ray survey, and even turned its telescopes on asteroids and comets in our own solar system. Swift data has contributed to about 7,000 published scientific papers. Its archive holds more than 1.5 million images from its ultraviolet-optical telescope alone, and about 12 terabytes of data in total — roughly the equivalent of 600 high-definition films — all of it public.

The breadth of that catalogue is easy to underestimate. Swift's all-sky X-ray survey mapped the high-energy sky in a way no previous mission had attempted, giving astronomers a reference chart of X-ray sources they still consult today. Its long-term monitoring of active galaxies — the brilliant cores of distant galaxies where supermassive black holes feed — turned what had been snapshot science into time-series science, revealing how these cosmic engines flicker, flare, and change over months and years. And its willingness to point at anything, from a comet drifting through the inner solar system to a star being shredded in a faraway galaxy, made it the observatory astronomers called when the sky did something unexpected.

## Why a telescope in orbit falls

Every satellite in low Earth orbit brushes against the outermost wisps of the atmosphere, and Swift had no engine to push back against that drag. For most of its life the effect was gentle: at 600 kilometres, the air is so thin that a spacecraft can fly for decades. But the Sun runs on an roughly 11-year cycle of activity, and a period of intense solar activity in recent years heated and puffed up the upper atmosphere, so Swift met more air at the same height and began to sink faster.

NASA had put the point of no return for any rescue at around 300 kilometres. Once Swift fell below that line, no servicing mission could safely reach it in time. On October 20 it crossed 275 kilometres, and the drag became too strong for the telescopes to gaze steadily at their targets. The observatory that had spent two decades holding perfectly still to catch the faintest flashes could no longer hold still at all.

The irony is that Swift's long life made its end harder. Spacecraft built for two years rarely have to worry about solar cycles. Swift lived through two of them, and the second one — stronger than forecast — is what finally pulled it down. Its fate is a warning for other long-lived observatories in low orbit, which face the same physics as the Sun's activity rises and falls.

## The rescue that came very, very close

NASA did try to save Swift. The agency paid Katalyst Space, a company based in Flagstaff, Arizona, 30 million dollars to build a robotic spacecraft called LINK that would meet Swift, grab it with robotic arms, and lift it back toward its original altitude. It would have been the first time a commercial robot captured an uncrewed government satellite that was never built to be serviced. Swift has no handles or fixtures for a servicer to latch on to, which made the attempt a genuine first.

Katalyst built LINK in less than a year and launched it on July 3, 2026, aboard a Pegasus XL rocket dropped from a jet aircraft over Kwajalein Atoll in the Marshall Islands. NASA signed the contract on September 25, 2025, so the whole effort — from agreement to launch — took barely nine months. About a week into the flight, a power switch failure hit one of LINK's reaction wheels, the spinning flywheels a spacecraft uses to point itself. An unresponsive thruster valve then sent LINK into a spin, and a second switch failure followed. With two of its three reaction wheels out, the propellant spent stopping the spin left too little for a safe capture.

NASA and Katalyst called off the rescue on August 19. LINK still flew within 12 to 15 kilometres of Swift and photographed it before burning up itself in late September. "We came very, very close," Kieran Wilson of Katalyst told an astronomy committee of the US National Academies. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman put it this way: "This is not the outcome we were working toward, but it does not change why this mission was worth attempting."

On the ground, the Penn State team that flies Swift tried to buy time while the rescue was being prepared. Controllers swapped science targets for sky positions that held the spacecraft in its most streamlined attitude, giving up pointed science for months in exchange for less drag. John Nousek, the mission director at Penn State, described the effort as "pioneering new methods to minimize drag experienced by the spacecraft." Once the rescue was scaled back, Swift went back to work with two of its three instruments, squeezing out hundreds more science targets — from the remnants of stellar explosions to active supermassive black holes in distant galaxies and comets in our own solar system — before drag ended its observations for good.

The failed rescue still proved something important. A small company built and flew a robotic servicer in less than a year, with thrusters and arms that worked, and it rendezvoused with its target. What it never proved was the hard part: catching a spacecraft that was never built to be caught. That lesson now hangs over every proposal to service ageing satellites in orbit, including telescopes far more famous than Swift.

## What fills the gap?

The bigger open question is what replaces Swift. NASA has not named a successor. Domagal-Goldman said the agency is "exploring our ability to use and augment NASA's fleet of existing space telescopes, from fast-to-orbit commercial capabilities to efficient, innovative new missions."

Other spacecraft already hunt for cosmic flashes. The Chinese-French SVOM mission and the Chinese-led Einstein Probe both watch the sky for bursts, and NASA's Fermi telescope still monitors the gamma-ray sky. But none of them combines Swift's three talents in one spacecraft: spotting a burst across a wide field, swinging around within about a minute, and studying the fading afterglow in X-rays and ultraviolet light with the same observatory. Swift was distinctive precisely because of that combination of rapid detection and multiple wavelengths, and how well the mix of successors replaces it is something astronomers will only learn in practice, the next time the sky flashes and the world scrambles to look.

Swift's end also sharpens a second question, one that has nothing to do with gamma-ray bursts. Satellites are living longer than their designers planned — Swift lasted more than ten times its design life — and that makes in-orbit maintenance increasingly important. The LINK rescue showed both halves of that lesson at once: a servicer can be built and flown in under a year and can find its target, but grabbing a spacecraft that was never designed to be grabbed remains an unsolved problem. Every future servicing proposal will be measured against those two facts.

For now, Swift's greatest legacy may be the observing strategy it helped establish: detect an event quickly, locate it precisely, respond immediately, and make the resulting data available for continued discovery. That playbook now drives much of modern time-domain astronomy, from gravitational-wave follow-up campaigns to the nightly surveys that scan the sky for supernovae and asteroids. Swift did not invent the idea that the sky changes fast, but it proved — night after night, burst after burst, for 22 years — that building a telescope around that idea was worth it.

## A farewell written in the archive

Swift's fall, expected within weeks, will be unremarkable to watch: a streak of light, most likely over an ocean, the end of 1,450 kilograms of aluminium, glass, and circuitry that spent two decades staring into the dark. There will be no recovery, no museum piece, no final photograph of the spacecraft itself — LINK's flyby images, taken from a dozen kilometres away, are the closest thing to a portrait of its last days.

What survives is the data. The Swift archive stays open to researchers, and Domagal-Goldman says it "will fuel new discoveries for decades more." Somewhere in those 12 terabytes, in the 1.5 million ultraviolet images and the thousands of burst light curves, there may be signals nobody has noticed yet: a strange afterglow, an unusual supernova, a burst that does not fit the pattern. Swift spent its life catching flashes that lasted seconds. Its afterlife may last far longer — a telescope that stopped looking at the sky but never stopped teaching us how to see it.
