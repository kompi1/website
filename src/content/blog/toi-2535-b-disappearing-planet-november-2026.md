For seven years, a planet a thousand light-years away kept an appointment with its star, crossing its face on a schedule astronomers could set their clocks by. Then the appointments started slipping. A transit that should have arrived on time came six hours early, then six hours late. The dips in starlight grew shorter and shallower, as if the planet were crossing nearer and nearer to the star's edge. And then, in 2026, the transits stopped coming at all. The planet had not been destroyed, and it had not left its system. It had simply tilted out of view — pushed sideways by the gravity of a neighbor no telescope has yet seen. Astronomers at the University of California, Los Angeles, who followed the whole slow vanishing act, call TOI-2535 b the first known disappearing planet, and its story is already rewriting ideas about how the strangest class of exoplanets gets built.

TOI-2535 b is a hot Jupiter, a gas giant that orbits extremely close to its host star. It whips around its star in only 8.25 days, roughly ten times faster than Mercury takes to complete a single lap of the Sun. At that distance, a Jupiter-sized world is baked by stellar radiation, its atmosphere heated to extremes, its year shorter than a working week on Earth. Hundreds of worlds like it have been found since the first hot Jupiters turned up in the 1990s, and they have puzzled astronomers from the start, because the standard picture of planet formation says giant planets need cold, icy material to grow — material that simply does not exist that close to a star. Every hot Jupiter must therefore have travelled inward from somewhere farther out, and the question of how it made that journey is one of the liveliest debates in exoplanet science. TOI-2535 b has now handed astronomers a rare piece of evidence in that debate, written across seven years of observations: a planet whose orbit is wobbling like a top, tilted by a companion that is still hiding.

## The Vanishing Act

The discovery began, as so many exoplanet stories do, with NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite, or TESS. The spacecraft watches patches of sky for the tiny, regular dips in a star's brightness that betray a planet passing in front of it. A transit only happens when the star, the planet, and the telescope line up almost exactly, and the dip repeats once per orbit, giving astronomers the planet's period and a measure of its size. TOI-2535 b was identified in TESS images of its star system taken between April 2019 and March 2026, and at first it behaved exactly as expected. Transit after transit arrived on schedule, the signature of a Jupiter-sized world circling its star every 8.25 days.

Then the schedule began to fray. Because TESS returned to the same region of the sky numerous times over the years, the team could track the planet's transits across a long baseline, and the pattern they found was unlike anything seen before in a transiting system. The transits did not follow a regular schedule the way most exoplanets' do. They ran dramatically early or late, by up to six hours. For a planet whose entire orbit lasts barely more than eight days, a six-hour swing is enormous — the equivalent of a commuter train that sometimes arrives half a day off timetable. And the timing was not the only thing changing. The transits were also getting shorter and shallower over the years, the dips in starlight shrinking as though the planet were crossing its star closer and closer to the edge, clipping the star's rim rather than sweeping across its full face. Then, in 2026, they failed to appear at all.

The UCLA-led team, headed by Samuel Yee and Erik Petigura, spent months making sure the disappearance was real and not an artifact of the data. The conclusion, published in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*, is that the planet is still there, still orbiting its star every 8.25 days, but its orbit has tilted just enough that it no longer passes between its star and Earth. From our vantage point, the planet now crosses above or below the star's disk, and its transits have gone dark. "We think of the universe as resembling clockwork, where everything is regular, but that's not true," said Yee, a 51 Pegasi b postdoctoral fellow at UCLA and the paper's corresponding author. "If there are multiple planets in a system, they can affect each other's orbits. We know of a few systems that are like this. This is the most extreme example we've seen because the two planets are interacting so strongly. The planet's orbit is tilting so quickly that its transit disappeared while we were watching."

That last phrase is what makes the discovery so unusual. Astronomers are used to reconstructing slow changes from snapshots taken years apart, or to inferring motion from a handful of data points. Here, the change happened on a human timescale, inside a single observing campaign. TESS watched the planet arrive, watched its timing wander, watched its transits thin to nothing, and caught the whole performance in real time. It is the first time a planet's transits have been seen to vanish while astronomers were looking.

## A Wobble Like a Top

The culprit is a planet that has never been directly seen. The team's calculations show that TOI-2535 b has a neighbor whose orbit is tilted relative to its own, and the two worlds are locked in a gravitational dialogue that makes each one's orbit wobble like a spinning top. As the wobble slowly tips TOI-2535 b's orbital plane, the planet's path across its star drifts toward the star's edge and then past it, out of alignment with Earth. The same wobble will eventually tip the planet back into view, but not soon. The astronomers calculate that TOI-2535 b will next transit its star sometime in 30 to 50 years, while its hidden neighbor may come into view sooner, if its own tilted orbit swings into alignment first.

The mechanism is precession, the same slow wobble that makes a spinning top trace circles with its axis, and it is familiar from closer to home. Petigura drew the parallel with the Moon: "If the moon's orbit around the Earth was perfectly aligned with the Earth's orbit around the sun, we would see an eclipse every month," he said. "But because the moon's orbit is tilted, eclipses are less common. The tilt of the moon's orbit is a relic of its violent formation history, which resulted from a collision with the early Earth." The solar eclipse, in other words, is rare precisely because orbital planes are rarely aligned — and TOI-2535 b's transits vanished for exactly the same reason. Its orbital plane tilted away from our line of sight, and the eclipse stopped happening.

What is striking is the speed of the change. Orbital precession in most planetary systems is a slow, stately affair, measured over thousands or millions of years. In the TOI-2535 system, the gravitational interaction is so strong that the tilt changed enough in just a few years to erase the transits entirely. That strength of interaction points to a neighbor that is massive and close — close enough and heavy enough to shove a Jupiter-sized world around on a timescale of years rather than eons. Hot Jupiters do not usually have massive neighbors close enough to affect their orbits, the team noted, which is part of what makes this system so valuable: it is an outlier that can test the rules.

The ground-based follow-up mattered as much as the space data. The team determined that TOI-2535 b is an extremely hot, Jupiter-sized planet based on the size of its transits and precise observations from the WIYN 3.5-meter telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona. The WIYN measurements pinned down the planet's nature — a genuine gas giant, not a small star or a false signal — and gave the team confidence that the fading transits were a real geometric effect rather than a misidentified object. It is a reminder that the exoplanet field still runs on a partnership between space telescopes that find the planets and ground telescopes that confirm what they are.

## Seven Years of Watching

The timeline of the discovery is itself a small lesson in how modern astronomy works: a space telescope revisiting the same sky, year after year, until a pattern too slow to notice in a single visit emerges from the accumulated record.

## Timeline: The Disappearance of TOI-2535 b

- **April 2019** — TESS begins imaging the TOI-2535 star system. TOI-2535 b is identified from its regular transits, a Jupiter-sized world on an 8.25-day orbit.
- **2019–2023** — Repeated TESS visits to the region build a multi-year baseline. The transits begin to arrive early or late, drifting by up to six hours from a strict schedule.
- **2023–2025** — The transits grow shorter and shallower as the planet's path drifts toward the edge of its star's disk, clipping the rim instead of crossing the full face.
- **2026** — The transits fail to appear at all. The planet's orbit has tilted out of alignment with Earth, and TOI-2535 b stops transiting.
- **9 November 2026** — Yee and colleagues publish the result in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*, identifying the system as the first known disappearing planet.
- **2056–2076** — The predicted window for the planet's next transit, 30 to 50 years after the disappearance, as the wobble tips its orbit back into alignment.

Every date and figure in that timeline comes from the published research and the reports that followed it. The long baseline is the point: a planet's orbit can tilt enough to erase its transits in just a handful of years, but only a telescope that keeps coming back to the same stars can catch it happening. TESS was built for exactly that kind of patient, repeated watching. It scans the sky in sectors, returns to the same regions, and builds up years of brightness measurements for hundreds of thousands of stars. Without that archive, TOI-2535 b would have looked like a planet that simply stopped transiting for no reason — or, more likely, like a planet that was never there at all, because a single season of observations in 2026 would have found no transits to report.

The seven-year arc also matters for what it says about the census of exoplanets itself. The transit method, which has found most of the known exoplanets, only works when a planet's orbit happens to be aligned with Earth. Astronomers have always known that for every transiting planet there must be many more whose orbits are tilted away from us, invisible to the method. TOI-2535 b is a case of a planet crossing that boundary in real time, moving from the visible column of the census into the invisible one — and, in a few decades, back again. It is a live demonstration of the selection effect that shapes everything we think we know about how common planets are, and a reminder that the catalog is a snapshot of a moving sky.

The comparison with Mercury is worth pausing on. Mercury, the innermost planet of our own solar system, takes 88 days to circle the Sun, and it is already a scorched world. TOI-2535 b completes its year in 8.25 days — more than ten times faster — around a star a thousand light-years away. That is the defining strangeness of the hot Jupiters: worlds the size of Jupiter, which in our solar system sits five times farther from the Sun than Earth does, crammed into orbits tighter than Mercury's. Understanding how they got there has been an open problem for three decades, and TOI-2535 b has just added a fresh clue.

## Two Roads to a Hot Jupiter

The formation puzzle is the deeper reason TOI-2535 b matters. Giant planets are thought to form far from their stars, where it is cold enough for ices to help a rocky core grow massive enough to grab hold of hydrogen and helium. A hot Jupiter, sitting a small fraction of an astronomical unit from its star, must have moved inward after forming — but how? Yee and Petigura are working on exactly that question, and they describe two broad possibilities.

In the first scenario, the journey is violent. The planet is kicked into a stretched, elliptical orbit — perhaps by a near-collision with another object — and then interactions with the star gradually drag it inward, circularizing the orbit close to the star. This pathway leaves a signature: a system that has been dynamically stirred, with tilted, eccentric orbits as the scars of the encounter. In the second scenario, the planet is pulled inward more gently, spiraling through the gas of the protoplanetary disk in which it was born, along with other planets. That kind of migration is expected to leave a "flat" system, with the planets' orbits neatly aligned in the same plane, like grooves on a record, and without the tilts between planets that a violent history would produce.

TOI-2535 b's wobble points toward something in between — or at least toward a history more complicated than the gentle picture. The planet has a close neighbor, and the two worlds are tilting each other's orbits. The wobble suggests that components of the system may have interacted with each other in ways that, over time, tilted the orbits of the planets, similar to how the Moon's orbit was likely tilted by a violent event in Earth's early history. "Because TOI-2535 b has a close neighbor, it probably migrated by slowly spiraling in instead of getting flung in," Yee said. "We may be seeing a formation channel that doesn't happen very often but is very interesting."

The reasoning is subtle but important. A planet flung inward by a violent encounter would be expected to end up alone, its companions scattered or ejected. A planet that spiraled in slowly, embedded in a disk with other planets, could keep a close neighbor — but then the orbits should be flat, not tilted. TOI-2535 b has both a close neighbor and tilted orbits, which suggests the system found a third way: a slow inward spiral that kept the neighbor, followed by gravitational interactions between the two planets that gradually tilted their orbits away from the flat plane. It is a formation channel that does not happen very often, Yee said, but one that may be exactly what this system is showing us. If more systems like it are found, astronomers will be able to measure how common each migration pathway is — and that, in turn, will say something about how planetary systems in general are put together, including our own.

The comparison with other extreme worlds found by the transit method sharpens the picture. TOI-2535 b is not the only planet whose geometry is a moving target. <a href="/blog/toi-1355-b-transit-window-closes-2033-nasa-13-exoplanets/">TOI-1355 b Transit Window Closes 2033: NASA Adds 13 Exoplanets</a> described a world whose transits will stop being visible from Earth within a few years, for related reasons of orbital geometry. The two cases together suggest that vanishing transits may be less of a rarity than the "first known" label implies — they may simply have been hard to catch before telescopes started watching the same stars for years on end. And on the imaging side, <a href="/blog/webb-flip-51-eridani-b-november-2026/">Webb FLIP 51 Eridani b November 2026: The Hidden Planet in Two-Year-Old Data</a> showed how a giant planet could hide in data astronomers already had, waiting for a better technique to reveal it. TOI-2535 b is the transit-method mirror of that story: a planet hiding in plain sight, not behind glare but behind geometry.

## Why This One Matters

There are hundreds of known hot Jupiters, and a single new one would ordinarily be routine. TOI-2535 b is not routine, for three reasons that reach beyond the planet itself.

The first is the timescale. Planetary orbits are supposed to change slowly, over millions of years. Watching an orbit tilt enough to erase a transit within a seven-year observing campaign is like watching a mountain range rise in a timelapse — a process everyone knew happened, suddenly visible at human speed. It gives astronomers a laboratory for gravitational interaction that does not require waiting for eons: the system is changing now, measurably, and the models of how planets tug on each other can be tested against data taken a few years apart rather than inferred from a single frozen snapshot.

The second is the census. Every catalog of exoplanets is shaped by the methods that built it, and the transit method's blind spot is geometry: it misses every planet whose orbit does not cross its star as seen from Earth. TOI-2535 b shows that this blind spot is not fixed. Planets drift in and out of it. A world counted in the census in 2019 is absent from it in 2026, and will return to it in the 2050s or 2060s. If enough systems behave this way, the statistics astronomers use to estimate how common planets are — and how common hot Jupiters in particular are — will need to account for a population that flickers in and out of view. That matters for questions as large as how often giant planets form and migrate, and therefore for how typical or unusual our own solar system is.

The third is the formation story. The hot Jupiter problem has been open since 1995, when 51 Pegasi b, the first exoplanet found around a Sun-like star, turned out to be a gas giant on a four-day orbit. Thirty years later, the two migration scenarios Yee and Petigura describe — the violent kick and the gentle spiral — are still both in play, and individual systems keep voting for one or the other. TOI-2535 b votes for a slow spiral with a companion kept close, plus a later tilting that the gentle picture alone does not predict. It is a single vote, but it is a clear one, and it came from a system that also happens to be the most extreme example of interacting planets the team has seen. Extreme cases are often the ones that break a theory or complete it.

There is also a quieter lesson in the discovery, about the value of looking again. TOI-2535 b was not found by a new telescope or a new technique. It was found by a telescope that had already been watching, returning to the same stars, accumulating years of data until a pattern emerged that no single observation could show. The same archive that revealed the planet's disappearance also holds the records of thousands of other systems, and the same slow changes may be written into many of them. The researchers' next step is, in a sense, to go back through the archive and look for other planets whose transits are drifting, shortening, and fading — other disappearing worlds that have not yet been noticed.

## What Happens Next

The most immediate question is the hidden neighbor. It has not been seen transiting, and its properties — its size, its mass, its exact orbit — are inferred from the way it tugs on TOI-2535 b, not measured directly. But the wobble that hid one planet may reveal the other. The astronomers note that the hidden neighbor may come into view sooner than TOI-2535 b itself, if its own tilted orbit swings into alignment with Earth before its companion's does. A transit of the neighbor, if it happens, would give astronomers a direct measurement of its size and period, and radial-velocity follow-up could weigh it. Until then, the neighbor remains a gravitational ghost, known only by its effects.

The second question is how many more disappearing planets are out there. The TOI-2535 system was identified because TESS happened to observe it repeatedly over seven years, and because the team looked closely at the timing of its transits rather than just their existence. Other systems in the TESS archive may show the same signature — transits drifting by hours, growing shallower year by year — and a systematic search could turn a single oddity into a population. That population, in turn, would tell astronomers how often close planetary companions tilt each other's orbits, and how often the gentle migration pathway ends in a tilted system rather than a flat one.

The third question is the longest-term one: the return. Sometime between 2056 and 2076, TOI-2535 b's orbit will have precessed far enough that the planet once again crosses the face of its star as seen from Earth, and its transits will resume. Nobody observing today will be watching with the same instruments — TESS itself will be long retired — but the prediction is written down, dated, and testable, a message in a bottle for the astronomers of the 2050s. They will be able to check whether the wobble behaved as the models predicted, and whether the neighbor appeared first, exactly as the UCLA team suggested it might.

For now, the planet is gone from view, and that absence is the discovery. A world the size of Jupiter, racing around its star in eight days, vanished not because anything happened to it, but because the geometry of its system shifted by a fraction of a degree — enough to break the alignment that made it visible. It is a reminder that the sky astronomers see is not a fixed map but a moving one, and that sometimes the most interesting thing a telescope can show you is a planet that is no longer there.

Its wobble, and the neighbor that causes it, will keep astronomers watching the patch of sky where it used to cross, waiting for the day the alignment returns.
