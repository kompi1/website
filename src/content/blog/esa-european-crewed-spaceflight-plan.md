For more than half a century, Europe has been a power in space without ever being able to send its own people there. It builds the rockets — some of the most reliable on the planet. It designs the science missions that rewrite textbooks. But every European who has ever reached orbit has done so as a passenger on somebody else's ship: first the American Space Shuttle, then the Russian Soyuz, and today SpaceX's Crew Dragon.

On October 5, 2026, the head of the European Space Agency said he wanted that arrangement to end. Director General Josef Aschbacher outlined a ten-year, €10 billion ($10.9 billion) plan to give Europe sovereign, independent crewed spaceflight — the ability to launch its own astronauts aboard its own rockets and its own capsules, and to bring them safely home again. The proposal now heads to ESA's member-state ministers, who are expected to review fully costed options at an intermediate ministerial meeting in Rome on December 15, 2026, with formal procurement and development contracts to follow at the 2028 Ministerial Council if the plan wins approval.

It is, by any measure, the most ambitious change of direction in ESA's fifty-year history. And its logic, as Aschbacher presented it, is brutally simple: Europe is going to spend the money anyway. The only question is whether it spends it in America or at home.

## Why now: the clock is ticking on the American ride

To understand why Europe's space chief is pushing this now, start with the uncomfortable fact sitting in plain view: Europe does not have a guaranteed ride to orbit, and its current ride has an expiry date.

Only three nations can currently launch human beings into space under their own flag — the United States, China, and Russia. Political tensions have closed off the Russian and Chinese options for Europe. That leaves the United States, and there ESA astronauts fly as customers of NASA's Commercial Crew Program, riding SpaceX's Falcon 9 and Crew Dragon. It has been a successful arrangement. But Aschbacher pointed out a problem in an interview this month with the Euronews programme *The Big Question*: SpaceX has signalled that it intends to retire the Falcon 9 and Crew Dragon, probably around 2029. What follows — which spacecraft, operated by whom, available to whom, at what price — is a question mark.

"That means in a few years, we will not have at least this capability available anymore," Aschbacher explained, noting that whatever succeeds the Dragon is not yet known. Europe, which has put astronauts in orbit continuously through partners since its first astronaut mission in 1983, suddenly faces a future in which it cannot reliably count on buying a seat from anyone.

Meanwhile, the destination itself is changing. The International Space Station, Europe's orbital laboratory since the turn of the century, is scheduled to be decommissioned around 2030 and deorbited. ESA carries out a significant share of its most important scientific research aboard the station, and the agency expects the ISS to be followed by commercial orbital stations. Without its own transportation, Europe's future in low Earth orbit would consist of two separate purchases: buying seats on American spacecraft to get there, and then leasing capacity on commercial stations once it arrives. Aschbacher's team estimates that bill at roughly €5 billion over the next decade.

"But this money is lost, or, let's put it this way, is being spent outside Europe," he said. His argument is not about ideology but about economics: "I would prefer to invest these funds in Europe, to strengthen our industry and increase Europe's autonomy in this field." The €10 billion price tag, in other words, is only €5 billion more than Europe would spend as a customer — and the money stays on the continent, building a European industrial base instead of renting somebody else's.

At the same time, Aschbacher was careful to say that independence is not isolation. "This does not mean Europe is turning its back on international cooperation," he stressed. The point is to cooperate from a position of capability rather than dependence — the same posture that has allowed Europe to be a respected partner rather than a junior passenger in robotic exploration.

## What the €10 billion actually buys

The plan, as outlined on October 5, is deliberately framed as buying services rather than building a monolithic government spaceship. ESA's 23 member states would fund a framework in which European industry develops crew transport, and the agency would act as the anchor customer — an approach consciously modelled on the commercial partnerships that gave NASA its Crew Dragon and Cargo Dragon fleets. The cost estimate spans a decade and covers the full anatomy of a human spaceflight programme:

**Human-rated launch vehicles.** The heaviest lift in the plan is certifying a rocket to carry people. Ariane 6, Europe's workhorse heavy launcher, is the leading candidate: it completed its first commercial launch in March 2025 and its first four-booster Ariane 64 flight in February 2026, demonstrating the kind of reliability record that human-rating programmes are built on. At the ILA Berlin air show in June 2026, Aschbacher confirmed ESA was investigating whether Ariane 6 could be human-rated, saying the chances of that outcome were "pretty high, or pretty good." The plan also keeps the door open to dedicated commercial launchers, a nod to the new generation of European rockets coming online.

**A pressurized crew capsule.** ESA would not be starting from zero. Its LEO Cargo Return Service — the initiative, modelled on NASA's COTS programme, through which the agency is buying European cargo transport to and from orbit — has already produced flying hardware in the form of The Exploration Company's Nyx capsule. On September 10, 2026, ESA awarded the company a €760 million contract, dubbed ALADDIN, for up to three Nyx cargo flights to low Earth orbit: a €310 million demonstration mission targeting an autonomous docking with the ISS in November 2028, plus €450 million in options for two additional cargo runs to the ISS or a future commercial station. Nyx is designed to carry up to 4,000 kilograms of cargo in a reusable capsule that returns under parachutes — and its founder, Hélène Huby, has confirmed the crew upgrade path, saying the vehicle was designed from the start to evolve toward carrying people. A second capsule path exists too: Spain's PLD Space is developing its own LINCE crewed capsule, initially targeting unmanned test flights in 2028.

**Escape, landing, and recovery.** A crew system is far more than a capsule on a rocket. The plan must engineer launch abort systems that can tear the crew away from a failing launcher in milliseconds, landing systems that bring people down safely, and ocean recovery infrastructure — ships, crews, and procedures to fish a capsule out of the Atlantic or Mediterranean and get its astronauts home.

**Ground operations and astronaut training.** The proposal includes modernizing ground segment operations and astronaut training facilities across Europe, building the institutional muscle that decades of flying other nations' vehicles never required.

This is not, in other words, a capsule contract. It is the acquisition of everything a sovereign human spaceflight programme needs, from the pad to the ocean.

## Europe has been here before — and walked away

The October proposal did not materialize from nothing. Europe's dream of crewed independence is older than many of the engineers who would build it, and its history is a cautionary tale that Aschbacher's plan must answer.

In the 1980s, ESA bet big on Hermes, a small spaceplane that would ride the Ariane 5 launcher and give Europe independent crewed access to space. The programme grew in ambition and cost until it collapsed in 1992 under its own weight. Ariane 5 was itself human-rated at first — the rocket was designed from the beginning to carry Hermes — but when the spaceplane died, the human-rating requirement died with it, and Europe settled into the role of reliable partner: building the Columbus laboratory and the Automated Transfer Vehicle for the ISS, flying its astronauts on the Shuttle and the Soyuz, and providing the service module that powers NASA's Orion capsule to this day.

That partnership model worked for decades. But the world around it changed. The Shuttle retired in 2011. Russia's invasion of Ukraine ended European reliance on Soyuz, which had carried ESA astronauts for a decade. Crew Dragon filled the gap, but as a commercial service from a single American company — one whose founder is now openly talking about retiring the vehicle. Each transition left Europe more exposed, and more aware of how little control it had over its own astronauts' access to space.

What is different this time is the industrial starting point. The Hermes era had no European commercial space industry to lean on; today's Europe has The Exploration Company, PLD Space, ArianeGroup, Thales Alenia Space, and a thriving ecosystem of launch startups nurtured by ESA's Launcher Challenge, under which PLD Space alone secured a €158.9 million contract in August 2026 for its MIURA 5 rocket. The commercial-cargo precedent is also real: the LEO Cargo Return Service deliberately abandoned ESA's traditional geo-return procurement rules — the policy that spreads contracts across member states in proportion to their contributions — in favor of fixed-price, service-based contracts, with the agency as anchor customer. "We buy a service," Aschbacher said at the time. "We give industry all the freedom to find the best solution technically, but also the best partners, with whomever they want to work with." The crewed proposal extends that philosophy to its logical conclusion.

## The math that ministers will weigh in Rome

When the ministers of ESA's 23 member states gather in Rome on December 15, 2026, they will be weighing more than a space programme. They will be weighing an industrial policy.

The headline figure is €10 billion over ten years — a large sum, but one that needs context. ESA's member states committed a record of more than €22 billion to the agency's programmes at the 2025 ministerial council, a 32 percent increase over 2022, and separately gave the agency a historic mandate to use space technology for non-aggressive security and defence. The crewed programme would be funded on top of that baseline, spread across a decade and across 23 national budgets.

Aschbacher's pitch reframes the number as a choice between two expenditures. Doing nothing, he told interviewers, still costs Europe about €5 billion over the same decade — paid to American providers for seats and station access, money that leaves the continent and builds nothing. Spending €10 billion instead means roughly €5 billion of additional outlay in exchange for a sovereign capability, thousands of high-skilled jobs across the European space sector, and a domestic industrial base that can sell its services on the world market. The space economy, currently valued at around €600 billion, is growing at roughly 10 percent a year, Aschbacher noted — and without its own crew transport, Europe is locked out of its fastest-growing segment.

The counterarguments are equally real, and ministers will have heard them before. Ten years and €10 billion are estimates for a programme Europe has never executed; human spaceflight has a global history of cost overruns, and Europe's only attempt at a crewed vehicle was cancelled. Human-rating a launch vehicle is among the most demanding engineering tasks in aerospace — Boeing's Starliner, built by one of the world's most experienced aerospace companies with NASA money, took years longer than planned and still flies uncrewed. Critics will ask whether €10 billion is enough, whether a decade is realistic, and whether Europe's astronauts might be better served by continuing to buy rides while investing the difference in the robotic science and Earth observation programmes where Europe already leads the world.

There is also the question of the destination. A crew transport system needs somewhere to go. The ISS will be gone by the early 2030s, and while commercial stations are expected to replace it, none is yet operational. Europe would be building a taxi service in the hope — the reasonable hope, but still a hope — that stations will be waiting when it is ready. Aschbacher's team has acknowledged this interdependency: the crewed plan is explicitly framed as supporting commercial post-ISS orbital destinations, meaning ESA would likely be an anchor customer for both the ride and the hotel.

## The contenders: who would build Europe's crew ship

Although ESA has not formally announced partnerships for the crewed capability, the industry has not waited for permission. Two companies have already planted their flags, and their parallel tracks read like a preview of the competition to come.

The Exploration Company, headquartered in Germany and France, is the furthest along. Its Nyx capsule began as a cargo vehicle under ESA's cargo return initiative, with the September 2026 ALADDIN contract — €760 million for up to three flights, starting with an ISS docking demonstration in November 2028 — providing the flight heritage that any crew rating would build on. Nyx's design is reusable, with a pressurized volume intended to scale from its 4,000-kilogram cargo configuration to a crewed variant. The company's leadership has been explicit that human transport is the long-term goal, and ESA's commercial-cargo model, with its relaxed geo-return rules and anchor-customer structure, is already the template the crewed programme would follow. If the ministers say yes in December, TEC starts the race as the house favourite.

PLD Space of Spain offers the more vertically integrated vision. The company is developing the LINCE capsule in parallel with its own launcher family: MIURA 5, the small reusable rocket due to fly in late 2026 and backed by a €180 million Series C round led by Mitsubishi Electric plus the €158.9 million ESA Launcher Challenge award, and the larger Miura Next — a 60-meter, partially reusable medium-lift vehicle with a 13.6-tonne LEO capacity, planned for a first flight around 2030, explicitly designed to work alongside the LINCE capsule for crewed missions. PLD Space's stated target is unmanned LINCE tests beginning in 2028, putting it on a timeline that dovetails neatly with Aschbacher's ten-year horizon. A European capsule on a European rocket, from a single company, is the purest expression of the sovereignty the plan seeks.

Behind them sit the established primes. Thales Alenia Space, the Franco-Italian contractor that won one of the two original cargo return Phase 1 contracts in May 2024, has spoken of an end-to-end commercial passenger and cargo transport service of its own. ArianeGroup, builder of Ariane 6, holds the keys to the likeliest crew-rated launcher. The pattern mirrors NASA's Commercial Crew Program, which pitted SpaceX against Boeing and produced Crew Dragon — the vehicle Europe currently depends on. ESA's bet is that competition among European firms can produce the same result: a safe, affordable crew vehicle, built under fixed-price service contracts rather than cost-plus development.

## What success — and failure — would look like

If the plan survives Rome and the 2028 ministerial, the timeline it implies is roughly this: development contracts in the late 2020s, uncrewed demonstration flights around the turn of the decade — the Nyx cargo demo in November 2028 and LINCE uncrewed tests from 2028 would serve as technical precursors — crew rating and test flights in the early-to-mid 2030s, and operational European crew transport to low Earth orbit by the mid-2030s, just as commercial stations mature and the ISS fades into memory. European astronauts would fly European rockets to European-served stations, and the €5 billion that would have flowed to American providers would instead circulate through factories in Germany, France, Italy, Spain, and beyond.

That is the vision. The failure modes are the familiar ones. The December ministerial could balk at the price tag, or approve a watered-down study phase that drifts. Costs could inflate past the €10 billion estimate once the hard realities of human-rating — redundant systems, abort testing, the unforgiving certification of every valve and weld — set in. The commercial stations Europe plans to visit might arrive late or not at all, leaving a crew vehicle with nowhere to fly. Or a change in American plans — a successor to Crew Dragon appearing sooner and cheaper than expected — could tempt ministers to keep renting rather than building.

Aschbacher himself has framed the decision as one of those rare moments when delay is also a choice. Every year Europe waits, he has argued, is another year of money flowing out of the continent, another year in which the engineers who could build the capability take their skills elsewhere, and another year closer to a future in which there may be no ride to buy at all. The €10 billion figure is his answer to the perennial European objection that sovereignty is unaffordable: it is, he insists, barely more expensive than dependence.

## The kicker: a continent decides what it wants to be

Strip away the budgets and the timelines, and the December decision in Rome is about something older than spaceflight. For fifty years, Europe has been the indispensable partner — the builder of the laboratories, the supplier of the service modules, the provider of the launchers — that could not launch its own astronauts. It was a comfortable position until the world made it uncomfortable: geopolitics severed the Russian option, and commercial reality is now threatening the American one.

Aschbacher's proposal asks Europe's governments a direct question: is that a position a €600-billion space economy's home continent should still accept? The €10 billion answer he proposes is not really about ten years or ten billion euros. It is about whether Europe intends to remain a passenger in the era of commercial space stations, or to become what it has never been — a nation-sized spacefaring power that can send its own people to orbit and bring them home, on its own terms, under its own flag.

The ministers will have their answer in December. The rockets, the capsules, and the astronauts of the 2030s are waiting on it.
