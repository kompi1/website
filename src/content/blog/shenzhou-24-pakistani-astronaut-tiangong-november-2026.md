China is about to open the hatch of its space station to the rest of the world. On November 25, 2026, a Long March 2F rocket is targeted to lift off from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in northwestern China carrying the Shenzhou-24 spacecraft — and aboard it, a Pakistani astronaut who will become the first foreign national ever to visit China's Tiangong space station.

The China Manned Space Agency announced the selection on Saturday, November 7, confirming that a Pakistani citizen will fly as a payload specialist, conduct scientific experiments aboard Tiangong, and return to Earth after approximately one week. Two days later, China's Foreign Ministry gave the mission its diplomatic seal, with spokesperson Guo Jiakun telling reporters at Monday's regular Beijing press briefing that the flight would mark "another major milestone in China-Pakistan cooperation."

It is, in engineering and diplomatic terms, a genuine threshold. In more than two decades of Chinese human spaceflight, no foreign national has ever been invited aboard a Chinese orbital outpost. Shenzhou-24 changes that — and it does so with a mission architecture so unusual that the visitor will not even come home on the spacecraft that carried them up.

## The announcement that made it official

The basic facts were established by the China Manned Space Agency, known as CMSA, on November 7. A Pakistani citizen, chosen from a pool of two trained candidates, will travel to Tiangong as a payload specialist, run scientific experiments, and return after about a week. The announcement also confirmed the spacecraft assignment: the visitor flies up on Shenzhou-24 and comes home on Shenzhou-23, the spacecraft that has been docked at Tiangong since May.

What the CMSA announcement did not do was name the astronaut or give a launch date. Neither government has yet said which of the two finalists will fly, and CMSA has not independently confirmed a specific departure day. Pakistan's planning minister, Ahsan Iqbal, publicly cited November 25 in October, and launch-tracking sites list that date as the earliest possible one. Until China confirms it, November 25 should be treated as a target rather than a promise — but the Foreign Ministry's confirmation on Monday signalled that nothing stands in the way of that target.

The routing of the announcement matters. When Beijing puts a space mission through a Foreign Ministry press briefing rather than leaving it to CMSA's technical channels, it is signalling that the flight carries foreign-policy significance — that it is not only an operational event but a statement about how China intends to conduct international relations in orbit. The timing reinforces the signal: 2026 is the 75th anniversary of Pakistan-China diplomatic relations, formally established in 1951.

CMSA also released the Shenzhou-24 mission emblem on November 7, the customary patch that crews wear and collectors prize. Chinese crews are normally named just before launch, and both governments have indicated that the formal naming announcement will come before the launch window opens.

## Who will fly

The two finalists have been known since April. Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, a decorated pilot from the Pakistan Air Force, and Khurram Daud, a pilot from the Pakistan Navy, began astronaut training in Beijing in April at the China Astronaut Research and Training Center, where China's own taikonauts prepare for orbital missions. One will fly; the other will serve as backup.

Both officers were selected through a multi-stage national process that began after a formal cooperation agreement was signed in Islamabad on February 28, 2025. Following preliminary screening and evaluation rounds conducted in Pakistan, the two finalists were enrolled in Beijing and cleared for the full training curriculum alongside China's taikonauts.

Their preparation was intensive and explicitly binational. Training included centrifuge runs, low-pressure chamber assessments, weightlessness adaptation exercises and orbital systems coursework — and one element that reflects the practical reality of working aboard Tiangong: mandatory Mandarin language instruction. Aboard the station, all operational communication takes place in Chinese. Pakistan's ambassador to China, Khalil Hashmi, reported strong training progress in September and noted that the two candidates were in excellent condition.

The payload specialist role itself is distinct from the taikonauts sharing the spacecraft. Whichever candidate flies will not be responsible for spacecraft systems, navigation or extravehicular operations. Their defined mission is to operate the Pakistani experiment hardware in the microgravity environment of Tiangong's science modules and conduct the prescribed research protocols. CMSA spokesperson Lin Xiqiang confirmed that the visitor will perform standard crew duties alongside the experiments — a framing China appears to have chosen deliberately to emphasise full crew status rather than passenger status.

## A week in orbit, and an unusual ride home

The Shenzhou-24 mission involves a crew-rotation mechanic worth understanding, because it is unusual by historical standards and reflects the specific way China is operating Tiangong in 2026.

Under Tiangong's standard scheduling, a Shenzhou spacecraft launches a crew of three taikonauts who remain aboard the station for approximately six months. The spacecraft stays docked at the station throughout that period, serving as the crew's emergency return vehicle. When the relief crew arrives, there is a brief handover overlap before the outgoing crew departs.

The Pakistani mission complicates — and illustrates — that model. Shenzhou-23 launched in May 2026 carrying China's first year-long crew rotation attempt: three taikonauts scheduled to remain aboard Tiangong for approximately 12 months. That crew is still aboard the station now. Shenzhou-24 will launch on November 25 carrying two taikonauts plus the Pakistani payload specialist, bringing the station's total occupancy to six during the overlap period — Tiangong's design maximum during crew handovers.

The Pakistani astronaut will not return aboard the spacecraft that carried them up. After completing approximately one week of experiments, they will return to Earth aboard Shenzhou-23 — the vehicle that has been docked at Tiangong since May — alongside some of the year-long crew completing their rotation. Shenzhou-24 will remain docked as the continuing crew's new emergency return vehicle. This cross-vehicle choreography is routine for Tiangong operations, but it is worth explaining to readers who might assume a simple up-and-back architecture: the Pakistani astronaut essentially hitches a ride home on a craft that arrived six months earlier.

The Shenzhou-24 crew itself will stay longer. Mission planning lists about 180 days for that crew, a standard half-year tour, while the visitor's stay is measured in days. Two of the three Shenzhou-23 crew members are expected to make the return trip with the visitor; the third is expected to stay on the station for a full year so scientists can study the effects of long stays in space.

## The science on board

Pakistan's space agency, SUPARCO, selected four experiments from 300 proposals submitted by Pakistani universities — a process that itself represents a significant expansion of Pakistan's engagement with space science research.

The four experiments span food security, climate resilience, life and biological sciences, and materials science. While SUPARCO has not released full technical specifications for each experiment, the domains reflect a research agenda oriented toward practical applications: understanding how biological processes and material behaviours change under microgravity and elevated radiation conditions, with downstream relevance to the agricultural and industrial challenges that figure among Pakistan's development priorities.

The choice of subjects is deliberate. A week in orbit is short, but it is long enough to run experiments that cannot be run on the ground — seeds germinating without gravity's pull, crystals growing without convection, cells responding to radiation they never meet on Earth's surface. For a country building its space science base from a standing start, four experiments flown on a first mission is a serious opening portfolio. It is also a reminder that human spaceflight is as much about what hands can do in orbit as what rockets can do getting there — something <a href="/blog/sophie-adenot-first-french-woman-spacewalk/">Six Hours Outside: Sophie Adenot Becomes the First French Woman to Walk in Space</a> showed from the European side earlier this year.

Separately from the crewed mission, Pakistan has another space milestone on the near-term horizon: Jinnah-1, the country's inaugural lunar rover. The rover weighs 35 kilograms and carries a terrain-testing instrument for soil analysis, radiation sensors and plasma sensors designed to characterise the lunar south pole environment, analysing plasma behaviour, radiation levels and surface geology. It is scheduled for deployment on China's Chang'e-8 mission, tentatively in 2029, and will be operated by Pakistani specialists at a ground station on Earth after deployment — extending Pakistan's space footprint from low Earth orbit to the Moon within roughly three years if the timeline holds.

## The road to Shenzhou-24

The mission did not appear from nowhere. It is the visible tip of a partnership that has been assembling, quietly, across multiple programs for several years:

- **May 2024** — Pakistan's ICUBE-Q CubeSat launches aboard China's Chang'e-6 lunar sample-return mission, giving SUPARCO its first experience with deep-space mission operations.
- **February 28, 2025** — China and Pakistan sign a formal cooperation agreement in Islamabad on astronaut selection, training and participation in space station missions.
- **April 2026** — Pakistan's PRSC-EO3 Earth-observation satellite launches on a Chinese rocket, adding to Pakistan's orbital Earth-monitoring capability. The same month, the two astronaut candidates begin training in Beijing.
- **May 2026** — Shenzhou-23 launches carrying China's first year-long crew rotation attempt. The spacecraft will later bring the Pakistani visitor home.
- **September 2026** — Pakistan's ambassador to China reports strong training progress and notes both candidates are in excellent condition.
- **October 2026** — Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal publicly cites November 25 as the launch date.
- **November 7, 2026** — CMSA announces the Pakistani payload specialist selection and releases the Shenzhou-24 mission emblem.
- **November 9, 2026** — China's Foreign Ministry confirms the mission at its regular press briefing, with spokesperson Guo Jiakun calling it "another major milestone in China-Pakistan cooperation."
- **November 25, 2026** — Targeted launch date from Jiuquan on a Long March 2F rocket (pending confirmation by CMSA).
- **Early December 2026** — Expected return of the visitor aboard Shenzhou-23, about a week after launch.

The strategic scaffolding underneath all of it is the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor, known as CPEC, the approximately 62 billion dollar infrastructure investment program linking China's Xinjiang region to Pakistan's Gwadar port and representing the flagship project of the Belt and Road Initiative. Analysts and Pakistani officials have explicitly described the astronaut program as "CPEC in orbit" — the logical expansion of a partnership that has already reshaped Pakistan's energy and transport infrastructure into a domain where prestige and capability are equally on the table. Prime Minister Shahbaz Sharif praised the astronaut selection publicly after it was announced, and President Xi Jinping in May 2026 called for deeper all-around cooperation between the two countries, with bilateral space work specifically cited in joint statements throughout the year.

## Why Tiangong exists — and why this flight matters

To understand what China is doing with the Pakistani mission, it helps to understand why China had a station to offer in the first place.

In 2007, China requested participation in the International Space Station program. The request went nowhere. In 2011, the United States Congress passed the Wolf Amendment — a provision in the annual Commerce, Justice, Science appropriations law, authored by then-Representative Frank Wolf — that blocked NASA from using federal funds to collaborate with China or Chinese-owned companies on space activities without prior FBI notification to Congress. The practical effect was to lock China out of the ISS framework permanently.

Beijing responded by building its own station. Tiangong's core module, Tianhe, launched in April 2021. The laboratory modules Wentian and Mengtian were added in 2022. The station has been continuously crewed since June 2022. It now has a mass of approximately 100,000 kilograms, a pressurised volume of 340 cubic metres, two robotic arms and a design life of 10 to 15 years. It orbits at approximately 390 kilometres altitude, completing roughly 15.5 orbits per day, and can accommodate three astronauts under normal circumstances and up to six during a crew change. The station is one half of a two-way race: <a href="/blog/china-moon-program-2030/">China's Methodical Sprint to the Moon: One Program, One Deadline — 2030</a> traces how the same program that built Tiangong is driving toward a crewed lunar landing by the end of the decade.

What the Wolf Amendment's architects did not anticipate was that forcing China to build Tiangong independently would also give Beijing both the motivation and the narrative tool to offer it as a home for nations the ISS historically underserved. The ISS is a partnership among the United States, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada. Its membership reflects Cold War alliance structures more than the actual distribution of space-aspiring nations in 2026. Chinese state media have consistently described Tiangong as "open to all humanity." Until November 7, 2026, that was a statement of aspiration. With CMSA's selection of a Pakistani payload specialist and the Foreign Ministry's confirmation, it has become a statement of operational fact.

A University of Washington analysis published in April 2026 framed Tiangong as the central node in what it called China's "Space Silk Road" strategy: a deliberate extension of the Belt and Road Initiative into orbit, aimed at building lasting technical dependencies and diplomatic relationships with Global South nations through the same mechanism that made ISS partnerships effective for the West — shared infrastructure, shared risk and shared prestige. Pakistan is the proof of concept. If the November 25 launch proceeds as planned and the week-long mission completes without incident, Beijing will have demonstrated that Tiangong is operationally capable of hosting foreign nationals — and will have handed China's diplomatic corps a template it can offer to every other Belt and Road partner nation that has space ambitions and no ISS invitation.

## What comes next

The immediate sequence is clear, even where details are still pending. The crew will be named just before launch. The Long March 2F will roll out at Jiuquan, and if the November 25 target holds, Shenzhou-24 will dock at Tiangong's Tianhe forward port the same day. For about a week, the station will hold six people — its design maximum — while the Pakistani payload specialist runs four experiments chosen from 300 university proposals. Then the visitor will climb aboard Shenzhou-23 with two members of the year-long crew and come home to Inner Mongolia, while Shenzhou-24 stays docked as the new crew's lifeboat. The contrast with the station Tiangong is meant to outlast could hardly be sharper: <a href="/blog/goodbye-iss-deorbit-commercial-stations/">Goodbye, ISS: How NASA Plans to Crash the Space Station — and Who Replaces It</a> explains how the International Space Station's planned end is opening the field for exactly this kind of new orbital address.

The broader context is a space architecture in flux. The International Space Station is currently funded through 2030, but its long-term future beyond that date remains unresolved. NASA's Artemis program is advancing toward a crewed lunar return with international partners under the Artemis Accords framework — a competing international coalition whose membership and China are explicitly not aligned. China has stated a goal of landing taikonauts on the lunar surface by 2030. The United States has the same general objective under Artemis. Both nations are separately developing plans for nuclear power reactors on the Moon to support potential permanent bases.

In that context, Pakistan's mission to Tiangong carries a meaning that extends beyond the two countries involved. It is China demonstrating to the global community of space-aspirant nations — the ones without ISS invitations and without independent launch capability — that there is a second address in orbit, and that Beijing is accepting visitors.

One dimension any nation considering a Tiangong partnership would need to weigh: Tiangong operations, including all data generated aboard the station, fall under Chinese law. That legal framework applies to CMSA and to the operational environment of Tiangong, regardless of the nationality of the astronauts or the ownership of the experiments conducted aboard it. Pakistan's experiment data will flow to SUPARCO, but the channel through which it flows is a Chinese station operating under Chinese legal authority. That is a fixed condition of the partnership, not a detail bilateral agreements can fully neutralise, and it is one future Tiangong partner nations will need to account for in their own calculations.

For now, though, the story is simpler and more human. Two pilots from Pakistan trained for months in Beijing, learned to work in Mandarin, spun in centrifuges and floated in weightlessness simulators — and one of them, still unnamed, will soon ride a Long March rocket to a station that did not exist five years ago, to spend a week doing science 390 kilometres above the Earth. When the hatch opens on November 25, Tiangong stops being China's station alone.
