For more than a century, Betelgeuse has refused to behave like a well-mannered star. The red supergiant marking the shoulder of Orion brightens and fades on a rhythm all its own, and in late 2019 it dimmed so dramatically that astronomers around the world wondered, half seriously, whether it was about to explode. It was not. But the mystery behind its strange behaviour has now gained its most persuasive explanation yet: Betelgeuse is not alone. A small companion star, recently named Siwarha, is orbiting so close to the giant that it plows directly through the supergiant's extended atmosphere, leaving a wake behind it and squeezing its host out of shape as it goes.

The newest evidence comes from radio images gathered by two of the world's most powerful observatories. A team led by Dr. Lynn D. Matthews, a principal research scientist and assistant director at MIT Haystack Observatory, combined observations from the U.S. National Science Foundation's Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA) in New Mexico and the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) in Chile. Their study, published in The Astronomical Journal in 2026, reports that Betelgeuse's atmosphere has largely recovered from its famous dimming episode — and that the star's shape itself betrays the gravitational fingerprints of its hidden companion.

"It was the combination of the incredible data from the NSF VLA and ALMA that gave us a convincing result here," Matthews said. "The fact that we can use these facilities to actually resolve the disks of nearby stars is amazing."

It is a striking claim about a star that has been watched since antiquity. Betelgeuse is so vast that, placed at the position of the Sun, it would swallow the inner planets. It is also so close, on cosmic scales, that modern telescopes can resolve its disk rather than seeing it as a mere point of light. That combination — enormous, nearby, and restless — has made it the most studied red supergiant in the sky, and also one of the most confounding. The new radio observations do not just add another portrait of the star. They show, layer by layer, how a companion star can stir a giant from the inside out.

## A star bouncing back from its great dimming

The story begins with the episode that made Betelgeuse famous beyond astronomy circles. In late 2019 and early 2020, the star faded to its faintest level in more than a century of record-keeping. The event, now known as the Great Dimming, was eventually traced to a massive ejection of material from the star's surface, which cooled and condensed into dust that partially blocked our view. The star was not dying. It was, in effect, coughing out a cloud in our direction.

Matthews had been watching the run-up to that event closely. In 2019, months before the dimming began, a study she led found that Betelgeuse's atmosphere at radio wavelengths was unusually cool — a sign, the researchers proposed, that a shock wave triggered by convection deep inside the star had disturbed the gas in its outer layers, setting the stage for the dust-forming outburst that followed. The new VLA and ALMA observations show the aftermath of that convulsion. The brightness temperature of the star's atmosphere at those same radio wavelengths has climbed back to more typical levels. The gas now measures around 3,500 kelvin — comparable to Betelgeuse's own surface temperature — near a radius of about 2.7 times the star's own size. In other words, the atmosphere has healed.

That recovery is scientifically valuable in its own right, because it confirms that the Great Dimming was a surface weather event, not the beginning of the end. But the same dataset also delivered something the team had not set out to find so cleanly: a confirmation of a long-suspected "temperature minimum" in Betelgeuse's atmosphere, a cool layer sandwiched between the star's visible surface and the chromosphere above it. The highest-frequency ALMA measurements, which reach closer to the star's surface than ever before, found gas temperatures dropping to roughly 2,200 kelvin — more than 1,000 degrees cooler than the photosphere itself. That chilly layer coincides with the so-called MOLsphere, a shell of molecular gas long inferred from infrared observations but rarely probed so directly at radio wavelengths.

The result matters because red supergiants lose mass at a prodigious rate, and the details of how material climbs out of a star's atmosphere determine how much of it escapes into space to seed future generations of stars and planets. A confirmed temperature minimum gives theorists a firm anchor point for models of that outflow. It also demonstrates the power of the technique itself: radio waves of different frequencies probe different depths in a star's atmosphere, so combining seven frequencies, from 22 GHz to 485 GHz, collected between 2021 and 2026, let the team build a layer-by-layer picture from just above the visible surface out to several times the star's radius.

## The case for Siwarha, a century in the making

The headline finding, however, concerns the companion. For decades, astronomers noticed that Betelgeuse's brightness follows two overlapping cycles rather than one. The shorter cycle matches the star's own pulsation. The longer one — a roughly 2,100-day rhythm, about six years, known as the Long Secondary Period — matched no known internal process. As early as a century ago, some astronomers proposed that an unseen companion might be responsible. The idea lingered, unproven, because any companion would be a faint pinprick next to a star hundreds of times larger and millions of times brighter in visible light.

The case sharpened in 2024, when two studies proposed that a companion star could explain the 2,100-day cycle and predicted that the companion would reach its greatest apparent separation from Betelgeuse in December 2024, its best chance of being seen. In July 2025, astronomers using the 'Alopeke instrument on the Gemini North telescope reported the first probable direct detection of the companion. Then, in January 2026, a team from the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian announced a different kind of evidence: using nearly eight years of Hubble Space Telescope observations, they had detected the companion's wake — a trail of denser gas stirred up as the smaller star plows through Betelgeuse's extended outer atmosphere. The results were published in The Astrophysical Journal and presented at the 247th meeting of the American Astronomical Society.

The decisive image arrived in July 2026. A team led by Miguel Montargès of the Observatoire de Paris used the SPHERE instrument on the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope in Chile to observe Betelgeuse at the predicted moment of greatest separation, and after months of processing they revealed the faintest of companions: a point of light about six magnitudes fainter than Betelgeuse itself, at a detection significance of 6.1 sigma, independently confirmed by two different image-processing methods. The companion, formally named Siwarha in September 2025 — Arabic for "her bracelet," echoing Betelgeuse's traditional name, often translated as "the hand of Orion" — is thought to be a young main-sequence star of roughly two to three times the mass of the Sun. It had stayed hidden for so long precisely because it orbits so close to the giant: at a distance of only about twice Betelgeuse's radius, deep inside the supergiant's extended atmosphere.

## The egg test: a star stretched by its companion

The new VLA and ALMA study adds an independent line of evidence that does not depend on seeing the companion at all. Siwarha itself is likely far too faint to detect directly at radio wavelengths — the team calculated it would be tens to hundreds of times too faint to appear in their observations. But its gravitational and physical presence, they found, leaves a mark on its giant host that radio images can measure: the shape of Betelgeuse's atmosphere.

By measuring the subtle egg-like shape, or ellipticity, of Betelgeuse's radio-emitting atmosphere across multiple epochs, the researchers found that the star becomes noticeably more elongated near the times when the companion is expected to be at its greatest apparent separation from Betelgeuse, and rounder when the companion is expected to be passing in front of or behind the star. The pattern matches the companion's predicted six-year orbit. It is, as the researchers put it, a bit like watching ripples on a pond and inferring that something is moving just beneath the surface: astronomers cannot see the companion directly at radio wavelengths, but they can see its influence on the shape of Betelgeuse's atmosphere as it moves along its orbit.

That correlation is what elevates the new result above a curiosity. A single distorted image could be dismissed as weather in a turbulent atmosphere — and Betelgeuse's atmosphere is nothing if not turbulent, with convection cells so large that a handful of them cover the entire visible surface. But a shape that stretches and relaxes in step with a predicted orbit, across observations spanning five years and seven frequencies, is much harder to explain away. The companion's wake, the direct image, and now the rhythmic distortion of the host star all point the same way.

Radio observations are uniquely suited to this kind of detective work because they see through the dust that veils visible light and because each frequency slices the atmosphere at a different height. A companion buried inside that atmosphere therefore leaves a depth-dependent signature: the distortion should look different in layers near the surface than in layers several stellar radii out, and the VLA-ALMA combination is the first dataset with the frequency coverage and the resolution to test that expectation across the whole extended envelope. Future campaigns that fill in the missing frequencies and follow the companion through a complete orbit will be able to map not just the shape of the distortion but its three-dimensional structure, turning a statistical correlation into a physical model of how a small star stirs a giant.

The finding also reframes the Great Dimming. For years, debate centred on whether the dimming was purely a surface event — a convective upwelling that launched a cloud of gas which condensed into obscuring dust — or whether something else was involved. The new work does not overturn that picture; if anything, the recovery of atmospheric temperatures to normal levels supports it. But it adds a second moving part to every future model of Betelgeuse's behaviour. A companion plowing through the outer atmosphere every six years will stir gas, compress it into ridges, and distort the star's shape, and those effects must now be separated from the star's own pulsations and convection before astronomers can claim to understand any single episode of brightening or fading.

This is where Betelgeuse becomes more than a celebrity star. It is the first red supergiant known to host a close stellar companion, a configuration with important consequences for how massive stars evolve. Most massive stars are born in pairs or groups, but a companion that survives into the red supergiant phase, orbiting inside its partner's atmosphere, is a rare laboratory. Tidal interactions are expected to drag Siwarha inward over time; some estimates suggest it could be engulfed by Betelgeuse within about 10,000 years, long before Betelgeuse itself explodes. Whether the companion affects the timing or the symmetry of that eventual supernova is, in the words of the VLT team, a question that is now truly open.

## Why astronomers are still cautious

For all the converging evidence, the researchers themselves urge care. Montargès has referred to the VLT detection conservatively as a candidate until a confirmatory observation can place the star on the opposite side of Betelgeuse at the predicted position. That opportunity is expected in late 2027, when Siwarha should re-emerge from behind the supergiant and become detectable again. The Matthews team, for its part, notes that additional observations spanning a fuller range of the companion's orbit — particularly at frequencies not yet well sampled — will be needed to confirm and refine the ellipticity signal.

That caution is a feature, not a weakness, of the story. Betelgeuse has embarrassed confident predictions before, most recently when its historic dimming was widely misread as a prelude to explosion. The star is not expected to explode anytime soon on human timescales, despite its fame as a future supernova. What the new observations show is subtler and, in the long run, more interesting: a giant star whose behaviour cannot be understood in isolation, because it is not isolated at all.

The next generation of observatories should make such studies routine. The Next Generation Very Large Array, currently in development, is expected to offer an even sharper view of how red supergiants shed material and evolve in their final stages — and how the pull of a nearby companion shapes that process. The European Southern Observatory's Extremely Large Telescope, under construction at Cerro Armazones in Chile, about 20 kilometres from the Paranal Observatory that houses the VLT, will eventually deliver sharper images of the Betelgeuse system itself, enabling more precise measurements of the companion's mass, orbit, and brightness.

## A star that keeps rewriting its own story

There is a pleasing symmetry in the way this discovery unfolded. The companion was predicted because Betelgeuse's light refused to fit a single-star model; it was found, in stages, by instruments that each saw a different piece of the puzzle — a probable pinpoint of light from Gemini North, a gas wake in Hubble spectra, a direct image from the VLT, and now a rhythmic stretching of the star's atmosphere in radio images from the VLA and ALMA. No single observation closed the case. Together, they form a picture that is difficult to explain any other way.

For skywatchers, nothing changes tonight: Betelgeuse will still blaze from Orion's shoulder through the winter months, as it has for all of recorded history. But the star we look at is no longer quite the star we thought it was. It is a binary system in disguise, a giant with a bracelet — a small, hot companion racing through its outer layers once every six years, shaking the rafters as it goes. The ghost in Betelgeuse's atmosphere finally has a name, an orbit, and a wake. The next time the giant dims or brightens out of step, astronomers will know to ask not only what the star itself is doing, but what its hidden companion just did to it.

That is the deeper lesson of the new radio images. The most familiar stars in the sky can still hide a second star in plain sight, and the tools to find them are not always bigger telescopes pointed at brighter light. Sometimes they are arrays of radio dishes, listening at frequencies the eye cannot see, measuring a shape that changes by a whisper — and finding, in that whisper, the unmistakable signature of a companion that has been there all along.
