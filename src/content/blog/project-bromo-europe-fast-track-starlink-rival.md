BRUSSELS — The European Union is preparing to give unusually swift antitrust approval to a satellite venture it openly describes as the continent's answer to SpaceX, after a year of quiet talks with its corporate partners and a geopolitical wake-up call that unfolded in the skies over Ukraine.

Bloomberg News reported on October 15 that EU officials are readying a fast-track review of Project Bromo, the planned combination of the space businesses of Airbus, Thales, and Leonardo, and expect the companies to formally file for approval within the coming weeks. With informal negotiations between the companies and competition authorities already running for more than a year, the deal could clear an initial review phase in little more than a month — a pace that would signal how seriously Brussels takes the business of catching up in orbit.

The timing is no coincidence. In the same week that the Bromo news broke, Ukraine's military found itself reminded of exactly how much of Europe's security still depends on an American billionaire's satellites — and European defense stocks surged as investors bet that the era of that dependence may be ending.

## The Spark: When Starshield Slowed Down

To understand why Brussels suddenly seems in a hurry, rewind to the night of October 13–14. Overnight, Starshield terminals — the military-grade version of SpaceX's Starlink that the U.S. Defense Department buys for Ukraine — stopped working on fast-moving platforms across the front. Ukrainian units reported that connections dropped when terminals moved faster than about 160 kilometers per hour, the standard cruising speed of the interceptor drones Ukraine uses to hunt Russia's attack drones. Some units reported links cutting out for 10 to 20 seconds at speeds as low as 110 to 120 km/h; others lost contact at even lower velocities. Missions were suspended. Drones were lost.

Service began returning on the evening of October 14 and was at least partly restored by the following day, but a persistent speed cap around 160 km/h remained in place for the fastest platforms, according to reporting compiled from multiple outlets. For Ukraine's drone forces, that was devastatingly specific: Russia began fitting jet engines to its Shahed attack drones in the early summer of 2026, and Ukrainian officials said the interception rate against them had fallen from more than 90 percent to about 60 percent. Serhii Beskrestnov, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy's adviser on defense technology, described Starshield-guided interceptor drones as Ukraine's most effective defense against the jet-powered Shaheds — and said the sudden cap knocked out the one capability Starshield existed to provide.

The question of who ordered the slowdown immediately became a diplomatic incident. Reuters reported, citing two U.S. officials and one Western official, that the order came from inside the White House. A senior U.S. official told Reuters that "no such request was ever made by the president." Elon Musk, writing on X, said that Starshield systems "are owned and controlled by the US government" and that any limits on speed or usage were decided "by the US military and ultimately POTUS." The White House and the Pentagon had not publicly clarified the chain of command as of October 16.

What nobody disputed was the lesson: a European country's most critical communications capability could be throttled, without warning, by decisions made in Washington. Markets drew the obvious conclusion. Reuters recorded Eutelsat shares up 20 percent at 12:02 GMT on October 16, taking its weekly gain to 31 percent, with SES up 7.1 percent — investors repricing European satellite providers as the indispensable backup plan. Even the SPCX ticker, tracking SpaceX's post-IPO shares, slipped for a third straight session as traders weighed the prospect of a real European challenger.

## What Project Bromo Actually Is

Strip away the geopolitics, and Project Bromo is an industrial consolidation a decade in the making. The three companies signed a preliminary agreement on October 23, 2025, to merge their satellite businesses — building spacecraft, not launching them — into a single European champion.

The proposed ownership splits the venture down the middle of Europe's aerospace map: Airbus would hold 35 percent, with Leonardo and Thales holding 32.5 percent each. The combined company would employ roughly 25,000 people and would not include space launchers — a deliberate carve-out that keeps Europe's still-fragmented rocket business out of the deal. Based on the companies' combined figures, the merged businesses generated about €6.5 billion in revenue in 2024.

That scale matters because scale is precisely what European space companies have lacked. For years, the continent's satellite manufacturing was divided between national champions: Airbus Defence and Space in France and Germany, Thales Alenia Space split between France and Italy, and Leonardo's Italian operations. Each was large by terrestrial standards and small by SpaceX standards. SpaceX builds thousands of Starlink satellites a year in its own factories, launches them on its own reusable rockets, and operates them as its own network — a vertically integrated machine that no European company can match alone. The industrial logic of Bromo is to create one company that, at least in satellite manufacturing and services, can bid against SpaceX as an equal.

The codename itself hints at the ambition's scale. "Bromo" is shared with an active Indonesian volcano — a restless giant that dominates its landscape. Whether or not the naming was deliberate theater, it has stuck, and this week it entered the news cycle as Europe's most politically freighted space project.

## A Year of Quiet Negotiations

What makes the Bloomberg report significant is not just that Brussels favors the deal — the European Commission's president, Ursula von der Leyen, has already publicly praised the venture as a potential European champion deserving top-level support — but that the competition authorities appear ready to move fast.

According to the reporting, EU officials have spent the past year working with the companies to flag and iron out competition concerns before a formal filing is ever submitted. That pre-notification dance is how large mergers usually avoid a lengthy investigation: fix the problems informally, then file a clean application. If outstanding issues can be resolved through concessions — including possible asset divestitures — the venture could be cleared in a Phase 1 review lasting slightly more than a month. If problems remain, Brussels could open an in-depth probe lasting several months, the standard second phase of European merger review.

That calculus has already produced one of the most interesting moments of the saga. Teresa Ribera, the European Commission executive vice president in charge of competition policy, told Bloomberg TV in late September that the venture would receive no special treatment, even with von der Leyen publicly cheering it on. "That doesn't mean we are not going to apply and to enforce our own regulation," Ribera said, drawing a deliberate line between the Commission's industrial-policy ambitions and its duty as a competition enforcer.

The tension is real, and it is exactly what makes Project Bromo a test case. Brussels recently revised its merger guidelines to foster larger European players that can compete with American and Chinese giants — a doctrinal shift that Project Bromo would be the first to embody. Yet the deal's critics are not only abstract free-market purists. Labor organizations have warned about potential job losses in a consolidation of 25,000 employees, and equipment suppliers worry that merging three of the world's largest satellite buyers into one will squeeze them on price and reduce competition for contracts. Those are the constituencies whose complaints could force real concessions — factory guarantees, asset sales, behavioral commitments — before a Phase 1 clearance becomes possible.

## IRIS²: The Sovereignty Project Already in Orbit's Shadow

Project Bromo does not exist in a vacuum. It is the industrial companion to IRIS² — Infrastructure for Resilience, Interconnectivity and Security by Satellite — the European Union's flagship sovereign broadband constellation, intended to become Europe's third great space program after Galileo for navigation and Copernicus for Earth observation.

IRIS² has moved from PowerPoint to procurement this year with remarkable speed. The Commission awarded the concession to the SpaceRISE consortium — led by Eutelsat alongside SES and Hispasat — on October 31, 2024, and signed the concession agreement on December 16, 2024. After seven months of negotiation, the implementation agreement was signed on August 7, 2026, formally launching the program's build phase. The design has since grown: an added 66-satellite defense and security layer brought the total constellation to 348 satellites — 330 in low Earth orbit and 18 in medium Earth orbit.

The price tag has grown too, to an estimated €15.6 billion, comprising roughly €11.6 billion in public funding from the EU and the European Space Agency and up to €4 billion from the SpaceRISE consortium. On September 10, 2026, Airbus Defence and Space signed the initial contract with Eutelsat to begin development of the first layer of LEO satellites: at least 66 military Ka-band spacecraft — the sovereign, government-grade layer of the network — to be delivered in 2029, with Airbus building the platforms at its serial-production facility in Toulouse and Thales Alenia Space supplying the payloads under a contract worth about €500 million. Thales Alenia Space is responsible for payloads on all 330 LEO satellites, while OHB of Germany is building the 18 MEO spacecraft for SES in a deal worth about €1 billion, and Belgium's Aerospacelab won the order for 264 dual Ku/Ka-band LEO platforms. First launches are targeted for 2029, with progressive services starting the same year.

According to the European Commission, the added defense layer is expected to increase secure governmental communications capacity by about 60 percent within the EU and by more than half globally — capacity intended for crisis response, military communications, border surveillance, and embassies. This is not a consumer broadband play to take on Starlink household by household; it is a sovereignty play, designed to guarantee that European governments can communicate securely when it matters most — exactly the scenario Ukraine illustrated this week.

## The French Contract That Lit the Fuse

The market's reaction on October 16 was not purely speculative. France's defense establishment had already put hard money behind the thesis. On June 15, 2026, Eutelsat announced its CENTAURE agreement with France's defense procurement agency, the DGA: an initial firm commitment of €138 million over four years, within a contract ceiling of €350 million over as many as eight years. It is the first call-off contract under the NEXUS framework France signed with its industry in June 2025 — a call-off being a defined work order placed under an existing umbrella agreement, converting a framework into concrete business.

That contract, covering OneWeb low-orbit capacity for French military use, is the model for how European satellite connectivity demand becomes real revenue. France bought OneWeb capacity for its armed forces; SES sells government services across the continent; IRIS² will add a fully sovereign layer. The Starshield episode has now supplied the political argument for accelerating all of it: when a commercial service controlled by another power can be dialed down overnight, sovereign capacity stops being a luxury and becomes an insurance policy.

EU Defence and Space Commissioner Andrius Kubilius made the political version of that argument at the Paris International Space Summit on September 9–10, where IRIS²'s implementation phase was celebrated. "We need European launching, by Europeans, and from Europe," he said — and then posed the question that now frames the whole debate: "Are we able to be in space a power which represents 450 mln people & more than €20 trillion GDP? That's what sovereignty is."

## Why Europe Fell Behind — and Why Catching Up Is Hard

To appreciate the scale of the challenge Bromo and IRIS² face, consider where Europe stood just a few years ago. After the retirement of Ariane 5, the loss of Russian Soyuz launches following the invasion of Ukraine, and the grounding of Vega C, Europe temporarily lost independent access to space altogether. The indignity was concrete: ESA was forced to launch its Euclid cosmology mission and the EarthCARE climate satellite on Falcon 9 — paying its chief competitor to carry its flagship science missions.

Thirty Ariane 6 launches are now booked, most of them for Amazon's Leo broadband constellation (formerly Project Kuiper), and ESA is studying whether 12 flights in 2029 — rising to 15 or even 20 a year by 2030 — is achievable. But studies are not commitments, and the structural problem runs deeper: according to Germany's SWP think tank, Europe's satellite market represents roughly 1 percent of the global launch market. Without Starlink-like internal demand — SpaceX flew well over a hundred Falcon 9 missions in 2023 alone, while Ariane 6 managed a single inaugural flight — the economics of building and reusing rockets do not close. SpaceX's dominance is not just technological; it is a demand-side advantage that compounds with every launch.

That is the gap Project Bromo is meant to close on the manufacturing side and IRIS² on the services side: guaranteed European demand, built by European companies, creating the production volumes that make a competitive space industry possible. The European Commission's revised merger doctrine — friendlier to consolidation in strategic industries — is the regulatory expression of the same strategy.

## What Happens Next

The immediate calendar is now set. The Bromo partners are expected to formally notify the Commission in the coming weeks, triggering the formal Phase 1 review. The companies' year of informal preparation means the filing should arrive largely pre-cleared — but "largely" is doing real work in that sentence. Labor unions and suppliers have made their objections public, and Ribera's insistence that political support buys no regulatory favors suggests any clearance will come with strings: divestitures, capacity commitments, or employment guarantees that the Commission can point to as evidence of genuine scrutiny.

If the deal clears Phase 1 quickly, it becomes a landmark — the first major test of Europe's new merger doctrine, and a signal to industry that Brussels will trade some domestic competition for global competitiveness in sectors it deems strategic. If it slips into an in-depth investigation, the delay itself becomes the story: a continent that says it wants strategic autonomy but cannot approve the merger its own Commission president champions. Either outcome will shape how European aerospace executives, investors, and rival manufacturers in the United States and China plan their next moves in a market that is consolidating faster than regulators can write the rules.

Meanwhile, the clocks that actually matter are already ticking. IRIS²'s first satellites are due in 2029. Ariane 6's flight rate is ramping. Eutelsat is hiring against the CENTAURE contract. And in Ukraine, drone operators have learned that the link between their interceptors and the sky can vanish at 160 kilometers per hour, without warning, on someone else's schedule.

That image — a terminal going dark at speed, a mission failing not for lack of courage but for lack of a connection — is the real reason Project Bromo is being fast-tracked. Sovereignty in space was once an abstract industrial ambition debated in Brussels conference rooms. This week, it became a field report. Europe is building its answer now, and it has named it after a volcano: something powerful, overdue, and impossible to ignore once it starts moving.
