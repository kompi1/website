SpaceX has not said a word about it. No date, no launch window, no named vehicles, no launch site. But at Starbase, Texas, the hardware for Starship's seventeenth integrated flight test has already been through the freezer — and, by every unofficial account, it came out the other side intact.

Ship 44, the Starship upper stage unofficially assigned to Flight 17, and Super Heavy Booster 24, its first-stage partner, both spent the week of 19 November at Massey's, SpaceX's proving ground west of the Starbase production site, undergoing cryogenic proof testing. By the early hours of 24 November, both vehicles had rolled back down Highway 4, their tanks frosted, their first major test campaign apparently behind them. It was, by the reckoning of the Starbase observers who tracked every move, the fastest turnaround from one flight to the next test campaign in the programme's history — less than a week after Flight 15 ended with the first-ever catch of a spacecraft returning from orbit.

None of this is official. SpaceX has confirmed none of it, and the pairing of Ship 44 and Booster 24 with Flight 17 rests on a photograph and a caption posted by an independent observer. But the pattern is unmistakable: the largest rocket ever flown is now being prepared at a pace that treats each flight as a rehearsal for the next, and the next two flights are already queuing up behind the one that has just flown.

## The freezer test, passed — mostly

Cryogenic proof testing is the least glamorous and most unforgiving step in preparing a new Starship vehicle for flight. Crews at Massey's fill the vehicle's tanks with super-cold liquid nitrogen, a stand-in for the liquid oxygen and methane the rocket burns in flight, and watch what the structure does under the thermal shock. Tanks contract. Welds are stressed. Seals are tested at temperatures far below anything the vehicle will meet on the ground. A vehicle that cannot survive the cold on the test stand will not survive the cold of a propellant load, and certainly not the cold of space.

Ship 44 went first. According to RGV Aerial Photography, whose photographers monitor Starbase around the clock, the ship headed to Massey's on 19 November, less than a week after Flight 15 lifted off. Cryogenic testing was under way on 20 November, and the next day RGV reported that the ship had completed its first cryogenic proof test. A third test was reported complete in a post at 1:00 a.m. Central Standard Time on 24 November.

Booster 24 followed hours behind its partner. Shortly after midnight on 20 November, the Super Heavy booster headed to Massey's. Its first cryogenic proof test was reported at about 3:16 p.m. CST on 22 November, with frost visible on both the liquid oxygen and methane tanks. In a post at 1:22 a.m. CST on 23 November, RGV said the booster had completed its first round of cryogenic proof testing.

Then both vehicles rolled back. At 11:50 p.m. CST on 23 November, RGV reported that Booster 24 had left the cryo station and moved to the Massey's exit. At 1:16 a.m. CST on 24 November, it reported that both Booster 24 and Ship 44 had entered Highway 4 — and called it the first time two vehicles had rolled in the same direction in the Starship programme. A later post that morning showed a timelapse of both vehicles leaving Massey's the night before.

That rollback is consistent with a finished stay at the test site, and some observers have already described the cryogenic campaign as complete. It is worth being precise about what that means, and what it does not. SpaceX has not posted that a Flight 17 test campaign is complete, and RGV described the booster's work as its "first round" — language that leaves open the possibility of more testing later. A finished stay at Massey's is not the same as a flight-ready vehicle, and a flight-ready vehicle is not the same as a launch date.

## A week at Massey's

The sequence of the past week is worth setting out in full, because it shows how compressed the Starship test cycle has become. Flight 15 launched on 13 November. Ship 42, its upper stage, spent about 22 and a half hours in orbit, deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites, and was caught by the launch tower's chopstick arms the following day — the first time a spacecraft returning from orbit has been caught by a tower. Booster 22, the flight's Super Heavy, did not make it back: its catch was aborted, and it was diverted to a splashdown in the Gulf.

Five days after that launch, the next pair of vehicles was already on the road to its first test. Ship 44 rolled to Massey's on 19 November. Booster 24 followed on 20 November. By 24 November, both had finished their cryogenic work and rolled home. The entire campaign — from first rollout to final rollback — took five days.

That speed is the story as much as the tests themselves. In the early years of the Starship programme, the interval between a flight and the next vehicle's first test was measured in months, not days. Vehicles were built, tested, flown, and then the lessons were folded into the next build over long stretches. What is happening now looks different: two flight vehicles are in the pipeline at once, one pair (Booster 23 and Ship 43, for Flight 16) already through its cryogenic tests in October, and the next pair (Booster 24 and Ship 44) through theirs in November. The production line at Starbase is now feeding the test programme almost as fast as the test programme can consume it.

The pairing itself, it should be stressed, is unofficial. SpaceX has not named the booster or the ship for Flight 17, and its official launches page lists no Flight 17 mission at all. The assignment comes from Starship Gazer, who on 21 November posted a photograph of both vehicles at Massey's and wrote: "Starship 44 and Super Heavy Booster 24 (Flight 17 vehicles) at Starbase TX Massey's test site for cryo proof testing." Starbase Surfer posted a photograph labelled "B24/S44" on 22 November showing both vehicles at the test site, without calling them Flight 17 hardware. Until SpaceX names the pair, the assignment should be treated as the best available identification, not as a confirmed manifest.

SpaceX has, on earlier flights, named the ship and booster only days or weeks before liftoff — on Flight 15, it named Ship 42 and Booster 22 before launch. There is no reason to expect an earlier announcement for Flight 17, and every reason to expect the same pattern: confirmation will come when SpaceX is ready to fly, not before.

## What cryogenic proof testing actually proves

It is easy to treat a cryo test as a formality — a cold soak before the real work of static fires and flight. It is not. For a vehicle the size of Starship, the cryogenic test is the first time the whole structure is asked to behave as a single pressure vessel at flight temperatures, and it is the test most likely to expose a manufacturing flaw before that flaw becomes a flight failure.

Starship's tanks are enormous. The Super Heavy booster stands about 71 metres tall and carries more than 3,400 tonnes of propellant when fully loaded; the Ship upper stage adds roughly another 1,500 tonnes. Both stages burn liquid methane and liquid oxygen, stored at temperatures around minus 160 degrees Celsius and colder. When those propellants are loaded, the stainless-steel tanks shrink by centimetres. Every weld, every pipe joint, every seal has to shrink with them and then expand again as the vehicle warms — repeatedly, across dozens of load cycles in a vehicle's life.

The proof test fills the tanks with liquid nitrogen, which is colder than liquid oxygen and a good deal colder than liquid methane, and pressurises them to levels above what flight demands. Engineers watch for leaks, for unexpected deformation, for any sign that a weld or a bulkhead is not carrying its share of the load. A vehicle that passes three cryogenic tests, as Ship 44 reportedly did, has demonstrated that its primary structure can take the thermal and pressure loads of a flight. A booster that completes its first round, as Booster 24 reportedly did, has cleared the same bar for its own, much larger tanks.

What the test does not prove is almost as important. It says nothing about the engines: a static fire, in which the Raptor engines are ignited on the ground, is a separate milestone, and none has been reported for Ship 44 or Booster 24. It says nothing about the vehicle's avionics, its guidance software, or its heat shield. And it says nothing about the launch licence. The most recent Starship licence revision that observers have seen authorised Flight 15; nothing has been announced for Flight 16 or Flight 17, and the Federal Aviation Administration licenses every Starship launch individually.

In other words, the cryogenic campaign is the first gate in a sequence, not the last. The vehicles have passed it — unofficially, and for the booster, in a first round that may be followed by more. The remaining gates are static fire, stacking on the launch mount, and a licence. None of those has been reported for the Flight 17 pair as of 25 November.

## Flight 16 first: the queue ahead of Flight 17

Flight 17 is not the next Starship flight. Flight 16 is. And Flight 16, like Flight 17, has no official date.

The Flight 16 pair — Booster 23 and Ship 43, both Version 3 vehicles like their Flight 17 successors — finished their own cryogenic tests in October. In late October, SpaceX completed what appeared to be catch tests of the upper stage, Ship 43, using the launch tower's chopstick arms at Pad 2. Reports have put Flight 16's launch in December, though SpaceX has announced nothing.

Where Flight 16 will fly from is an open question, and it is the most interesting unresolved detail in the Starship programme right now. Flight 16 was supposed to be the first Starship launch from Launch Complex 39A at NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida — a milestone in its own right, the first Starship flight from the historic pad that launched Apollo and the Space Shuttle. But a presentation from CADENA, dated 19 November, has fuelled speculation that the honour may go to Flight 17 instead, with Flight 16 launching from Starbase. The presentation's "Upcoming High-Profile Missions" slide makes no mention of any Starship mission, which is itself a data point: if either flight had a firm Florida date, it would be a strange thing to leave off.

One informed reading, offered by observers of the programme, is that SpaceX may opt to fly a repeat of Flight 15 from Starbase as Flight 16, and then fly Flight 17 from 39A. That would require a booster and a ship to be sent to Florida — the phrase used by one commentator was "sent on You'll Thank Me Later," the name of the transport vessel — and a fair amount of testing at 39A first. It is speculation, but it is speculation grounded in the physical reality that a Florida launch needs hardware in Florida, and the Flight 17 vehicles are, for now, in Texas.

What is certain is that Flights 12, 13, 14 and 15 all lifted off from Pad 2 at Starbase, Texas, and that SpaceX is also preparing to launch Starship from Florida. Which flight breaks the Texas streak, and when, remains to be seen.

## The shadow of Flight 15

To understand why Flight 17 matters, it helps to remember what Flight 15 did — and what it did not do.

Flight 15, which launched on 13 November, was historic for more than one reason. Ship 42 reached orbit, deployed all 26 of its Starlink V3 satellites, and then, after about 22 and a half hours and 14 orbits, came back to Starbase and was caught by the tower's chopstick arms. It was the first time a spacecraft returning from orbit had been caught by a tower, and it was the second half of a double catch that SpaceX had been chasing since the programme began: a booster caught on the way up, a ship caught on the way down.

Only the second half happened. Booster 22's catch was aborted around six and a half minutes into the flight, and the booster was diverted to a splashdown in the Gulf. SpaceX's post-flight analysis said that one of the booster's 33 Raptor engines failed to reignite for the boostback burn, which ended early because of low fuel, and that the booster's guidance, navigation and control system then triggered an automated abort. The booster exploded just moments before splashdown.

That is the context in which Flight 16 and Flight 17 are being prepared. SpaceX has caught a Super Heavy booster three times already, but the aborted catch of the latest Version 3 booster — the first catch attempt of the newest iteration of the vehicle — means the programme still has something to prove on the booster side. The Starship programme's goal is full and rapid reusability: both stages caught, refurbished, and flown again. Flight 15 proved the ship half of that equation in the most dramatic way possible. The booster half, for the Version 3 vehicle, is still open.

<a href="/blog/starship-flight-15-booster-catch-aborted-ship-42-orbit/">Starship Flight 15: Booster Catch Aborted as Ship 42 Reaches Orbit</a> covered the flight itself, and <a href="/blog/ship-42-mechazilla-catch-starship-flight-15/">Ship 42's Mechazilla Catch</a> told the story of the tower catch the following morning. The two articles together are the essential background for what comes next: a programme that has now demonstrated it can bring a ship home, and is working to make the booster side as reliable.

## The road to the pad

Between the cryogenic tests and a launch, the Flight 17 vehicles still have to clear the milestones that every Starship flight clears. As of 25 November, the status of each is as follows — with the important caveat that everything below the line marked "unofficial" comes from Starbase observers, not from SpaceX.

| Milestone | Flight 15 (flown) | Flight 16 (Booster 23 / Ship 43) | Flight 17 (Booster 24 / Ship 44) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cryogenic proof tests | Complete | Complete (October) | Reported done, unofficial (19–24 Nov) |
| Static fire | Complete | Not reported | Not reported |
| Stacking on launch mount | Complete | Not reported | Not reported |
| FAA licence | Authorised | Not announced | Not announced |
| Launch date | 13 Nov 2026 | Not announced; December reported | Not announced; early 2027 expected |
| Launch site | Pad 2, Starbase | Not announced; 39A or Starbase debated | Not announced; 39A or Starbase debated |
| Key outcome | Ship 42 caught; Booster 22 catch aborted | — | — |

The table makes the shape of the programme clear. Flight 16 is one step ahead of Flight 17 in testing, and its launch is reported to be targeted for December. Flight 17's vehicles have finished their cryogenic work, but everything else — static fire, stacking, licence, date, site — is still to come. A finished cryogenic campaign, even if confirmed later, would not by itself set a Flight 17 date. Flight 16 still has no official date either, and some of the same licence and pad work may need to happen for that flight first.

There is also the question of what Flight 17 will carry. Flight 15 deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites, the larger next-generation satellites that are a central part of SpaceX's case for Starship: the vehicle exists, in large part, to put those satellites into orbit in numbers no other rocket can match. Whether Flight 17 repeats that mission profile, attempts another double catch, or tries something new has not been announced — and will not be, in all likelihood, until SpaceX is ready to fly.

## Timeline: from Flight 15's catch to Flight 17's freezer

The last twelve days at Starbase, in order:

- **13 November 2026** — Starship Flight 15 launches from Pad 2 at Starbase. Ship 42 reaches orbit and deploys 26 Starlink V3 satellites. Booster 22's catch is aborted; the booster is diverted to the Gulf.
- **14 November 2026** — After about 22.5 hours and 14 orbits, Ship 42 is caught by the tower's chopstick arms — the first catch of a spacecraft returning from orbit.
- **19 November 2026** — Ship 44 heads to Massey's for cryogenic proof testing. A CADENA presentation dated the same day fuels speculation about whether Flight 16 or Flight 17 will debut from Launch Complex 39A in Florida.
- **20 November 2026** — Ship 44's cryogenic testing is under way; the next day it is reported to have completed its first cryogenic proof test. Shortly after midnight, Booster 24 heads to Massey's.
- **21 November 2026** — Starship Gazer photographs Ship 44 and Booster 24 at Massey's and identifies them as the Flight 17 vehicles.
- **22 November 2026** — Booster 24's first cryogenic proof test is reported at about 3:16 p.m. CST, with frost on both propellant tanks. Starbase Surfer photographs both vehicles at the test site.
- **23 November 2026** — At 1:22 a.m. CST, Booster 24 is reported to have completed its first round of cryogenic proof testing. At 11:50 p.m. CST, the booster leaves the cryo station for the Massey's exit.
- **24 November 2026** — At 1:00 a.m. CST, Ship 44 is reported to have completed its third cryogenic proof test. At 1:16 a.m. CST, both vehicles enter Highway 4 together, rolling back from Massey's.
- **25 November 2026** — SpaceX has still announced no date, window, vehicles, or launch site for Flight 17. Flight 16, which would fly first, is likewise unannounced.

Twelve days from a historic catch to a finished cryogenic campaign for the flight after next. That is the pace SpaceX is now setting, and it is the pace its competitors — and its own launch licence paperwork — will have to keep up with.

## Why Flight 17 matters

It would be easy to dismiss a cryogenic test campaign as a footnote — a routine step in a programme that now produces them almost weekly. That would miss the point. Flight 17 is the flight where the Starship programme's new rhythm becomes visible: two flights in preparation at once, a production line that no longer waits for one flight to finish before starting the next, and a test cadence measured in days rather than months.

It is also the flight where several open questions converge. Will SpaceX repeat the double catch that Flight 15 only half achieved, and can the Version 3 booster be caught after its first attempt ended in an abort? Will the Florida debut finally happen, and if so, will it be Flight 16 or Flight 17 that takes the honour? And can the programme sustain a launch cadence — December for Flight 16, early 2027 for Flight 17 — that would put Starship on a path to the operational tempo its Starlink deployment plans demand?

None of those questions has an answer yet. What has an answer is narrower, and more concrete: two new vehicles have been built, taken to the test site, frozen, and brought back. The freezer did not break them. That is what a cryogenic proof test is for, and it is, for now, the most that can honestly be said about Starship Flight 17.

The rest — the static fires, the stacking, the licence, the date — will come in their turn. If the last twelve days are any guide, it will not take long.
