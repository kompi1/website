Somewhere about 400 kilometres above Earth, in a laboratory module the size of a bus, a cloud of rubidium atoms has just become the coldest thing ever measured. Not the coldest place, or the coldest object in some loose sense, but the coldest gas of atoms ever produced: 9.7 picokelvins, or 9.7 trillionths of a degree above absolute zero. It is roughly 280 billion times colder than the afterglow of the Big Bang that fills the universe. And it was made not in a basement laboratory on Earth, but aboard China's Tiangong space station, in a rack of lasers and vacuum chambers that has been quietly running experiments since 2022.

The result, published in the peer-reviewed journal *Science* on November 5, 2026, matters for more than the record books. The atoms were cooled into a Bose-Einstein condensate, a strange state of matter in which thousands of individual atoms stop behaving like a crowd and start behaving like a single quantum wave. It is the fifth state of matter, predicted by Satyendra Nath Bose and Albert Einstein a century ago and first created in laboratories in 1995. Getting there in orbit — and, crucially, holding the cloud still long enough to study it — opens a door to measurements that cannot be done properly on the ground: exquisitely precise tests of gravity, of Einstein's equivalence principle, and of the instruments that may one day navigate spacecraft by the atoms themselves.

What makes this result different from every cold-atom experiment before it is not only the temperature. It is the way the temperature was reached. For the first time in orbit, atoms in this quantum state were confined using lasers alone, with no magnetic field holding them in place. And in weightlessness, the laser trap could be weakened almost to nothing, letting the cloud expand in slow motion for seven full seconds — a duration that atomic physicists say is scientifically as significant as the temperature itself.

## The coldest cloud ever made

The experiment began with roughly 20,000 rubidium atoms, a soft silvery metal that has become the workhorse of ultracold physics because its atoms respond cleanly to laser light. Trapped inside the Cold Atom Physics Rack — known as CAPR — in Tiangong's Mengtian laboratory module, the atoms were first coaxed into a Bose-Einstein condensate, the state in which they occupy the same quantum state and move as one.

Then came the cooling itself. The team, led by physicists Wei Xiong, Tang Li and Shengjie Jin, working with colleagues from Peking University, the Shanghai Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Technology and Engineering Center for Space Utilization, used a two-step recipe. First they let the most energetic atoms escape from the trap, carrying heat away with them — the same principle, in essence, as steam leaving a cup of coffee. Then they sharply reduced the laser power, narrowing the spread of velocities left in the cloud still further.

Here weightlessness did the heavy lifting. On Earth, a cloud this cold falls to the bottom of its vacuum chamber within a fraction of a second, pulled down by gravity before it can be measured. In a drop tower — a tall shaft in which an experiment falls freely to simulate weightlessness — the cloud can be watched for about two seconds. Aboard Tiangong, the released cloud stayed observable for seven seconds. The atoms were barely moving at all, at speeds below 100 micrometres per second, slower than a garden snail crossing a leaf.

The temperature itself was never read off a thermometer. No thermometer can register 9.7 picokelvins. Instead, the researchers calculated it from how slowly the cloud spread out after the trap let it go — the slower the expansion, the colder the gas. The figure carries an uncertainty range of 7.0 to 12.9 picokelvins, and the paper describes it as the lowest equivalent temperature ever measured for a gas of atoms.

To put that number in perspective, consider what it beats. The previous record of 38 picokelvins was set in 2021 at the Bremen Drop Tower in Germany, a German-French collaboration at the University of Bremen, during a brief free fall. NASA's Cold Atom Laboratory aboard the International Space Station — the only other orbital cold-atom facility — had reached about 52 picokelvins using magnetic trapping and a technique called matter-wave lensing. The Tiangong result is roughly four times colder than the Bremen record and more than five times colder than the space station's previous best.

And the natural universe is not even close. The cosmic microwave background, the faint radiation left over from the Big Bang that fills all of space, sits at roughly 2.7 kelvins. The cloud aboard Tiangong was about 280 billion times colder than that. There is no known natural environment in the universe as cold as this laboratory-made gas. The coldest natural temperatures in space — the dark molecular clouds where stars are born — hover around 10 kelvins, more than a trillion times warmer.

## Lasers instead of magnets

The technical detail that specialists are most excited about is the trap itself. Every Bose-Einstein condensate previously made in orbit was confined using magnetic traps built onto atom chips — tiny devices that can hold only atoms in certain quantum states. A magnetic trap is picky: it works for some atomic states and not others, and its fields can disturb the very measurements the experiment is trying to make.

The Tiangong team did it differently. They used nested optical traps — focused beams of laser light that attract atoms regardless of their quantum state. Light, unlike magnetism, does not care which internal state an atom is in. That freedom matters enormously for the experiments that come next, because the most sensitive tests of fundamental physics need atoms in states that magnetic traps cannot hold.

In orbit, the optical approach gains a second advantage that no ground laboratory can match. On Earth, an optical trap must be strong enough to hold atoms up against gravity, and that strength itself heats and disturbs the cloud. In weightlessness, the atoms do not drift relative to the apparatus at all, so the trap can be weakened almost to zero. The team exploited exactly that: with gravity out of the picture, they dialled the laser power down until the cloud was barely held, and then let it go entirely, watching it expand in slow motion for seven seconds.

It is worth pausing on what seven seconds means in this field. An atom cloud released on Earth is gone from view in a fraction of a second. In the Bremen Drop Tower, the best ground-based simulation of weightlessness, researchers had two seconds. Seven seconds is not a marginal improvement; it is a different regime. Holger Mueller, an atomic physicist at the University of California, Berkeley, who was not involved in the study, told *Science* that the seven seconds is an even more important figure of merit than the 10 picokelvins. He added that ground-based experiments may eventually match the temperature achieved on Tiangong — "Someday somebody may get something on the ground as cold as 10 picokelvin" — but matching the observation time is another matter entirely.

The Cold Atom Physics Rack that made all of this possible has been aboard Tiangong since 2022, installed in the Mengtian laboratory module. It is, in effect, a miniaturised ultracold-atom laboratory: lasers, vacuum chambers and control electronics packed into a rack that runs experiments by remote command while the station circles the Earth every 90 minutes. The station itself has been busy on other fronts this month, with the <a href="/blog/shenzhou-24-pakistani-astronaut-tiangong-november-2026/">Shenzhou-24 Pakistani Astronaut Tiangong November 2026: China's First Foreign Visitor Heads to Orbit</a> mission preparing to carry the first foreign visitor to the station — a reminder that Tiangong is now a working research outpost, not just a symbol.

## The race to absolute zero

Absolute zero — 0 kelvin, or minus 273.15 degrees Celsius — is the temperature at which thermal motion stops entirely. It is a limit that can be approached but never reached, and the history of ultracold physics is a history of approaching it in ever smaller steps. The Tiangong result is the latest and largest of those steps, but it is best understood as part of a race that has run for three decades across laboratories, drop towers and two space stations.

| Milestone | Year | Temperature | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| First Bose-Einstein condensate created | 1995 | ~170 nanokelvins | Laboratory, Earth |
| Bremen Drop Tower record | 2021 | 38 picokelvins | Free fall, ~2 seconds of observation |
| NASA Cold Atom Lab, ISS | 2018–2026 | ~52 picokelvins | Orbit, magnetic trap |
| Tiangong Cold Atom Physics Rack | 2026 | 9.7 picokelvins | Orbit, optical trap, 7 seconds of observation |
| Cosmic microwave background (for comparison) | — | ~2.7 kelvins | All of space |
| Coldest dark molecular clouds | — | ~10 kelvins | Interstellar space |

The table makes two things clear. First, the Tiangong result is about four times colder than the previous record — a jump comparable in scale to the whole advance of the previous five years. Second, the natural universe stopped competing long ago: even the coldest corners of interstellar space are more than a trillion times warmer than this cloud. The coldest thing we know of is now something humans built, in orbit, on purpose.

There is a subtlety worth stating plainly, because it is often lost in headlines about "the coldest temperature ever." The 9.7 picokelvins is an equivalent temperature — a number calculated from the motion of the atoms, not a reading from a thermometer, and not the temperature of a macroscopic object you could touch. A block of metal at 9.7 picokelvins does not exist and cannot exist. What exists is a cloud of 20,000 atoms moving so slowly, so uniformly, that the temperature describing their motion is 9.7 trillionths of a degree above absolute zero. That is precisely what makes the measurement useful: the slower and more uniform the motion, the sharper the quantum behaviour, and the more precise the instruments built from it.

## Seven seconds that matter more than the temperature

Why does anyone need atoms this cold? The answer lies in a technique called atom interferometry, and it is the reason space agencies on both sides of the Pacific have been putting cold-atom laboratories in orbit.

An atom interferometer works by splitting a cloud of atoms into two parts, sending them along different paths, and then bringing them back together. Even the smallest difference in gravity between the two paths changes the result — the two halves of the cloud interfere with each other like waves, and the interference pattern reveals the difference. The sensitivity of such an instrument increases with the square of the observation time. Double the time the cloud can be watched, and the instrument becomes four times more sensitive. Seven seconds instead of two is not a 3.5-times improvement; by that scaling, it is more than a twelvefold gain in sensitivity.

That is why Mueller's comment matters. The temperature record is the headline, but the seven-second observation window is the capability. An orbital atom interferometer could measure Earth's gravitational field more accurately than any instrument on the ground, mapping underground water, magma movements and the slow rebound of continents after the last ice age with a precision that today's satellites cannot match. It could also serve as a navigation instrument: a spacecraft that measures gravity precisely enough can work out where it is without any signal from Earth — a capability that matters enormously for missions to the Moon and Mars, where GPS does not exist.

The same apparatus points at a deeper question. One of the research applications the authors list for an orbital laser trap is testing the equivalence principle — the idea, at the heart of Einstein's general theory of relativity, that all objects fall at the same rate in a gravitational field regardless of what they are made of. To test it, clouds of atoms of two different chemical elements are placed in a trap, released simultaneously, and an atom interferometer compares whether they fall at exactly the same rate. No such measurement has yet been conducted aboard the space station. But if the two clouds ever fall even slightly differently, Einstein's theory would have to be reconsidered — and a trap that can hold any atomic state, for seven seconds, in weightlessness, is exactly the tool that makes the test possible.

## What the cold means for the space race

The Tiangong result also lands in a wider contest that has little to do with thermometers. The International Space Station's Cold Atom Laboratory, built by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, has been operating since 2018 and set the previous space record of 52 picokelvins. The station that hosts it is ageing: NASA has released its final request for proposals for commercial stations to replace it before its planned fiery plunge into the Pacific in 2030–2031, a transition covered in <a href="/blog/goodbye-iss-deorbit-commercial-stations/">Goodbye, ISS: How NASA Plans to Crash the Space Station — and Who Replaces It</a>. The Tiangong result is a reminder that the centre of gravity in orbital science is shifting, and that China now operates the most capable cold-atom laboratory ever flown.

That is not a story of one side winning and the other losing. Cold-atom physics is one of the few fields in which the major spacefaring nations have pursued strikingly parallel programmes — NASA's Cold Atom Lab, the European and German drop-tower experiments, and now China's orbital optical trap — and the results build on one another in the open literature. The Bremen record taught the field what free fall could do; the ISS laboratory taught it what a magnetic trap could do in sustained orbit; Tiangong has now shown what an optical trap can do when gravity is removed altogether. Each step made the next one conceivable.

China's broader space programme has been moving at a similar pace. The same month the cold-atom paper appeared in *Science*, the country's crewed lunar programme was laying out its clearest roadmap yet toward a landing before 2030, as described in <a href="/blog/china-moon-landing-2030-long-march-10-plan/">China Moon Landing 2030 Long March 10 Plan: Two Rockets, One Rendezvous, One Deadline</a>. The cold-atom result is a different kind of milestone — fundamental physics rather than flags and footprints — but it comes from the same infrastructure, the same stations and the same long-term investment in doing science in orbit rather than merely visiting it.

For NASA and its partners, the result is also a prompt. The techniques demonstrated aboard Tiangong — optical trapping, long free-expansion observation, state-independent confinement — are directly applicable to the next generation of instruments the agency wants in orbit, from gravitational sensors to the quantum clocks that would underpin deep-space navigation. The equivalence-principle tests that Tiangong's authors list as future work are tests that any space agency would want to run, and the seven-second window is now the benchmark they will have to match.

## The long road from a prediction to a record

It is easy, reading a number like 9.7 picokelvins, to miss how long the road to it has been. Bose and Einstein predicted the condensate in the 1920s, working from the new quantum statistics of indistinguishable particles. It took seventy years — and the invention of laser cooling, which won its own Nobel Prize in 1997 — before anyone made one, in 1995. It took another quarter-century to carry the apparatus into orbit, first with magnetic traps on the International Space Station, and now with an all-optical trap on Tiangong.

Each of those steps solved a problem that had looked fundamental. Laser cooling solved the problem of reaching microkelvin temperatures at all. Evaporative cooling solved the problem of the last thousandfold drop to condensation. Drop towers solved the problem of watching a condensate without gravity pulling it apart. And orbit solved the problem of watching it for long enough to do real physics — seven seconds, and counting.

The researchers themselves are careful about what comes next. No equivalence-principle measurement has yet been conducted aboard the station. No atom interferometer has yet flown on the optical trap. The paper reports a capability — a trap, a temperature, a window of time — and lists the experiments that capability now makes possible. That restraint is part of why the result is credible: it is a measurement, published in a peer-reviewed journal, with an uncertainty range stated openly and a method that other laboratories can examine, criticise and build upon.

What is already clear is the scale of the achievement. A cloud of 20,000 atoms, held by light alone, drifting slower than a snail, colder than anything nature has ever produced, watched for seven seconds while the station carrying it swept around the Earth at 27,600 kilometres per hour. The universe is 13.8 billion years old, and in all that time, in all that space, nothing has ever been that cold — until a laboratory the size of a wardrobe, bolted to a space station, made it so.

## What to watch next

The first thing to watch is what the Tiangong team does with the seven seconds. The paper points to atom interferometry and equivalence-principle tests as the natural next experiments, and the history of this field suggests they will not wait long: a trap that can hold any atomic state is an invitation to load it with two different elements and drop them together. If those tests run, and if the two clouds fall at the same rate to ever greater precision, Einstein's theory survives its sternest examination yet. If they do not — if some tiny difference appears — physics has a new problem, and a new century of work.

The second thing to watch is the response. NASA's Cold Atom Laboratory is still operating aboard the International Space Station, and the techniques demonstrated on Tiangong will inform whatever replaces it — whether on a commercial station after 2030 or on a dedicated free-flying quantum laboratory. The Bremen Drop Tower team, whose 38-picokelvin record stood for five years, will be asking what their next experiment needs to look like. And the wider community of atomic physicists, who have spent a decade arguing about whether orbit is worth the cost for fundamental physics, now has a number to argue with: 9.7 picokelvins, seven seconds, lasers alone.

The third is the quietest and perhaps the most important. The coldest thing in the universe is no longer a place. It is an instrument — and instruments get better. The trap can be weakened further. The cloud can be held longer. More atoms can be loaded, different elements can be tried, and the expansion can be watched with better cameras. The record of 9.7 picokelvins will fall, very likely within a few years, and very likely to a team standing on the result reported this month. That is how science works: a record is not an ending but a floor, and the floor has just been lowered by a factor of four.

In the meantime, the cloud is gone — released, expanded, measured, and dispersed in the vacuum of the Cold Atom Physics Rack, somewhere above an ocean or a continent, in the dark. It lasted seven seconds. It was the coldest thing ever made. And the next one will be colder still.
