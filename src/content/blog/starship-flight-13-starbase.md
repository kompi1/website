Thirteen has a reputation. Across aviation, it is the number airlines skip. At SpaceX's Starbase facility on the Texas Gulf coast, it became the number that finally delivered. On Friday, July 24, 2026, at 5:51 p.m. Central time, Starship — the largest and most powerful rocket ever flown — rose from Orbital Launch Pad 2 on its thirteenth integrated test flight, ending a frustrating eight-day stretch of false starts and carrying something no Starship had ever carried before: a real, working payload.

Two weeks earlier the mission had been penciled in for mid-July with quiet confidence. Instead, it took three launch attempts, one engine abort, one tropical storm, and a round-the-clock engine swap to get the 407-foot vehicle off the ground. When it finally flew, it did nearly everything SpaceX asked of it: all 33 Raptor 3 engines lit at liftoff, the Super Heavy booster completed the first full 33-engine boostback burn ever performed by a V3 vehicle, the upper stage deployed 20 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites and talked to every one of them, a single Raptor engine relit in the vacuum of space, and the ship survived reentry to settle upright into the Indian Ocean — intact, floating, and still transmitting. It was, by every available measure, the best Starship flight yet. The one sour note came from the booster, which hit the Gulf of Mexico harder than planned and was lost. But on a day when the spacecraft carried its first operational cargo, SpaceX was not dwelling on the booster.

## The storm before the calm

Flight 13 was supposed to fly on July 16. On the pad that morning, Starship stood fully stacked — Ship 40 atop Booster 20, both in the new V3 configuration — while the launch team worked through the countdown. But during the final engine chill sequence, one of the booster's Raptor engines showed ignition parameters the flight computers did not like. SpaceX's automated systems aborted the countdown, a textbook response for a vehicle that lives or dies by the health of its 33 booster engines.

The abort triggered days of around-the-clock work. Engineers pulled several Raptor 3 engines from the booster for inspection and replacement, combed through telemetry for the root cause, and made software adjustments to the engine startup sequence — particularly the booster's directional flip maneuver, the dramatic rotation that reorients the 232-foot-tall booster after stage separation. By midweek, the rocket was back on the pad and targeting Thursday, July 23.

Then the weather intervened. Tropical Storm Bertha had organized over the Gulf and was tracking toward a Texas landfall, dragging bands of rain and gusty winds toward the Boca Chica site. SpaceX pushed the launch a day. The 90-minute window that had been planned to open at 5:45 p.m. Central on the 23rd slid to the 24th instead, and crews buttoned up the pad for the storm's brush.

By Friday afternoon, the skies over Starbase had cleared. What followed was a clean countdown and, at 5:51 p.m. CDT, the ignition of all 33 Raptor 3 engines — a pillar of fire and steam that pushed the 407-foot stack off the pad with more than 16 million pounds of thrust. For a program that has now flown a dozen times, liftoff still drew the familiar wall of sound across South Padre Island and the Boca Chica viewing areas.

## A tale of two vehicles

Super Heavy Booster 20's flight began flawlessly. All 33 engines lit and stayed lit through the climb, a notable achievement for the V3 booster, which flies the newest Raptor 3 engines with simplified, more rugged plumbing and shielding. About two minutes and forty seconds in, hot staging separated the booster from Ship 40 in a shower of flame, and the booster flipped for home — the directional maneuver its new startup software had been tuned to smooth out.

The boostback burn was a milestone in its own right. Every one of the booster's 33 engines ran at high thrust to reverse the booster's course and send it back toward the Gulf — the first time a V3 Super Heavy had completed a full 33-engine boostback. Then came the landing burn, and this is where the story split.

Only a fraction of the engines needed for the landing burn started up. SpaceX spokesperson Dan Huot, narrating the company webcast, explained that not all of the Raptors slated for the burn lit as planned, and the booster descended toward the water faster than its programmed profile. The result was a hard splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico — fast enough to destroy the vehicle. It was a pointed reminder that for all the progress in V3's propulsion reliability, the final seconds of booster recovery remain the toughest part of the flight.

SpaceX, however, had never planned to catch Booster 20 or save it. Neither vehicle on this flight was meant to be recovered; both were always destined for the sea. The hard landing was a data point, not a failure of the mission plan — and the full-duration boostback burn gave engineers a rich new dataset on the V3 booster's most demanding phase of flight.

## The ship earns its keep

If the booster gave a mixed performance, Ship 40 delivered the flight of the year. After separation, its six Raptor engines burned nominally to reach the planned suborbital speed, and about half an hour into the flight, the ship's Pez-style dispenser began deploying its cargo: 20 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites, the first operational payload in Starship program history.

This was no mass simulator, no dummy cargo. These were functioning spacecraft — larger and more powerful than any Starlink flown before, with solar arrays that generate roughly twice the power of prior generations, built by SpaceX's team in Redmond, Washington. They were also on a deadline: released onto a suborbital trajectory, the satellites had only about 20 minutes of free flight before reentering the atmosphere, and they needed to prove they could deploy, power up, and talk to SpaceX's networks in that narrow window.

They did. The satellites unfolded their solar arrays and antennas, and SpaceX engineers established contact with all 20 using both radio-frequency and laser links, downloading key telemetry before the satellites fell back over the Indian Ocean. SpaceX executive Michael Nicolls confirmed on social media that the team had communicated with every satellite and pulled down data — a 100 percent link success rate. Every one of the Starlinks "accomplished all their jobs," as SpaceX engineer Kate Tice put it on the webcast, calling the flight "lucky flight 13."

Then came the second act. Ship 40 shut down one of its Raptor engines at a planned moment — and then, in the vacuum of space, lit it again. The in-space relight succeeded exactly as designed. It sounds like a small thing next to the fire and thunder of liftoff, but an in-space relight is one of the fundamental capabilities Starship needs to become a true orbital vehicle: without it, no controlled deorbit, no orbital rendezvous, no precision landing from orbit. A failed relight attempt on an earlier flight had made this demonstration a point of pride for the propulsion team, and this time the engine behaved.

## The best reentry yet

What happened next was the part SpaceX had been chasing for years. Ship 40 turned its belly to the atmosphere and plunged back at hypersonic speed, generating the characteristic pink plasma glow of reentry — a shimmering cocoon of ionized gas that played across the ship's stainless steel skin. The vehicle executed a dynamic banking maneuver, shedding speed in the upper atmosphere, and then flipped vertical for its landing burn over the Indian Ocean.

More than 10,000 miles from where it launched, the spacecraft hovered upright above the waves, lowered itself into the water, and stayed there — upright, intact, and floating. Cameras caught the moment: a silver cylinder resting on the ocean like a buoy, wreathed in only a few small flames. Previous flights had ended with the ship engulfed in fire, exploding at splashdown, or rupturing midair. This time, SpaceX called it the best reentry yet. The company even got something it had never had before: the first clear views of an undamaged heat shield after a full flight, giving thermal protection engineers their first look at how the tiles actually perform when everything goes right.

It was a vindication of the V3 design. The upgraded ship flies improved thermal protection, refined flaps, and the newest Raptor 3 vacuum engines — all of which had been waiting for a flight like this one to prove themselves.

## Why Flight 13 mattered more than 12

Context makes Flight 13's significance clearer. Flight 12, which flew the V3 configuration for the first time, had been a partial story: a successful launch shadowed by the failed in-space relight and a rougher reentry. Flight 13 was the correction. It checked every box that Flight 12 had left open, and added the payload milestone that turns Starship from a test article into a working launch system.

That payload milestone is the one the space industry will remember. Starship exists to fly Starlink's next generation — the V3 satellites are too large for Falcon 9's fairing, and their deployment is the linchpin of SpaceX's satellite internet business plan, which is also the revenue engine funding the Mars program. Demonstrating that a Starship can carry real satellites to space, release them, and have every one phone home is the moment the rocket began earning its keep.

The timing adds another layer. This was SpaceX's first Starship flight since the company became a publicly traded entity, meaning a new class of shareholders watched alongside the enthusiasts — and what they saw was a flight that succeeded on every objective within its control. With more than 10,000 Starlink satellites already in orbit and the constellation still growing, the V3 deployment demonstration mapped directly onto the company's balance sheet.

## What comes next

SpaceX is not resting. Flight 14 is already on the horizon, and it carries ambitions Flight 13 deliberately deferred: an actual attempt to catch the Super Heavy booster back at the launch tower with the "chopstick" arms, and potentially the first attempt to recover the ship. The Flight 13 data — particularly the heat shield imagery and the relight telemetry — feeds directly into those plans.

Meanwhile, the test campaign's geography is expanding. While Starbase remains the program's home, SpaceX is advancing Starship facilities on Florida's Space Coast, where a growing Starship pad and processing infrastructure at the Cape will eventually let the vehicle fly from both coasts. The Florida expansion is not cosmetic: polar and high-inclination missions, certain national security payloads, and the sheer cadence SpaceX envisions all argue for an East Coast Starship base. In 2026, the Cape's Falcon pads have been running at a relentless Starlink tempo; Starship's arrival there will mark the beginning of the transition from the Falcon era to the Starship era.

For now, though, the story belongs to a Friday evening in South Texas, when a rocket named unlucky by tradition turned out to be anything but. Two scrubs, a tropical storm, and an engine swap later, Starship Flight 13 did what the previous twelve flights had been building toward: it flew like a spaceship, not an experiment. It carried cargo. It talked to its cargo. It relit its engine in the dark. And then it came home — gently, upright, and floating on a warm Indian Ocean swell — while engineers watched the clearest pictures of a post-flight heat shield anyone had ever seen. After everything the program has been through, that counted as a very good night indeed.

## The road from Flight 12

To understand why July 24 felt like a turning point, it helps to rewind to Flight 12 — the V3 debut. That flight proved the upgraded vehicle's fundamentals: the new Raptor 3 engines, the revised booster structure, the ship's aerodynamic changes. But it left two conspicuous gaps. The in-space engine relight failed, and the ship's reentry, while survived, was rough enough that engineers wanted a cleaner data set before committing to recovery attempts.

Flight 13 was therefore designed as the answer flight. The mission profile intentionally repeated Flight 12's hardest tests — the relight, the reentry, the satellite deployment that had been downgraded on earlier missions — so that the team could compare results directly. When the relight worked, when the deployment worked, when the reentry came back cleaner than any previous attempt, it closed the loop. SpaceX's test program has always been built on this kind of iterative closure: fly, find the weak point, fix it, fly again, and let each flight retire risk for the next one.

The engine swap between the July 16 abort and the July 24 success exemplified the approach. Rather than accepting marginal engine performance, the team pulled hardware, inspected it, replaced what needed replacing, and tuned the startup software. The result was a countdown where all 33 booster engines lit and stayed lit — the kind of clean ignition that had eluded the program on its hardest days.

## A word about the booster

It would be easy to read the hard splashdown as the flight's failure, and plenty of headlines did. But SpaceX's framing was different, and it deserves a hearing. Booster 20 was an expendable test article from the moment the flight plan was written. The landing burn experiment — throttling and relighting a subset of 33 engines after a full-duration boostback — was exactly the kind of high-risk test the early Starship flights exist to run. The partial ignition that doomed the booster generated the telemetry needed to understand why those engines did not light, and that understanding is what makes the next attempt safer.

There is also a subtlety in the booster results worth appreciating. The full 33-engine boostback burn was a first for V3, and it worked. The boostback is arguably the more technically demanding burn: 33 engines at high thrust, a precise trajectory reversal, and the thermal and structural loads that come with it. The landing burn is a smaller subset of engines in a more forgiving regime. Fixing the landing-burn ignition sequence is, in the grand scheme of Starship development, a tractable problem — and the flight gave engineers a head start on it.

## The Starlink V3 connection

One detail that has not received enough attention: Flight 13's 20 satellites were not just any payload. The Starlink V3 spacecraft are the reason Starship exists in its current form. Too wide for Falcon 9's 5.2-meter fairing, the V3 buses need Starship's cavernous payload bay — and Starship needs the V3 deployment cadence to justify its economics. Each Starship flight can carry dozens of these satellites once operational, and the constellation's next phase of growth depends on that capacity.

The satellites' design reflects the leap. With roughly double the power generation of the previous generation, the V3 spacecraft carry larger antennas and more capable processors, enabling higher throughput per satellite and new service tiers. SpaceX's Redmond team, which has been iterating the Starlink design at a furious pace since the constellation's early days, treated Flight 13 as the V3 line's graduation day. All 20 satellites communicating on their first flight — via both RF and laser crosslinks — was the kind of result that lets a program move from testing to production with confidence.

For a company that now describes itself as the operator of the world's largest satellite constellation, the demonstration closed a circle: the rocket built to deploy the next generation finally did so, and the next generation proved it was ready to be deployed.

## The public company era

Flight 13 also carried a quiet historical footnote: it was the first Starship flight since SpaceX became a publicly traded company. For two decades, SpaceX's test program played out in front of enthusiasts and journalists, with Elon Musk's social media account as the primary channel of record. Now there is a shareholder base watching, and the July 24 flight gave those new investors their first taste of the SpaceX experience — the scrubbed attempts, the storm delay, the triumphant recovery, and a webcast narrated with characteristic candor about what worked and what did not.

If the public-market debut added pressure, it did not show. The flight's open communication — Dan Huot's real-time explanation of the booster's hard landing, Michael Nicolls' satellite-by-satellite updates — followed the company's long tradition of narrating failures alongside successes. For a program whose entire philosophy is that testing is learning, that transparency is not a liability; it is the product.

## Looking ahead to Flight 14

With Flight 13 in the books, attention turns to what SpaceX has been building toward since the first chopstick catch: a flight where both stages come home. Flight 14 is expected to attempt a booster catch at the Starbase tower, using the data from Booster 20's flight — including its imperfect landing burn — to refine the terminal guidance. And the ship, now proven through the softest splashdown in program history, is a candidate for the first ship recovery attempt, whether at sea or, eventually, back at the tower.

The Florida pads add a longer-term dimension. As Starship infrastructure rises at the Cape, the program is laying the groundwork for a two-coast flight rate that Falcon 9 could never achieve with Starship-class payloads. In 2026, that is still a future vision — but every successful V3 flight makes it a little less distant.

On the evening of July 24, though, none of that was on anyone's mind. There was just a silver ship floating on the Indian Ocean, a few small flames dying out around its base, and the first clear pictures of a heat shield that had done its job. Lucky flight 13, indeed.
