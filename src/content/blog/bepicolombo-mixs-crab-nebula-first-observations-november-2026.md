After eight years in space and a journey of 10 billion kilometres, a telescope that had never once looked at the sky finally opened its eyes — and what it saw was a dead star, 6,500 light-years away, glowing in X-rays. The Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer, known as MIXS, aboard the European Space Agency and Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's BepiColombo mission, has captured its first observations of an astronomical object: the Crab Nebula, one of the brightest X-ray sources in the sky.

The result, announced by the University of Leicester on 20 November, is more than a pretty picture. It is the first proof that the only UK-led science instrument on BepiColombo survived the long cruise to the inner solar system in full working order — and it arrives on the very day the spacecraft is due to enter orbit around Mercury, the smallest and least explored rocky planet in our solar system.

For the team that built MIXS, the stakes could hardly have been higher. The instrument spent its entire voyage sealed behind the Mercury Transfer Module, the propulsion stack that carried the two science orbiters across the solar system. Its optics could not see open space at all until September, when that transfer module finally separated. The Crab Nebula observations, made on 11 and 12 September, were the first chance to find out whether eight years of deep-space travel — of radiation, temperature swings and vibration — had damaged a delicate instrument that had last been tested in a basement laboratory in Leicester in 2015.

It had not. In fact, the telescope is performing slightly better in space than it did on the ground.

## Eight years blind

MIXS is not a camera in the ordinary sense. It is an X-ray spectrometer, designed to read the chemical fingerprints of Mercury's surface by catching the fluorescent X-rays that rocks emit when high-energy solar X-rays strike them. Different elements give off X-rays at characteristic energies, so by measuring the spectrum an instrument can work out what a planet's surface is made of — without ever touching it, landing on it, or collecting a single sample.

That is an elegant idea, and a demanding one. X-rays cannot be focused with an ordinary glass lens; they pass straight through it. MIXS uses a novel optic design, pioneered by Professor George Fraser of the University of Leicester, that reflects X-rays at grazing angles onto its detector. Around 80 per cent of the technology on MIXS is new, according to the mission team — a fact that makes the clean bill of health after eight years in space all the more remarkable.

The instrument was built by a consortium of institutes and companies across Europe, led by the University of Leicester. Partners include the Open University, the Rutherford Appleton Laboratory and Magna Parva in the United Kingdom; the University of Helsinki, the Finnish Meteorological Institute, Patria, SSF, Oxford Instruments and Beneq in Finland; INTA, CAB, Lidax and Crisa in Spain; the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research, MPG-HLL, the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics and PNSensor in Germany; and PHOTONIS, now Exosens, in France. Funding in the UK comes from the UK Space Agency as the lead agency, together with the Science and Technology Facilities Council, the European Space Agency and the University of Leicester.

But for all that engineering, MIXS travelled to Mercury effectively blind. The Mercury Transfer Module sat in front of its aperture for the whole cruise, and while engineers could monitor the instrument's electronics and temperatures throughout the journey, they could not test its optics against the sky. The first opportunity came only after the transfer module separated from the joined science orbiters — ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter, or MPO, and Japan's Mercury Magnetosphere Orbiter, known as Mio — on 3 September 2026, the start of BepiColombo's arrival phase.

With its view finally unobstructed, the MIXS team moved quickly. Within days they had pointed the telescope at the Crab Nebula and begun the calibration campaign that would tell them whether their instrument was still the one they had built.

## Why the Crab Nebula?

The choice of target was no accident. The Crab Nebula is the expanding remnant of a massive star that exploded and was recorded by skywatchers on Earth almost a millennium ago. At its heart spins a pulsar, a neutron star rotating furiously and pouring energy into the surrounding gas, which glows across the electromagnetic spectrum — including, brilliantly, in X-rays.

For X-ray astronomers, the Crab is the closest thing to a standard candle in the sky: a source so well studied, so bright and so stable that it serves as a calibration reference for telescopes across the world. X-ray observatories have relied on such reference sources for decades, and the same kind of high-energy astronomy recently turned up a very different surprise — a stellar-mass black hole hiding behind what looked like a failed supernova, as covered in <a href="/blog/at-2016blu-chandra-supernova-impostor-november-2026/">AT 2016blu Chandra Discovery November 2026: The Supernova Impostor Hiding a Black Hole</a>. By observing it, the MIXS team could measure exactly how their instrument behaves in space and compare the result, number for number, with the calibration measurements made with the same instrument in the laboratory in 2015.

The main observation sequence ran for 39 hours across 11 and 12 September. During that time the spacecraft performed 55 carefully controlled changes in orientation — slews — repositioning MIXS across the target in a series of 30-minute pointings at different offsets from the Crab, interrupted by pauses to unload the spacecraft's reaction wheels. Each frame of the resulting movie integrates one minute of data, and the composite image built from the whole campaign shows the Crab's X-ray glow overlaid on a visible-light background from the Digitized Sky Survey.

The key measurement was the telescope's angular resolution: how sharply it can distinguish fine detail. The team determined it by measuring how widely the Crab Nebula's X-ray signal spreads across the detector. A sharper instrument concentrates the light into a tighter spot; a damaged or misaligned one smears it out. MIXS, they found, concentrates the light slightly more tightly than it did in the laboratory in 2015.

That is an extraordinary result for an instrument that has spent eight years in the harshest environment in the solar system, and it is the single most important number to come out of the campaign. When MIXS turns to Mercury, its angular resolution will decide how finely it can map the planet's surface chemistry — how small a crater, a lava plain or a geological boundary it can resolve. A telescope that had lost sharpness in flight would have delivered a blurrier map of Mercury for the whole mission. Instead, the team now knows it will see the surface more crisply than the laboratory tests ever promised.

## A clean bill of health — with homework still to do

Professor Emma Bunce, the principal investigator for MIXS and director of the Institute for Space at the University of Leicester, welcomed the results as a major achievement for the international team that built the instrument.

"We have waited a long time to make measurements with MIXS and it is fantastic to see it operating brilliantly in space," Bunce said. "This is testament to our engineering and technical team, and our international partners who we have worked with over many years to build this incredible instrument. 80% of the technology on MIXS is new!"

The calibration campaign produced a detailed health report, and it is strikingly positive. The team found no effects in their images associated with stray light — optical photons leaking in where they should not. They continue to see the effects of galactic cosmic rays, as they did during cruise, but they understand those effects and can filter them out during data processing. They found a small amount of self-fluorescence — X-ray emission generated inside the instrument itself when sunlight illuminates its optic — but that will not affect dayside observations of Mercury's surface. And, most importantly, the detector has suffered no radiation damage after eight years in space.

"During our calibration observations we have determined that MIXS is generally performing fantastically well in space!" Bunce said. "We have no effects in our images that are associated with 'stray light' (optical photons getting in when they should not). We continue to see effects of galactic cosmic rays as we did during cruise, but we understand these and can filter them out. We see some self-fluorescence which appears when the Sun is illuminating the optic, but this won't affect our dayside observations of Mercury's surface. And the detector has suffered no radiation damage after 8 years in space."

Not everything is fully understood yet. The team is still studying some effects in the instrument and expects to carry out optimisation procedures during the Mercury commissioning phase, fine-tuning MIXS in preparation for the science phase. That is normal for a complex instrument arriving at its destination: calibration is a process, not a single test, and the Crab Nebula campaign was the first step rather than the last word.

But the headline is unambiguous. The first imaging X-ray telescope ever flown on a planetary science mission works — and works well — after the longest, hardest journey of its life.

## How X-rays will read Mercury's surface

Mercury is the least explored of the rocky planets, and one of the strangest. It is the smallest planet in the solar system, and it orbits closer to the Sun than any other world, enduring the most extreme conditions of any terrestrial planet. Yet despite its proximity, it remains surprisingly mysterious: scientists hope BepiColombo's data will improve our understanding of both the origin and evolution of a planet located close to its parent star, and of the overall evolution of the solar system itself.

One of the mission's major goals is to uncover Mercury's chemical composition and geological history. Those findings could help explain the planet's unusual characteristics — the puzzles that have lingered since NASA's MESSENGER spacecraft orbited Mercury from 2011 to 2015 — while providing broader insights into how planets form and change under extreme conditions close to a star.

MIXS sits at the centre of that effort. Carried aboard the Mercury Planetary Orbiter, it will work with the mission's other instruments to produce a global map of Mercury's surface atomic composition in unprecedented detail. It is the first imaging X-ray telescope to operate on a planetary science mission, and the only UK-led science instrument on BepiColombo.

The physics is straightforward in principle. When powerful X-rays from the Sun strike Mercury's surface rocks, they knock electrons out of the atoms in those rocks. As the atoms settle back down, they emit X-rays of their own at energies that are characteristic of each element — a fluorescent fingerprint. By collecting those fluorescent X-rays and sorting them by energy, MIXS can identify which elements are present, and in what proportions, across the surface below.

To do that accurately, the team also needs to know exactly what the Sun is doing at any given moment, because the fluorescent signal depends on the incoming solar X-rays. That is the job of the Solar Intensity X-ray and particle Spectrometer, known as SIXS, which monitors the solar radiation and particles reaching Mercury and lets the team correct the MIXS measurements accordingly.

MIXS will also look at Mercury's nightside, where a different process takes over. On the dark side of the planet, charged particles trapped in Mercury's magnetic environment rain down onto the surface and trigger X-ray emission of their own. Observing those nightside X-rays could reveal the connection between Mercury's surface and its surrounding space environment — the interaction between a rocky world and the magnetosphere that shields it, or fails to.

Together, those measurements will feed into the global composition map that is MIXS's main deliverable: a chemical atlas of an entire planet, built element by element, from orbit.

## The road to Mercury

The Crab Nebula first light is the latest milestone on a journey that began more than eight years ago. BepiColombo launched on 20 October 2018 aboard an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou in French Guiana, carrying the two science orbiters stacked together with the Mercury Transfer Module that would power them across the solar system.

The cruise was long and intricate. To reach Mercury, the spacecraft could not fly straight at its target; it had to shed enormous amounts of orbital energy, using nine planetary flybys — one of Earth, two of Venus and six of Mercury itself — together with its solar-electric ion engines to brake gently into the Sun's gravity well. Along the way, the mission covered roughly 10 billion kilometres.

The journey did not go entirely to plan. A power problem with the transfer module's solar arrays forced the mission onto a lower-thrust trajectory, pushing the arrival at Mercury from December 2025 to November 2026. The delay cost the mission a year, but it did not cost it the mission: the spacecraft, its instruments and its science goals all arrived intact.

The arrival phase began on 3 September 2026, when the Mercury Transfer Module separated from the spacecraft stack — the moment that also uncovered MIXS's aperture for the first time since launch. BepiColombo's twin orbiters are now due to enter orbit around Mercury on 21 November, the same day this first-light result was announced. The two science orbiters, MPO and Mio, are then expected to separate from each other on 9 and 10 December, and the mission's primary science phase is scheduled to begin in April 2027.

That timeline is the reason the Crab Nebula campaign mattered so much, and why it was run in September rather than later. Had the team waited until after orbit insertion to discover a problem with MIXS, there would have been little time — and few options — to fix it before the science phase began. By testing the instrument in September, while the spacecraft was still in its final approach, they bought themselves months to study the remaining effects and to plan the optimisation procedures that will run during the commissioning phase at Mercury.

## A twenty-year project, and a legacy

For the people behind MIXS, the first-light result is the payoff of careers, not just of a cruise phase. Dr Adrian Martindale, the MIXS instrument scientist at the University of Leicester's School of Physics and Astronomy, first became involved in the mission in 2003 as an undergraduate student. He has, by his own account, touched almost every part of the instrument before it was launched.

"BepiColombo, and MIXS in particular, are like part of my family," Martindale said. "I've been involved since I was an undergraduate and touched almost every part of the instrument before it was launched."

"This first data is mind-blowing to me. The last time we could see through this telescope it was in the basement under the School of Physics and Astronomy in Leicester, but after a rocket launch, eight years in space and 10 billion kilometres travelled it looks just the same. I can't wait to see the science data it produces when we get into orbit."

The milestone also carries a personal meaning for the team. MIXS is the lasting legacy of Professor George Fraser, the instrument's original principal investigator and the driving force behind its novel optic design, who died in March 2014, four years before the spacecraft launched. Bunce said the team had Fraser in mind as they operated MIXS fully in space for the first time.

"We are thinking of George as we operate MIXS fully in space for the first time and know that he would be very, very proud of the team and delighted at our first light observations," she said.

That thread — a design conceived by a scientist who never lived to see it fly, built by a team that included an undergraduate who stayed for more than two decades, tested against a dead star 6,500 light-years away — is part of what makes this first-light story more than a calibration report. Planetary missions are long enough that they span careers, and sometimes generations. MIXS was designed, built, launched and finally switched on by people whose professional lives are measured against its timeline.

## Why this calibration matters for all of BepiColombo

It is worth pausing on why a telescope looking at a nebula matters to a mission going to Mercury. The answer is that every planetary instrument carries the same risk: it is built and tested on Earth, then launched into conditions that cannot be fully reproduced in any laboratory, and then expected to work perfectly, years later, at a destination where no engineer can ever reach it to fix it.

For MIXS, that risk was unusually sharp. The instrument's aperture was covered for the entire eight-year cruise, which meant the first real test of its optics could only happen weeks before arrival. If the optics had degraded — if the detector had been damaged by radiation, if stray light had flooded the images, if the resolution had blurred — the team would have had almost no time to adapt their science plans. The Crab Nebula campaign was, in effect, the moment the mission learned whether one of its key instruments had survived the journey.

The answer is yes, and slightly better than expected. That matters beyond MIXS itself. BepiColombo's science depends on its instruments working together: MIXS's composition map gains meaning when combined with the mineralogy, topography, magnetic field and exosphere measurements of the mission's other instruments aboard MPO and Mio. A healthy MIXS means the mission's central scientific product — the most detailed chemical map of Mercury ever made — is on track.

There is also a broader lesson. MIXS is the first imaging X-ray telescope on a planetary mission, and its success in flight validates a technique — grazing-incidence X-ray optics for planetary surface mapping — that future missions to other airless worlds could reuse. The Moon, asteroids and the moons of the outer planets all have surfaces whose chemistry could be read the same way, by the same kind of fluorescent X-rays. The calibration campaign that MIXS has just completed is, in that sense, a rehearsal for a whole class of future exploration.

For now, though, the focus is Mercury. The spacecraft is entering orbit today, the orbiters separate in December, and the science phase begins in April 2027. When MIXS finally turns its sharpened gaze from a dead star 6,500 light-years away to the cratered plains of the innermost planet, it will be reading a world no X-ray telescope has ever mapped before — and doing it with an instrument that has already proved it can survive the journey there.
