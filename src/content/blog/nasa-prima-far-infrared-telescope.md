On Wednesday, 23 September 2026, NASA announced that a mission to explore the history and evolution of the universe — PRIMA, the PRobe far-Infrared Mission for Astrophysics — is advancing to the next phase of development. The decision makes PRIMA the first mission in an entirely new class of NASA astrophysics missions, called Probe Explorers, within the agency's longstanding Explorers Program.

"The PRIMA mission is humanity's next window into the deep universe," said Nicky Fox, associate administrator of NASA's Science Mission Directorate at the agency's Washington headquarters. "It will unveil the obscure across cosmic time to better understand the formation of planets, stars, black holes, and even how water on Earth came to be."

With the selection, PRIMA moves into Phase B, the stage of development that advances the preliminary design and technology work for the mission. It remains subject to a confirmation review — an assessment of technical, programmatic, and cost performance that will determine whether it is ready to begin implementation in Phase C. If confirmed, the project's cost is capped at $1.2 billion, not including launch and other non-project costs. The observatory is targeted to launch in 2033 for a planned five-year mission.

For astronomers, the news lands as the end of a long drought. PRIMA will observe the universe in far-infrared light, a band of the spectrum that has been effectively dark to space telescopes for more than a decade. In that darkness lie answers to some of the biggest questions in astrophysics: how the first galaxies assembled, how dust and heavy elements built up across cosmic time, and how the water in Earth's oceans arrived here in the first place.

## A telescope for the cold, dusty universe

At the heart of PRIMA is a 5.9-foot — 1.8-metre — telescope, cryogenically cooled so that its own heat does not drown out the faint signals it is built to detect. It will conduct deep, sensitive surveys of the sky in far-infrared light, spanning wavelengths from roughly 24 to 235 microns. That cooling is not a luxury; it is the entire game. Far-infrared detectors must operate at temperatures barely above absolute zero, because at these wavelengths, the thermal glow of a room-temperature telescope would outshine the distant galaxies it is trying to see.

One of the mission's key instruments is PRIMAger, an imaging polarimeter that will capture detailed images of astronomical objects while simultaneously measuring the polarization of their light. Polarization data opens a new dimension of information: it encodes the imprint of magnetic fields and scattering processes in cosmic environments, revealing the hidden architecture of star-forming clouds and galactic nuclei in ways that brightness alone cannot.

The choice of the far-infrared is deliberate and strategic. This is the wavelength range where cold cosmic dust shines brightest — and dust, for all its humble reputation, is one of the most important substances in the universe. The carbon in our bodies, the oxygen we breathe, the silicon in the rocks beneath our feet: all of it was forged in stars and scattered across space as dust. Far-infrared light is the direct signature of that material, glowing faintly as it absorbs starlight and re-radiates it at longer wavelengths.

## Bridging the gap between Webb and the radio sky

PRIMA's wavelength coverage places it in a scientifically precious gap. On one side sits the mid-infrared, now mapped in exquisite detail by NASA's James Webb Space Telescope. On the other lies the submillimetre and radio regime, probed by ground-based facilities such as ALMA in Chile. Between them stretches the far-infrared — a band that neither Webb's instruments nor radio telescopes can fully reach, and one that has been without a dedicated space observatory since the European Space Agency's Herschel Space Observatory ended operations in 2013, following NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope into retirement.

Herschel, operational from 2009 to 2013, was the last space observatory able to detect these longer wavelengths, and its legacy has only grown with time. Its surveys revealed that much of the universe's star formation is hidden behind dust, invisible to optical telescopes. Entire populations of galaxies — the dusty star-forming galaxies that built most of the stars in the cosmos — shine primarily in the far-infrared. Without a successor, astronomers have been studying this crucial population with aging data and indirect proxies for over a decade.

PRIMA is designed to end that drought decisively. By conducting deep, sensitive surveys rather than pointed observations of individual targets, it will map the far-infrared sky systematically, building the statistical samples that modern astrophysics demands. The mission's survey strategy echoes the approach that made missions like Kepler and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey so transformative: don't just look at interesting objects — count them, map them, and let the population tell its story.

## The questions PRIMA will answer

The science case for PRIMA spans nearly the full sweep of cosmic history. By studying radiant energy that only emerges in the far-infrared, the mission will address the origins of planets outside our solar system, how galaxies and their central black holes have grown and evolved over cosmic time, and how dust and heavy elements have accumulated in the universe — all helping to paint a better picture of why the universe looks the way it does today.

Consider the problem of galaxy evolution. The most vigorous star-forming galaxies in the universe's history are also the dustiest; the ultraviolet light from their newborn stars is absorbed by surrounding dust clouds and re-emitted in the far-infrared. An optical telescope sees these galaxies dimly or not at all. PRIMA will see them in their full glory, measuring their star-formation rates, dust masses, and gas content across billions of years of cosmic time. Combined with Webb's mid-infrared data and ALMA's submillimetre measurements, PRIMA's surveys will complete the multi-wavelength picture of how galaxies assembled.

Then there is the question of black holes. Nearly every massive galaxy hosts a supermassive black hole at its centre, and the growth of galaxies and their black holes appears to be intimately linked — but the details of that co-evolution remain murky, especially in the dust-enshrouded early universe. Far-infrared observations can peer through the obscuring dust to measure both star formation and black-hole activity in the same galaxies, disentangling two processes that are otherwise hopelessly confused.

And then there is water — the substance that makes Earth, Earth. The origin of Earth's water remains one of planetary science's great unsolved puzzles. Did it arrive aboard icy asteroids and comets, or was it locked into the planet's building blocks from the start? Far-infrared spectroscopy can trace water vapour and ice through the planet-forming disks around young stars, mapping the reservoirs of water at the epoch when planets are assembling. PRIMA's surveys of these disks, across hundreds of young stellar systems, will test competing theories of water delivery with statistical power that no previous mission could muster.
## A new class of mission

PRIMA's selection matters for a second reason beyond its science: it inaugurates the Probe Explorer line, a new tier in NASA's astrophysics portfolio. The structure of NASA's space science missions has long had a missing middle. At the small end sits the Explorers Program, which flies focused, relatively inexpensive missions on a regular cadence. At the large end stand the flagships — multi-billion-dollar observatories like the James Webb Space Telescope and the upcoming Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, each the product of decades of development.

Between those poles lies a gap: missions too ambitious for the Explorer cost caps but not requiring flagship-scale investment. The Probe Explorer class is designed to fill it, with individual missions capped near the billion-dollar threshold. The concept responds directly to the National Academies' 2020 Decadal Survey for astronomy and astrophysics, which recommended a new line of probe-class missions to maintain a balanced, resilient astrophysics programme — one that can pursue major scientific objectives without placing all of the agency's eggs in a single flagship basket.

Establishing Probe Explorers as a recurring line, rather than a one-off, also provides something the space science community has long sought: predictability. A regular cadence of probe-class announcements gives university laboratories and industrial contractors a stable planning horizon for developing the specialised detectors, cryogenic systems, and precision optics that missions like PRIMA require. It creates a pipeline of medium-scale opportunities that keeps technical teams and scientific expertise alive between flagship generations.

PRIMA, as the inaugural Probe Explorer, will therefore be watched closely not only for its science but as a proof of concept for the entire mission class. Its journey through Phase B, confirmation review, and — if all goes well — Phase C implementation will establish the template: the cost-control practices, the schedule discipline, and the management approach that future Probe Explorers will inherit.

## From selection to launch: the road ahead

NASA's mission lifecycle is deliberately gated, and PRIMA now faces its most consequential gates. Phase B, which the mission has just entered, covers preliminary design and technology development — the stage where engineering concepts are turned into buildable hardware designs, where remaining technology risks are retired, and where the cost and schedule estimates that will govern the rest of the programme are baselined.

The confirmation review that follows is the moment of truth. An independent assessment of technical readiness, programmatic realism, and cost performance will determine whether PRIMA proceeds to Phase C, the implementation stage where metal is cut and instruments are built. Missions can and do fail confirmation; the review exists precisely to prevent the agency from committing billions to a design that cannot deliver. For PRIMA's team, the years ahead will be an exercise in de-risking — proving, subsystem by subsystem, that a cryogenic far-infrared observatory can be built on a probe-class budget and schedule.

If confirmed, the project's cost is capped at $1.2 billion, excluding launch and other non-project costs. That figure deserves context: it is a ceiling, not a budget spent, and the exclusion of launch costs is standard practice — launch services are procured separately and their costs vary with the vehicle selected. The discipline of a hard cap, however, is real. It forces trades early, rewards simplicity, and keeps the mission's scope aligned with its resources.

The target launch date is 2033, with a planned five-year prime mission. Seven years from selection to launch may sound distant, but in the timescale of space observatories it is brisk — Webb took more than two decades from concept to launch. A 2033 launch would place PRIMA's survey operations in the mid-2030s, an era when the astronomical landscape will look markedly different from today's: Roman will be conducting its wide-field infrared surveys, next-generation ground-based observatories will be operating, and PRIMA's far-infrared data will slot into a rich multi-wavelength ecosystem.

## Who builds it

Management of the PRIMA mission sits with NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California, the centre behind some of the agency's most storied science missions, with contributions from Goddard Space Flight Center and Marshall Space Flight Center. The division of labour reflects the mission's technical demands: cryogenic systems, precision optics, and far-infrared detectors each draw on institutional expertise concentrated at different NASA centres.

The mission is also international in scope. The September announcement, updated on 24 September to reflect the current partnership roster, lists collaborators spanning France, Italy, Germany, Canada, South Korea, Japan, and the United Kingdom. International partnerships of this breadth are increasingly the norm for major space science missions: they distribute cost, bring specialised technical capabilities — European groups, for instance, carry deep heritage from Herschel in far-infrared instrumentation — and bind the world's space agencies into shared scientific enterprises. The updated partner list in the release underscores how these coalitions evolve even as a mission moves through its formulation phases.

## The water question, in depth

Of all the science PRIMA will pursue, the origin of Earth's water may be the most viscerally compelling — because it is, in a real sense, the story of us. Every ocean, every rainstorm, every cell in every living thing depends on water whose cosmic provenance remains debated.

The far-infrared is uniquely suited to this investigation because water, in its various forms, leaves fingerprints across this band. In the cold outer regions of planet-forming disks, water exists as ice coating dust grains; closer in, where temperatures rise, it sublimates into vapour. Far-infrared spectroscopy can distinguish these reservoirs and measure their abundances, effectively taking inventory of a young planetary system's water at the moment of planet assembly.

PRIMA will conduct this inventory not for one or two showcase systems but across statistical samples of young stars at different ages and in different environments. That statistical power is what elevates the investigation from suggestive to decisive. If water is consistently abundant in the planet-forming zones of disks, it supports models in which planets inherit their water locally. If it is scarce there but plentiful in the outer disk — the realm of icy asteroids and comets — it favours delivery scenarios, in which water arrives later via bombardment. Either answer rewrites textbooks; PRIMA is designed to deliver one.

## Dust: the universe's memory

There is a poetic symmetry in the fact that PRIMA studies dust to understand cosmic history, because dust is, quite literally, the universe's memory. Every heavy element — every atom heavier than helium — was forged in stars and scattered by stellar winds and supernova explosions. The dust grains drifting through interstellar space are the physical record of billions of years of stellar birth and death.

Far-infrared observations read that record directly. By measuring how dust emission varies across galaxies of different ages, masses, and environments, PRIMA will trace the buildup of heavy elements over cosmic time — the chemical enrichment history of the universe. This is the story of how a cosmos born as hydrogen and helium became one capable of producing rocky planets, organic chemistry, and eventually observers.

The mission's deep surveys will be particularly powerful for studying the era often called "cosmic noon" — roughly ten billion years ago, when star formation across the universe peaked. The galaxies of cosmic noon are dusty almost by definition; they are forming stars at furious rates, and much of that activity is hidden from optical view. Herschel gave astronomers their first systematic look at this population. PRIMA, with greater sensitivity and vastly larger survey areas, will turn that first look into a census.

## Why 2033 matters now

It is tempting to file a 2033 launch under "the distant future" and move on. That would be a mistake. The decisions made in the next few years — during Phase B and the confirmation review — will determine what PRIMA becomes. Detector technologies matured now, partnership agreements solidified now, cost reserves protected now: these are the unglamorous foundations on which the 2030s science will rest.

There is also a strategic dimension. The far-infrared gap has been open since 2013. Every year without a successor is a year in which questions about dusty galaxies, water delivery, and cosmic chemical evolution go unanswered — or are answered partially, through heroic efforts with ill-suited instruments. PRIMA's selection signals that NASA intends to close the gap deliberately and soon, in astronomical terms.

And the Probe Explorer precedent matters beyond PRIMA itself. If the mission navigates Phase B and confirmation successfully — on budget, on schedule, with its science intact — it validates an entire tier of the astrophysics programme. Future probe-class missions, whatever targets they pursue, will inherit both the management template and the political credibility that PRIMA establishes. The Decadal Survey's vision of a balanced programme, with flagships, probes, and explorers each playing their role, begins here.

## A new window, long awaited

For more than a decade, one of the richest bands of the electromagnetic spectrum has been dark to space astronomy. In that darkness, galaxies assembled, black holes grew, dust accumulated, and water found its way to newborn worlds — the processes that made the universe we inhabit. On 23 September 2026, NASA committed to turning the lights back on.

PRIMA will not launch until 2033. Its confirmation review still lies ahead, its instruments are not yet built, and the $1.2 billion cap will demand discipline at every step. But the direction of travel is now set: humanity's next window into the deep universe will open in the far-infrared, and through it, as Nicky Fox put it, we will unveil the obscure across cosmic time — the formation of planets, stars, and black holes, and the story of how water on Earth came to be.
