Some of the most violent objects in the universe leave almost no visible trace of the violence itself. They announce themselves in gamma rays, the most energetic form of light, yet the engine doing the work can remain hidden behind glare, distance and doubt. For a decade, one such engine has sat in a neighbouring galaxy, pouring out gamma rays at a rate that made it the most luminous binary of its kind ever found, while astronomers argued over what, exactly, was producing them. Now a trail of cold gas, 26 light-years long, may have given the argument its sharpest piece of evidence yet.

The trail was found not by photographing a jet, but by mapping what a jet would leave behind. Using the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) in Chile, a team led by researchers at Nagoya University and Gifu University in Japan traced carbon monoxide emission in the gas surrounding the system known as LMC P3. What emerged was a strikingly straight, narrow ribbon of molecular gas, roughly 26 light-years long and three light-years wide, pointing toward the binary. The researchers argue that the ribbon is the wake of an energetic outflow: gas compressed and warmed where a jet from the system's compact object ploughed into the surrounding interstellar medium. Their paper, published on October 30 in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, is titled "ALMA Observations of DEM L241/LMC P3 in the Large Magellanic Cloud: Evidence for the Formation of Cool Molecular Jets Driven by a Microquasar." The word "evidence" in that title matters, and the team does not hide it. This is a stronger case for one explanation, not a closing verdict on a ten-year mystery.

## A ribbon of cold gas, warmer than it should be

ALMA does not see the universe the way our eyes do. It detects millimetre and submillimetre radiation, the faint glow of cold molecules and dust that optical telescopes miss entirely. For this study, the team used ALMA's Bands 6 and 7, measuring carbon monoxide emission at wavelengths around 1.3 millimetres and 0.87 millimetres. Carbon monoxide is a useful tracer: where it shines, molecular gas is present, and the details of that glow reveal the gas's distribution, its motion and its physical conditions.

The campaign began in 2022, and the picture it built up was not a diffuse cloud but a structure with shape and direction. The carbon monoxide feature is about eight parsecs long and one parsec wide — roughly 26 light-years by three light-years — and it is notably straight and one-sided, extending away from the binary in a single direction rather than spreading in all directions. That geometry is itself a clue. A roughly spherical explosion or a broadly expanding shell would not be expected to leave so narrow and so linear a mark. A collimated outflow, a jet, would.

Temperature adds weight to the picture. The gas in the trail is estimated at roughly 33 to 60 kelvin, compared with about 15 kelvin in the neighbouring gas. In everyday terms both figures are staggeringly cold — tens of degrees above absolute zero, far colder than anything on Earth — but the contrast is what counts. Something has heated this ribbon to roughly two to four times the temperature of its surroundings, across a structure light-years long. The researchers' interpretation is that a high-energy jet from the compact object slammed into the surrounding cloud, driving shocks through it, compressing the gas and warming it. Lead author Yasuo Fukui, professor emeritus at Nagoya University, put it plainly: "We discovered a gigantic molecular jet."

There is also a hint of more structure to come. The paper describes a fainter possible second carbon monoxide feature, oriented slightly differently from the main trail. Whether it is connected to the same engine, or is an unrelated wrinkle in a complicated cloud, is not yet settled. It is the kind of loose thread that keeps an observational programme alive: a second feature could reveal a change in jet direction over time, a counter-jet, or simply the messiness of a real interstellar environment. Follow-up observations will test whether it belongs to the same story.

## What is LMC P3?

To understand why a cold gas trail matters, it helps to picture the system it points to. A gamma-ray binary is a pair of stars — one massive and luminous, the other compact and extreme — whose combined activity produces powerful gamma-ray emission. In LMC P3, the massive star is reported to weigh more than 30 times as much as the Sun, and the two objects circle each other roughly once every ten days. The compact companion is either a neutron star, the crushed remnant of a massive star, or a black hole. After a decade of study, astronomers still do not know which.

That uncertainty is not a minor detail. It is the heart of the puzzle. A neutron star and a black hole would power gamma rays in different ways, and the two leading explanations for LMC P3 map roughly onto those two identities. The system sits inside a supernova remnant called DEM L241, the expanding debris cloud left behind by the stellar explosion that created the compact object in the first place. In other words, LMC P3 lives inside the ashes of its own birth: a young, violent system still surrounded by the material its progenitor star threw off when it died.

LMC P3 came to prominence in 2016, when astronomers investigating bright gamma-ray emission from beyond the Milky Way identified it as an exceptionally luminous source. It was the first gamma-ray binary discovered in another galaxy, and it remains the most luminous known in gamma rays, X-rays, radio waves and visible light. That superlative is part of what makes it so tantalising — and so demanding. Whatever powers LMC P3 is operating at an extraordinary level, and any explanation has to account for that output, not merely for the fact that gamma rays are present at all.

The Large Magellanic Cloud itself is part of the appeal. At roughly 163,000 light-years away, it is a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way, close on cosmic scales and offering astronomers a relatively clear view of star-forming regions, supernova remnants and high-energy sources. Its gas and stars are less obscured by the dust and crowding that complicate views through the plane of our own galaxy. A system like LMC P3, studied in the Large Magellanic Cloud, can therefore be examined in context: its surroundings, its remnant and its neighbouring clouds are all visible, and that context is precisely what the ALMA study exploits.

## Two engines, one mystery

The debate over LMC P3 comes down to two competing engines, and the new result speaks to both. In the first picture, the pulsar-wind scenario, the compact object is a rapidly spinning neutron star — a pulsar — whose powerful wind of particles collides with the wind streaming off its massive companion. The collision creates a shock front, and in that shock, particles are accelerated to extreme energies. Those particles, in turn, can produce the gamma rays astronomers detect. It is a violent but essentially wind-driven explanation: two outflows meet, and the crash between them lights up the system.

In the second picture, the microquasar scenario, gas from the massive star feeds an accretion disk swirling around the compact object, and that disk launches jets — narrow, high-speed streams of material fired outward along the disk's poles. Those jets, interacting with nearby clouds, can drive shocks and accelerate particles, producing gamma rays and leaving marks on the surrounding gas. A microquasar is, in effect, a scaled-down quasar: the same basic machinery — accretion plus jets — that powers the brilliant cores of distant galaxies, operating in a single star system rather than around a supermassive black hole.

The molecular trail lends weight to the second picture. A wind collision would be expected to produce a more localised, more symmetric interaction region near the binary, where the two winds meet. A jet, by contrast, travels far beyond the binary itself, and its passage through a cloud should leave exactly the kind of long, straight, heated feature ALMA found. Fukui has described the relatively high temperature observed over a large fraction of the molecular cloud as "a very strong case" that the gas is interacting with a high-energy jet coming from the compact object — the same kind of jet that could power LMC P3's gamma rays.

That is a meaningful shift in the balance of evidence, but the researchers and the reporting around the result are careful about its limits. The trail strengthens the microquasar hypothesis; it does not by itself eliminate the pulsar-wind alternative, and it does not disclose the identity of the compact object. A jet-like signature is not the same thing as a confirmed black hole. Neutron stars, too, can launch jets under the right conditions. What the observation shows is a structure consistent with a jet's wake. The engine behind it still has to be pinned down.

## A wake, not a photograph

There is an important distinction at the centre of this story, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to lose in a headline. ALMA did not film a near-light-speed jet streaming out of LMC P3. No telescope has done that. What ALMA measured was molecular gas: cold, carbon-monoxide-bearing material whose temperature, shape and alignment are consistent with having been struck and shaped by an energetic outflow. The jet itself is inferred, not directly imaged in motion. The physical account connecting the gas trail to a relativistic jet is a model, supported by the trail's geometry and temperature, not a direct measurement of jet velocity.

That distinction does not weaken the result. In astronomy, indirect evidence of this kind is often the only evidence available, and it can be powerful when it is specific. A ship's wake is not a photograph of a ship, but a long, straight, warm wake in an otherwise calm sea tells you a great deal about what passed through it: its direction, its scale and something about its energy. The same logic applies here. The trail is narrow rather than broad, straight rather than tangled, warm rather than ambient, and it points at LMC P3. Each of those properties narrows the range of explanations, and together they make the jet interpretation difficult to dismiss.

The approach also illustrates a growing habit in modern astronomy: studying not only an energetic object, but what it does to its surroundings. A jet moving through an interstellar cloud drives shocks, changes the gas temperature, compresses material and leaves patterns that can remain observable long after the fastest particles have passed. The gas becomes a recording medium, preserving a physical record of an encounter that would otherwise be invisible. In LMC P3, that record spans dozens of light-years, written in gas only a few dozen degrees above absolute zero, and it may be the most durable evidence yet of an engine that otherwise reveals itself only in fleeting, high-energy light.

## Why the temperature tells a story

It is worth pausing on the temperature measurement, because it is the least intuitive part of the finding and the most informative. Fifteen kelvin is extraordinarily cold — about minus 258 degrees Celsius — and it is typical of the undisturbed molecular clouds from which stars form. Such clouds are dark, dense by interstellar standards and quiet, and they persist for millions of years unless something disturbs them. Raising a large fraction of such a cloud to 33 to 60 kelvin, across a structure 26 light-years long, requires a substantial and sustained input of energy.

That is why the researchers treat the warmth as evidence of interaction rather than coincidence. A chance alignment between a binary and a slightly warmer patch of cloud is conceivable. A chance alignment that is also straight, narrow, one-sided and pointed at one of the most luminous gamma-ray binaries known is much harder to accept. The temperature contrast, combined with the geometry, is what turns a curious feature into a case. It is also why the team emphasises the extent of the heating: it is not a single warm spot, but warmth spread over a large fraction of the cloud, consistent with a jet sweeping through it rather than a brief, localised flare.

There are, of course, other ways to heat interstellar gas. Radiation from hot stars, supernova shocks and turbulence within clouds all play a role in the interstellar medium, and any careful analysis has to consider them. The strength of the jet case rests on the combination of properties rather than any single one: the straightness, the narrowness, the one-sidedness, the alignment with LMC P3 and the warmth, all together, in a system already known to be an extreme gamma-ray source. No single property proves a jet. The pattern, taken as a whole, points toward one.

## A familiar comparison: SS 433

Astronomers trying to make sense of LMC P3 often reach for a comparison closer to home: SS 433, a long-studied system in our own galaxy. SS 433 is a microquasar in the Milky Way, a binary whose compact object launches energetic jets into a complex surrounding nebula. It helped establish that compact objects in stellar systems can produce jet phenomena reminiscent of the far larger jets fired by supermassive black holes in galactic nuclei. The Japanese universities behind the new study compare the energy implied for LMC P3 with powerful microquasar systems such as SS 433, and the comparison is instructive — but it comes with its own caution.

SS 433 and LMC P3 are not identical objects, and no comparable jet speed has been measured in LMC P3. The comparison works at the level of mechanism: if a stellar-mass compact object in our galaxy can drive jets energetic enough to reshape its surroundings, then a similar mechanism in LMC P3 is physically plausible, and it can plausibly account for a large share of the system's extreme output. What the comparison does not do is settle the details. The mass of the compact object, the rate at which it feeds, the speed of any jet and the history of its activity all remain to be constrained by further observation.

That is a recurring theme in the study of microquasars. They are laboratories for extreme physics, but small ones, and their most interesting processes happen on scales that telescopes cannot always resolve. SS 433 has been studied for decades and still holds surprises. LMC P3, discovered only in 2016 and observed with ALMA only since 2022, is at a much earlier stage of its investigation. The new trail gives astronomers a new handle on the system — a large, measurable structure rather than a point of light — and that handle may prove more valuable than any single measurement, because it opens the system up to the tools of cloud physics as well as high-energy astrophysics.

## What the trail does not prove

Good science is as much about stating limits as about announcing results, and the limits here are clear. First, the compact object's identity remains unresolved. The trail supports a jet-driven explanation, but it does not tell astronomers whether the jet comes from a black hole or a neutron star. That question will require other kinds of evidence, quite possibly across many wavelengths, and it may remain open for years.

Second, the jet's own properties — its speed, its power, its history — are not yet measured. The trail records that something energetic passed through the gas, but reconstructing the jet from its wake is an exercise in inference. How long ago did the interaction happen? Has the jet been steady, or does it switch on and off? Does the fainter second carbon monoxide feature represent a change in direction, a counter-jet, or something unrelated? Each of these questions points to a different follow-up observation: more detailed mapping of the gas's geometry and velocity field, searches for high-energy counterparts, and multi-wavelength campaigns that can test whether the same engine explains the gamma rays and the gas trail together.

Third, the broader population question remains. Gamma-ray binaries are rare, and the first one found in another galaxy turning out to be the most luminous known raises its own questions. Is LMC P3 exceptional because it is genuinely extreme, or because the most luminous systems are the easiest to spot at intergalactic distances? The answer matters for understanding how common these engines are and what they contribute to the universe's supply of accelerated particles. A single object, however dramatic, cannot settle a population question. But a single object studied in unusual depth — its remnant, its cloud, its wake — can show what to look for in the next ones.

## Why a neighbouring galaxy is the right place to look

There is a final reason this result matters beyond LMC P3 itself, and it concerns where the system sits. The Large Magellanic Cloud is close enough that its star-forming regions, remnants and high-energy sources can be studied in detail, yet it is a genuinely different galactic environment from the Milky Way, with its own history of star formation and its own population of massive stars. Findings there test whether the physics astronomers work out in our galaxy holds up elsewhere, or whether different environments produce different outcomes.

The <a href="/blog/webb-n159-star-forming-region-large-magellanic-cloud/">Webb N159 star-forming region in the Large Magellanic Cloud</a> offers a useful parallel: a turbulent stellar nursery in the same galaxy, imaged in detail by the James Webb Space Telescope, showing stars at several stages of birth at once. The LMC P3 result belongs to the same tradition of using a nearby galaxy as a laboratory, but at the opposite end of the stellar life cycle — not stars being born, but the violent aftermath of a star's death, still shaping its surroundings. Together, they illustrate why the Large Magellanic Cloud remains one of astronomy's most productive hunting grounds: it is near enough to see clearly, and different enough to surprise.

The same logic connects LMC P3 to the study of jets elsewhere. The <a href="/blog/3c-120-black-hole-jet-2026-xrism-hot-inner-flow/">3C 120 black hole jet studied by XRISM</a> shows how a rapidly feeding supermassive black hole can launch a powerful relativistic jet from a hot inner flow, while the <a href="/blog/black-hole-solar-storm-ngc-3783-neupert-effect/">solar-storm-like flare around the black hole in NGC 3783</a> shows magnetic reconnection at work in a black hole's corona. Those systems operate on vastly larger scales than LMC P3, and their jets are powered by black holes millions or billions of times more massive than the Sun. But the underlying physics — accretion, magnetic fields, shocks, particle acceleration — rhymes across scales. A jet's wake in a cold cloud 163,000 light-years away and a flare in a distant galaxy's core are different chapters of the same story: how gravity and magnetism together turn infalling matter into some of the most energetic phenomena in the universe.

## The team and the paper

The study was led by Yasuo Fukui of Nagoya University, with collaborators including Bhuvana Gadikere Rajendra, Hidetoshi Sano and Kisetsu Tsuge at Gifu University, alongside an international research team. The formal announcements identify Fukui as professor emeritus, Sano as associate professor, Rajendra as researcher and Tsuge as designated assistant professor. The work draws together two observational communities that do not always overlap: researchers probing particle acceleration and high-energy emission, and researchers mapping the molecular clouds from which stars form. That bridging is part of the result's value. The gamma-ray puzzle pointed astronomers at LMC P3; the molecular-gas measurements gave them a new way to attack it.

The ALMA campaign itself is a reminder of how long modern astronomy takes. Observations began in 2022, four years before the paper's publication in October 2026, and the system had already been known for six years before that. A decade of inquiry, from the 2016 identification to the 2026 trail, has moved the question from "what is this brilliant gamma-ray source?" to "what kind of engine leaves a 26-light-year wake in cold gas?" That is progress of a particular kind: not a sudden revelation, but a slow narrowing of possibilities, each new observation ruling out some explanations and strengthening others.

It is also a reminder of the role international facilities play. ALMA, in the high Atacama Desert of Chile, is operated by an international partnership, and its array of antennas provides the angular resolution and sensitivity needed to pick out thin gas structures in another galaxy. Japanese participation in ALMA, through the Nagoya and Gifu teams, made this study possible, and the result is a small illustration of how a facility built for one purpose — mapping cold gas and dust — can end up illuminating one of the hottest questions in high-energy astrophysics.

## What comes next

The immediate next steps are observational. Astronomers will want a more detailed map of the trail's geometry and velocity field: is the gas moving in a way consistent with a jet-driven shock, and does its motion point back to LMC P3 along the trail's axis? They will want to test whether the fainter second feature is connected to the same engine, and to search for high-energy counterparts that link the gas structure to the gamma-ray emission directly. Multi-wavelength observations — radio, X-ray and gamma-ray data taken together — will be essential for distinguishing a pulsar wind from an accretion-powered jet, since the two models predict different patterns across the spectrum.

Longer term, the result sets a template. If a microquasar jet can leave a 26-light-year molecular wake in the Large Magellanic Cloud, similar wakes may be detectable around other gamma-ray binaries, including ones whose jets are too faint or too distant to observe directly. Searching for those wakes would turn a single discovery into a method: a way of finding and characterising jets not by their light, but by their footprints. That would be a significant expansion of the toolkit, particularly for systems in other galaxies, where direct imaging of a jet is hardest.

There is also the deeper question the trail raises about energy. The gamma rays from LMC P3 imply particles accelerated to extraordinary energies, and jets are among the few mechanisms known that can do that job in a stellar system. Understanding how efficiently a microquasar converts infalling matter into a jet, and how much of that jet's energy ends up in accelerated particles versus heated gas, bears on questions far beyond one binary — including the origin of some of the universe's high-energy cosmic rays. LMC P3 will not answer those questions alone. But it has just become one of the best places to ask them.

## A cold clue to a hot mystery

Strip away the technical language, and the discovery has a pleasing symmetry. To investigate one of the brightest gamma-ray binaries known, astronomers studied gas only a few dozen degrees above absolute zero. To find evidence of a jet moving at a substantial fraction of the speed of light, they mapped material so cold and so slow that it would seem, at first glance, to have nothing to do with high-energy astrophysics at all. The contrast is the point. The universe does not keep its phenomena in separate boxes: the hottest engines leave marks in the coldest matter, and the most violent events write their records in the quietest places.

For ten years, LMC P3 has been a brilliant, unresolved question mark in a neighbouring galaxy — a system pouring out gamma rays while astronomers debated whether a spinning neutron star's wind or a black hole-like engine's jets were responsible. A ribbon of warmed molecular gas, 26 light-years long and pointing straight at the binary, has now tipped that debate. It has not ended it. The compact object remains unidentified, the jet's speed unmeasured, and a fainter second feature unexplained. But the debate is no longer only about light from a point in the sky. It is about a physical structure, measured and mapped, with a length, a width, a temperature and a direction. The next time astronomers argue over what powers LMC P3, they will have to account for the trail — and that, in science, is what progress looks like.
