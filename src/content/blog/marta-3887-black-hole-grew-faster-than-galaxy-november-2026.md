Twelve billion years ago, when the universe was less than a quarter of its present age, a small, clumpy galaxy was already breaking the rules. Its stars added up to only about 145 million Suns — a lightweight by galactic standards — yet at its centre sat a black hole weighing roughly 3.3 million Suns. That makes the black hole about 2.3 percent of its galaxy's entire stellar mass, a proportion roughly 100 times higher than the relationship seen in nearby galaxies. In the local universe, black holes and their host galaxies grow in step, keeping a fairly steady ratio. In MARTA-3887, the black hole appears to have raced ahead.

The finding, reported in a paper posted to the arXiv preprint server in November 2026, comes from a team led by Mirko Curti of the INAF–Astrophysics and Space Science Observatory of Bologna. Combining spectroscopy from the James Webb Space Telescope with imaging from the Hubble Space Telescope and archival data from the Very Large Telescope's MUSE instrument, the researchers argue that this distant galaxy "possibly" hosts an overmassive black hole — one whose growth outpaced the assembly of the stars around it. The word "possibly" matters, and the team is careful about it. A supernova interacting with dense gas, or a compact and thick outflow, could in principle mimic the signal. But after weighing several lines of evidence, the authors conclude that a feeding black hole is the best explanation, and that the system extends the population of individually detected, actively feeding black holes into a fainter, more metal-poor, lower-mass regime than before.

It is a small object telling a large story. How the first massive black holes formed remains one of the open questions in astronomy, and the puzzle has only sharpened since Webb began operating. Black holes weighing about a billion Suns have been found within the first billion years of the universe, and they require extraordinary origin stories. Did they grow from the collapsed remnants of the first stars, feeding furiously for hundreds of millions of years? Did they begin life as far heavier "direct collapse" seeds, born when giant clouds of gas fell straight into black holes without ever forming stars? Or did something else entirely happen in the early universe? MARTA-3887 does not settle that debate. What it does is add a new kind of data point: not another giant, but a comparatively modest black hole in a small, poor galaxy — exactly the kind of system that is hardest to detect, and therefore the kind that may tell astronomers the most about how typical black holes actually grew.

## A black hole out of proportion

The galaxy at the centre of the story is MARTA-3887, seen at a redshift of 3.7694 — meaning its light has been travelling for roughly 12 billion years to reach us. It is compact and clumpy, low in heavy elements at about 10 percent of the Sun's level, and it is forming stars fast, at roughly five to nine solar masses of new stars per year, mostly in a recent burst. In other words, it looks like a young galaxy in the middle of a growth spurt. What makes it remarkable is not its stars but what appears to sit among them.

Using Webb's spectroscopic observations, Curti and colleagues detected a wide hydrogen-alpha line — light emitted by hydrogen gas — which revealed gas moving at about 1,270 kilometres per second, or roughly 790 miles per second. Crucially, the signal appeared only in hydrogen, not in other kinds of lines such as those from oxygen that come from calmer gas. That pattern pointed to a black hole rather than an ordinary outflow. Gas whipping around a compact, massive object at more than a thousand kilometres per second is the signature of material caught in a deep gravitational well; a wind blowing out of a star-forming region would be expected to show up in the oxygen lines too.

From the width of that line, the team estimated the black hole's mass: roughly 3.3 million times the mass of the Sun. That places it among the lower-mass black holes found by Webb so far — a long way from the billion-solar-mass giants that dominate the headlines — and that is precisely what makes it interesting. The galaxy, by contrast, holds only about 145 million solar masses of stars. Divide one by the other and the black hole is roughly 2.3 percent of the stellar mass. The researchers note that this relation is "approximately 2 dex above the local relation," which means it is about 100 times higher than the relationship extrapolated from nearby galaxies. In the team's own words, "black-hole growth has outpaced the assembly of the observed stellar component."

That single sentence captures the central tension. In the nearby universe, the masses of supermassive black holes track the masses of their host galaxies' central bulges with surprising tightness — a correlation so consistent that astronomers have spent decades arguing over whether black holes regulate galaxy growth, galaxies regulate black hole growth, or both are fed by the same gas supply. Whatever the mechanism, the two grow together. MARTA-3887, if the interpretation holds, is a galaxy where that partnership broke down early, with the black hole taking a commanding lead while its host was still small, poor in metals, and only just hitting its stride in star formation.

## How astronomers weighed a black hole 12 billion years away

No telescope can photograph a black hole at a redshift of 3.7694. At that distance, the galaxy itself is a smudge of light, and the black hole is far smaller than a single pixel. Astronomers therefore weigh such objects indirectly, by watching how gas moves around them. The method is the same in spirit as the one used to weigh the black hole at the centre of our own Milky Way, where decades of tracking stars orbiting Sagittarius A* pinned down its mass — except that for a galaxy 12 billion light-years away, the "stars" are replaced by glowing gas, and the measurement is made from a spectrum rather than from images taken year after year.

The key instrument was Webb's spectrograph, which spreads the galaxy's light into its component colours. When gas orbits a massive object, some of it moves toward us and some away, stretching and compressing the light it emits. The result is a spectral line that is broadened — smeared across a range of wavelengths — by an amount that reflects the speed of the gas. The broader the line, the faster the gas, and the heavier the object it orbits. In MARTA-3887, the hydrogen-alpha line was wide enough to imply gas moving at about 1,270 kilometres per second. For comparison, Earth orbits the Sun at about 30 kilometres per second. Gas moving more than 40 times faster than that, in a galaxy only a few thousand light-years across, needs something very massive and very compact at the centre to hold it in orbit.

Hubble imaging and archival data from the Very Large Telescope's MUSE instrument provided the supporting evidence. Hubble's sharper view of the galaxy's shape helped the team characterise it as compact and clumpy, while the MUSE data — an integral-field spectrograph that records a spectrum at every point in an image — added archival context to the analysis. It was the combination of instruments, rather than any single observation, that let the team build its case: Webb supplied the crucial spectral line, Hubble supplied the morphology, and MUSE supplied supporting spectroscopy. That multiwavelength approach is also what separates this study from a one-line detection. The authors used many lines of evidence rather than one, precisely because the alternatives — a supernova, an outflow, an extreme star — can each fake parts of the signal, but struggle to fake all of it at once.

The weighing technique has a long pedigree, and it is worth noting how it differs from other ways of measuring black holes. In the nearby universe, astronomers can track individual stars as they swing around a galactic centre, as was done for the Milky Way and, more recently, for the intermediate-mass black hole candidate in the hidden galaxy IC 342. SpaceNews covered that measurement in <a href="/blog/ic-342-intermediate-mass-black-hole-november-2026/">IC 342 Intermediate-Mass Black Hole: Keck Weighs a 195,000-Sun Monster in the Hidden Galaxy</a>, where the Keck Observatory measured the motions of stars swirling at the galaxy's heart. For MARTA-3887, no individual star can be resolved, so the gas itself becomes the tracer. The principle is identical — motion reveals mass — but the tracer is a glowing cloud rather than a point of light, and the uncertainty is larger. That is one reason the authors present their result as a candidate rather than a certainty.

## Three ways to grow a black hole

Finding an overmassive black hole is only half the puzzle. The other half is explaining how it got that way. Curti's team explored the question by building simple growth trackers — models that assume one black hole grows steadily from a seed, with no mergers, at a constant feeding rate, with each track set to end at the measured mass at the time the galaxy was observed. The exercise is deliberately simple: it is not a simulation of a real galaxy, but a way of asking what starting conditions the observed endpoint requires.

The answer depends on when growth began. If black hole growth started very early, when the universe was about 0.1 billion years old — roughly 100 million years after the Big Bang — then seeds with masses of 100, 10,000, and 100,000 Suns would need to feed at about 34 percent, 19 percent, and 11 percent of the maximum rate, respectively. The "maximum rate" here is the Eddington limit, the point at which the radiation pressure from infalling gas balances gravity and pushes further gas away. Feeding at a tenth to a third of that limit, sustained for billions of years, is demanding but not impossible. It is the kind of steady, efficient growth that astronomers can at least imagine happening in a gas-rich young galaxy.

The picture changes sharply if growth began late. If the black hole only started growing with the galaxy's recent star-formation burst, then a 100-solar-mass seed — the kind left behind by the death of a massive first star — would need to feed at about 570 percent of the maximum rate to reach 3.3 million solar masses in the time available. That is extreme. Feeding at nearly six times the Eddington limit, a regime called super-Eddington growth, is not forbidden by physics — radiation can escape through channels while gas continues to fall in — but sustaining it long enough to grow a black hole by a factor of tens of thousands strains credibility. A more massive initial seed, on the other hand, could grow more slowly and still reach the observed mass, which is why the authors note that the late-start scenario favours a heavier birth mass.

This is where MARTA-3887 connects to the wider debate about black hole seeds. If black holes in the early universe began as the remnants of the first stars, they started small — tens to hundreds of solar masses — and needed either very early starts, very sustained feeding, or episodes of super-Eddington growth to reach millions or billions of solar masses. If instead they began as "heavy seeds" of tens of thousands of solar masses, formed when a massive gas cloud collapsed directly into a black hole without ever forming a star, then the growth problem becomes much easier. Webb's "little red dots" — compact, red, distant sources that may be newborn supermassive black holes hidden inside the gas disks that formed them — are one of the leading candidates for that heavy-seed channel, and SpaceNews examined that research in <a href="/blog/jwst-little-red-dots-november-2026-direct-collapse-black-holes/">JWST Little Red Dots November 2026: The First Supermassive Black Holes Explained</a>. MARTA-3887, with its modest mass and its requirement for either an early start or a heavy seed, adds a data point that sits between the two pictures: too massive to be explained by lazy growth from a small seed, but far too light to be one of the billion-solar-mass monsters.

The growth tracks also carry a quiet warning about how astronomers read the early universe. Surveys can only detect black holes that are bright enough, or fast-moving enough, to stand out. That means the samples are biased toward the heaviest black holes for a given galaxy mass — the tip of the iceberg, as the researchers put it. To really understand how black holes form and grow, astronomers need to look for the smaller, more typical ones too. But those are also hard to detect, which is why a 3.3-million-solar-mass black hole in a 145-million-solar-mass galaxy is a valuable catch: it is about as small and as faint as the current generation of observations can reach, and it suggests that the population of overmassive black holes may extend much further down the mass scale than the headline giants imply.

## How MARTA-3887 compares

| System | Black hole mass | Host stellar mass | Black hole share | Status |

| MARTA-3887 (z = 3.77) | About 3.3 million Suns | About 145 million Suns | About 2.3% | Candidate, reported Nov 2026 |
| Local relation (implied) | Tens of thousands of Suns for a galaxy this size | 145 million Suns | About 0.02% | Derived from the reported ~100× offset |
| UHZ1 (z = 10.05), original claim | 10–100 million Suns | Comparable to all stars in the galaxy | Up to ~100% | Claimed 2023, now challenged |
| UHZ1, reanalysis (2026) | No compelling evidence for a luminous buried black hole | About 100 million Suns | Not required | ApJ, October 2026 |

The comparison table makes two points. First, MARTA-3887 is genuinely unusual: a black hole share of 2.3 percent against a local expectation of roughly 0.02 percent is a hundredfold excess, and it is an excess measured in a small galaxy, not a giant one. Second, the field is still arguing about how much to trust the most extreme claims. UHZ1, a galaxy seen when the universe was around 480 million years old, made news three years ago for hosting an unusually massive and heavily dust-obscured black hole, possibly formed through direct collapse, with an estimated mass of 10 million to 100 million solar masses — comparable to the mass of all the stars in the whole galaxy. A reanalysis led by Fan Zou of the University of Michigan, published in The Astrophysical Journal on October 14, 2026, found no signs of hidden black hole activity. The maximum X-ray signal allowed by the data turned out to be 10 to 1,000 times lower than the original X-ray claim required, and the infrared data ruled out a black hole as powerful as proposed. The team's conclusion was blunt: "no compelling evidence for such an AGN exists," and UHZ1 could instead be a low-mass, metal-poor, star-forming galaxy of around 100 million solar masses in stars, with no black hole required to explain it.

That self-correction is not a failure of the field. It is how the field works, and it is the reason the MARTA-3887 team hedges its own claim. Extraordinary claims need stronger proof than the data can yet supply, and a single observation — however rich — cannot rule out every alternative. The difference between MARTA-3887 and UHZ1 is instructive: the MARTA-3887 claim is more modest, rests on several independent lines of evidence rather than a single X-ray excess, and is presented as a candidate. It is the kind of result that invites follow-up rather than demanding belief.

## The case for caution

The authors are careful to note that this galaxy "possibly" hosts a black hole. An interacting supernova — a massive star exploding into dense gas — or a compact and thick outflow could still mimic the signal, though the team regards those explanations as less likely. They cannot rule out these scenarios from data gathered during a single observation, and they say so plainly.

The caution is warranted because the early universe is full of impostors. The signals from supernovae exploding into dense gas, shocks, and outflows can mimic faint black holes. In young, metal-poor galaxies, the spectral line ratios used to differentiate black hole-powered gas from star-powered gas may overlap, and extreme stars can mimic an active galactic nucleus — a galaxy powered by a massive black hole. MARTA-3887 is exactly the kind of galaxy where those confusions are worst: young, clumpy, metal-poor, and in the middle of a star-formation burst. A starburst produces supernovae, winds, and shocks in abundance, and each of those can stir gas to high speeds and light it up in hydrogen.

What tips the balance toward a black hole is the combination of features rather than any one of them. The broad hydrogen line is there, the oxygen lines from calmer gas are not, the velocity is high, and the inferred mass sits in a range that is consistent with a feeding black hole but hard to explain with a single supernova or a wind. No one of those facts is decisive on its own. Together, they form a coherent picture — but a picture that needs a second observation to confirm. Deeper spectroscopy, ideally at higher resolution and over a longer baseline, could test whether the broad line persists, whether it varies as a feeding black hole's emission should, and whether the alternative explanations can be excluded more firmly. Until then, MARTA-3887 remains a candidate: a strong one, by the authors' account, but a candidate nonetheless.

That honesty about uncertainty is part of what makes the paper useful. Astronomy's early-universe literature is moving fast — Webb has been returning data for only a few years — and the most durable results are often the ones that state their limits clearly. A paper that says "possibly" and then explains exactly what would settle the question gives the next team a clear target. A paper that overclaims forces the field to spend its time on rebuttals instead.

## A possible leaker

There is a second thread to the MARTA-3887 story, and it reaches beyond black holes altogether. The galaxy may also be leaking ultraviolet light. Some of the high-energy ultraviolet photons from early galaxies had to escape into space to help clear the neutral hydrogen fog of the early universe during the period of cosmic reionization — the era, hundreds of millions of years after the Big Bang, when the first stars and galaxies ionised the gas around them and made the universe transparent to light. For that to happen, galaxies needed porous gas through which such light could escape. Several signs suggest MARTA-3887 has exactly that kind of porous gas.

About 44 percent of the Lyman-alpha light produced by the galaxy escapes, which is a high fraction. Lyman-alpha is a particular wavelength of ultraviolet light emitted by hydrogen, and it is easily trapped: a single neutral hydrogen atom can scatter it, so a high escape fraction means the gas along our line of sight must be thin, clumpy, or full of holes. Because the team cannot yet measure the escape fraction of the ultraviolet photons themselves — the ionising photons that actually drive reionization — they call MARTA-3887 an indirect "leaker candidate" rather than a confirmed leaker. The distinction is honest, but the hint is tantalising: a small, clumpy, metal-poor galaxy, forming stars in a burst, with a feeding black hole at its centre and holes in its gas through which light can escape, is a plausible portrait of the kind of object that helped reionise the universe.

That connection is one reason the discovery matters beyond black hole physics. The galaxies that reionised the universe were not the giants. The giants came later, built up by mergers over billions of years. The reionisers were more likely small, numerous, and messy — galaxies like MARTA-3887, seen 12 billion years ago, still in the first flush of star formation. If such galaxies also hosted black holes that grew faster than their stars, then the black holes may have played a role in shaping them: heating gas, driving winds, punching holes through which radiation could escape. The physics is speculative, and the paper does not claim more than it can support. But the combination — an overmassive black hole and a possible leaker in the same small galaxy — is the kind of coincidence that makes astronomers pay attention.

## Why the overmassive problem matters

Step back, and MARTA-3887 is one more entry in a pattern that has defined Webb's first years: the early universe keeps turning up black holes that look too big for their galaxies. Many recent studies have revealed early black holes with a different relationship to their host galaxies compared with what is seen in the nearby universe. Early black holes are too heavy compared with their host galaxies' stellar masses, which suggests that the black holes in the early universe may grow quicker than the stars. The question is whether that pattern is real, or whether it is an artefact of what telescopes can see.

The detection bias is real and acknowledged. Surveys can only detect black holes that are bright or fast-moving enough, so the samples have the heaviest ones for a given galaxy mass. Those giant black holes are only the tip of the iceberg. If the only black holes astronomers could find were the brightest ones, then of course the early universe would look as though it were full of overmassive monsters. The test of that idea is to push the detection limit downward — to find smaller black holes in smaller galaxies — and see whether the overmassive pattern persists. MARTA-3887 is a step in that direction. At 3.3 million solar masses, it is among the lower-mass black holes Webb has found, and it is still overmassive by a factor of about a hundred. That suggests the pattern is not purely a selection effect, although one object cannot settle the question on its own.

The seed question is the other side of the same coin. If black holes grow from heavy seeds — gas clouds collapsing directly into black holes of tens of thousands of solar masses — then an early start and modest feeding is enough to explain MARTA-3887. If they grow from light seeds — the remnants of the first stars, at around a hundred solar masses — then the same endpoint requires either a very early start or a period of super-Eddington feeding. The growth tracks in the paper make that trade-off explicit: a 100-solar-mass seed starting when the universe was 0.1 billion years old needs to feed at 34 percent of the maximum rate, but the same seed starting only with the recent starburst would need 570 percent. That is why astronomers care so much about finding the smallest early black holes and the earliest seeds. Every object like MARTA-3887 narrows the range of possible histories.

Other Webb results frame the same problem from different angles. The "little red dots" may be newborn supermassive black holes hidden inside the gas disks that formed them — a possible heavy-seed population. The Firefly Sparkle galaxy, with its ten young star clusters, may be a factory where dense clusters grow into the seeds of future giants, as SpaceNews reported in <a href="/blog/jwst-firefly-sparkle-black-hole-seeds-2026/">JWST Firefly Sparkle Black Hole Seeds: 10 Young Star Clusters May Grow Supermassive Giants</a>. And the UHZ1 reanalysis shows that the most dramatic claims do not always survive a second look. Taken together, these results describe a field in the middle of a sorting process: some candidates will be confirmed, some will be reclassified as starbursts or outflows, and the pattern that survives will be the one that the data, rather than the headlines, support. MARTA-3887's contribution is a modest, well-documented candidate at the faint end of the population — the end where the real answer is most likely to be found.

## What comes next

The immediate next step is confirmation. The team cannot rule out a supernova or an outflow from a single observation, so the obvious follow-up is more spectroscopy: deeper Webb observations of MARTA-3887, ideally with higher spectral resolution, to see whether the broad hydrogen line persists and whether its profile matches a feeding black hole more closely than any alternative. Variability would be a strong test — a black hole's feeding can flicker on timescales of months to years, while a supernova fades and an outflow is steadier — but at a redshift of 3.7694, even a year of waiting compresses to a few months in the galaxy's own frame, so patience will be required.

The wider programme is a survey problem. If MARTA-3887 is typical of a population of modest black holes in small, metal-poor galaxies, then there should be more of them, and finding them means looking deliberately at faint, clumpy, low-mass hosts rather than at the brightest quasars. That is a harder search, and it is the one the authors say is needed: to really understand how black holes form and grow, astronomers need to look for the smaller, more typical ones too. Each new object at the faint end tightens the constraints on seed masses, on feeding rates, and on how early growth had to begin. A handful of MARTA-3887-like systems would start to show whether the hundredfold excess is a universal feature of the early universe or a property of a particular kind of galaxy. A larger sample would let astronomers test whether the excess depends on metallicity, on star formation rate, or on the clumpiness of the host — the very properties that make MARTA-3887 distinctive.

There is also the reionization angle to follow up. If MARTA-3887 is genuinely leaking ionising photons, then similar galaxies may have contributed more to reionization than current models assume, and the role of their central black holes — as sources of heat, wind, and radiation — deserves a closer look. Measuring the escape fraction of ionising photons directly is difficult at this redshift, because the intervening gas absorbs them, but deeper observations and careful modelling can narrow the range. For now, the "leaker candidate" label is a flag planted for future work, not a conclusion.

None of this will happen quickly. Webb's schedule is oversubscribed, the objects are faint, and the analysis is painstaking. But that is the nature of early-universe astronomy: each answer is provisional, each candidate is a hypothesis, and the field advances by accumulating objects like MARTA-3887 until the pattern becomes impossible to ignore. Twelve billion years ago, a small galaxy grew a black hole faster than it grew its stars. Whether that was the rule or the exception is the question the next few years of observations will have to answer — and it is a question that, a decade ago, astronomers could not even have asked, because they could not see galaxies this faint, this far away, in this much detail at all.
