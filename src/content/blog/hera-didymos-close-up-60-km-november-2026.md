Four years after humanity deliberately smashed a spacecraft into an asteroid, the follow-up detective has finally arrived at the scene — and the first thing it saw made the scientists say it out loud. On 5 November 2026, the European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft returned its first close-up view of the Didymos binary asteroid system, photographed from just 60 kilometres away by its black-and-white Asteroid Framing Camera. The image shows the 780-metre-wide parent asteroid Didymos in crisp detail, boulders and craters and strange groove-like patterns spread across a hemisphere no spacecraft had ever seen before, while its smaller moonlet Dimorphos — the 151-metre rock that NASA's DART mission struck in 2022 — lurks mostly in shadow at the edge of the frame.

It is, in the words of Hera's principal investigator Patrick Michel, like looking at a whole new world. And it is only the beginning. The image marks the start of a six-month forensic investigation into the most consequential collision humans have ever staged in space, an examination that will decide whether the kinetic impactor — the technique of shoving a dangerous asteroid off course by hitting it very hard with a spacecraft — can be trusted as a real planetary defence tool, or whether the DART experiment left behind more questions than answers.

## The image that made scientists say "wow"

The photograph itself is deceptively simple. It is a monochrome frame, taken by Hera's Asteroid Framing Camera, and it shows Didymos as a lumpy, boulder-strewn sphere filling much of the view. Dimorphos, the moonlet that orbits it, is mostly hidden in shadow at the lower left, a dark crescent that gives away almost nothing of its shape. But for the scientists who have waited years for this moment, the plainness of the image is the point: after two years of deep-space cruise, after braking burns and a careful approach, the spacecraft is close enough that a single asteroid fills the camera's view, and every boulder on its surface is a piece of data.

"The first thing we said was wow!" said Patrick Michel, Hera's principal investigator and director of research at CNRS and the Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur, in ESA's announcement of the image. "This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART's camera showed us shortly before impact, so this feels like looking at a whole new world."

That last detail matters more than it might sound. When NASA's DART spacecraft approached the Didymos system in September 2022, its camera was pointed at Dimorphos, and the final images it returned showed one face of the parent asteroid only in passing. Hera's new frame shows the opposite hemisphere — the side DART never photographed. It is the first time anyone has seen this half of Didymos at all, and it does not look the way the team expected.

The familiar feature is still there: the same central ridge running along Didymos's equator that appeared in the DART-era views, a raised belt of rock that gives the asteroid its distinctive spinning-top silhouette. But around that ridge, the new hemisphere is strikingly different from the one DART saw. The surface is crowded with boulders of many sizes, pocked with craters, and crossed by what Michel described as patterns of grooves or ridges — linear features whose origin nobody can yet explain with confidence.

That uncertainty is exactly why Hera is here. A rubble-pile asteroid is not a solid rock. Didymos is thought to be a loose collection of boulders and gravel held together by its own weak gravity, and its surface features record a long history of collisions, spin-up, and material shifting under forces far gentler than anything on Earth. The grooves and ridges on the newly seen hemisphere could be scars from ancient impacts, or signs of material sliding toward the equator as the asteroid spins, or something else entirely. Sorting out which explanation fits will take the months of close observation that are now beginning.

## Ejecta, or something stranger?

There is a second, more tantalising possibility behind the new terrain, and it is the one that has the mission's scientists most excited. When DART slammed into Dimorphos on 26 September 2022, it did not merely dent its target. The impact blasted an enormous cloud of debris into space — a plume that stretched some 10,000 kilometres and was tracked by telescopes around the world and by the small Italian LICIACube companion satellite that flew past the scene in DART's wake. Much of that material escaped the system entirely. But some of it, the team calculates, would have fallen back — and some of it would have crossed the roughly one kilometre of space separating Dimorphos from Didymos, raining down on the parent asteroid.

"Since we expect a lot of impact-ejected material to have made it across the approximately 1 km of space from Dimorphos to Didymos, it may be that some of these characteristics originate from this transport of material," said ESA's Hera mission scientist Michael Küppers. "We look forward to seeing much more of both bodies and systemically mapping all the changes we identify."

In other words, some of the boulders and surface textures in Hera's new image may be shrapnel from the DART impact itself, delivered to Didymos by the very collision Hera was sent to study. If that is true, the new hemisphere is not just an unexplored landscape — it is a crime scene with the debris still lying where it fell. Distinguishing impact-delivered material from Didymos's own native surface will be one of the central tasks of the coming months, and it will require the full range of Hera's instruments: cameras to map the surface, a laser altimeter to measure the shape, and spectrometers to read the composition of the rocks.

The stakes of that forensic work reach far beyond the Didymos system. DART proved that a kinetic impactor can change an asteroid's orbit. What it did not prove is how, exactly, the energy of the impact was spent — how much went into pushing Dimorphos, and how much went into shattering rock, throwing debris, and deforming the target. If a future deflection mission needs to move a genuinely dangerous asteroid, planners must know whether the impactor will shove it cleanly or merely rearrange it. Hera's measurements are what turn a single spectacular experiment into a reliable technique.

## What DART did — and what we still don't know

The numbers from the DART impact are now familiar, but they still repay a moment's attention, because they frame everything Hera is about to measure. On 26 September 2022, NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test spacecraft — a 580-kilogram probe travelling at about 6.1 kilometres per second — struck Dimorphos head-on. The collision shortened the moonlet's orbit around Didymos by about 33 minutes, give or take a minute, a far larger change than mission planners had predicted before impact. It was the first time humanity had measurably altered the orbit of another body in the Solar System, and it was the proof of concept that planetary defence by kinetic impact is not merely a theory.

But the size of that orbital change is itself a puzzle. Pre-impact models predicted a smaller shift, and the extra deflection is thought to have come from the recoil of the ejected debris — the material blasted off Dimorphos acted like a rocket exhaust, pushing the asteroid further than the spacecraft's own momentum alone could have done. That "momentum enhancement" is good news for planetary defence, because it means a given impactor can do more than its mass alone would suggest. It is also a source of deep uncertainty, because the size of the enhancement depends on the target's internal structure, and nobody yet knows what Dimorphos is made of inside.

One open question is whether DART merely excavated a crater in Dimorphos or reshaped the moonlet more profoundly. Dimorphos is believed to be a weakly bound rubble pile, and an impact at 6.1 kilometres per second could have rearranged its constituent rocks rather than simply blasting a hole. Some researchers have raised the possibility that the asteroid is now tumbling rather than rotating in step with its orbit around Didymos, as it did before the impact. If Dimorphos was pushed close to the point of breaking apart, the implications for planetary defence would be serious: a deflection mission must move an asteroid onto a safer course, not accidentally shatter it into a swarm of hazardous fragments.

"Something a bit heavier or faster than DART may have destroyed the asteroid, which is not a desirable outcome for planetary defence," Küppers told *Astronomy Now*. That is the nightmare scenario the technique must be engineered to avoid, and it is why Hera's most important single measurement may be the most basic one: the mass of Dimorphos.

"This could not be determined by the impact," Küppers explained. "With this we will know how efficient the impact was. The secondary effect may even have been stronger than the direct impact effect. But to really know this, we need to know the mass."

Mass, shape, volume, and internal structure — together, those four measurements will reveal how loosely packed Dimorphos is, how the impact energy propagated through it, and how much of the orbital change came from the spacecraft itself versus the debris it threw off. That is the physics that a future deflection mission will be built on.

## How Hera got here

Hera's arrival at Didymos was the end of a two-year journey that began with a launch on 7 October 2024, when a Falcon 9 rocket lifted the 1,150-kilogram spacecraft from Cape Canaveral. The cruise was anything but a straight line. In March 2025, Hera swung past Mars, using the planet's gravity to bend its trajectory toward the Didymos system, and came within about 1,000 kilometres of the surface of the Martian moon Deimos along the way — a flyby that doubled as a rehearsal for the asteroid encounter, testing the cameras and navigation systems on a real, moving target. A course-correction manoeuvre in February 2026 set the spacecraft on its final approach path.

The approach itself was choreographed with unusual care, and SpaceNews has followed it step by step. Because Didymos's gravity is roughly 40,000 times weaker than Earth's, Hera cannot simply fall into orbit around the pair. Instead, it must slow down gently and fly a series of slow arcs past the asteroids, adjusting its path as it goes. The approach began to get interesting in early October 2026: on 7 October, exactly two years after launch, Hera imaged its destination for the first time, from approximately 236,500 kilometres away, and the entire binary system was a single pixel of light. On 18 October, from about 1,280 kilometres, the Asteroid Framing Camera resolved Didymos and Dimorphos as two separate objects for the first time — the first image of the pair as a pair, which SpaceNews covered in <a href="/blog/hera-first-image-didymos-dimorphos-2026/">Hera's First Image of the Didymos Asteroids: Two Worlds, One Pixel at a Time</a>.

Then came the braking. On 15 October, Hera fired its hydrazine thrusters for 93 minutes in its first major braking manoeuvre, cutting its speed relative to the asteroids. A second burn followed on 22 October, when the spacecraft was about 2,300 kilometres from Didymos, trimming its relative velocity by a further 70 metres per second. Together, the pair of firings, a week apart, slowed Hera to just 20 metres per second relative to its targets — roughly the speed of a car on a motorway — slow enough to approach gradually and safely. SpaceNews covered those braking burns as they happened, in <a href="/blog/esa-hera-braking-burns-didymos-arrival/">Hera Is Braking for History: ESA's Asteroid Detective Arrives at the Scene of NASA's DART Crash</a>.

From a single pixel on 7 October, to two separate points of light on 18 October, to a 60-kilometre close-up on 5 November: the progression is the visible record of a rendezvous executed with extraordinary precision, across a distance of 196.8 million kilometres from Earth, with an 11-minute one-way radio delay that means every command takes more than a fifth of an hour to arrive and every confirmation the same time to come back. The spacecraft is, for practical purposes, flying itself through the final approach, following plans uploaded hours in advance.

## The CubeSats and the months ahead

The close-up image is only the opening frame of the investigation. After its initial survey, Hera will release its two shoebox-sized CubeSats, Milani and Juventas, each weighing just 12 kilograms. Milani will study Dimorphos's surface, composition, and dust environment, flying lower than Hera itself. Juventas will use radar to probe beneath the asteroid's surface and search for signs of large internal blocks, voids, or finer debris — the first radar ever flown to sound an asteroid's interior — and will eventually attempt to land on Dimorphos to measure its gravity from the ground.

The mothership's own campaign will tighten steadily. A closest pass of about 10 kilometres follows in December, and the CubeSats deploy in January 2027. After that comes a close observation phase taking Hera down to about 4 kilometres from the asteroids, and then an experimental phase aiming for a single kilometre from Dimorphos. Hera itself may end its mission by touching down on one of Didymos's poles, an option still under consideration. In total, the spacecraft is expected to spend at least six months surveying the Didymos system.

The next milestone is close at hand: detailed images of both asteroids will be unveiled on 25 November, less than three weeks after the first close-up. Those frames should finally show the DART impact site on Dimorphos itself — the crater, or the reshaped terrain, or whatever the impact left behind — and they will be the first direct look at the place where humanity's first planetary defence experiment actually happened.

"Seeing this image is a hugely exciting moment for the whole Hera family," said Hera mission manager Ian Carnelli. "It has taken years of hard work and dedication to reach this point — but we're motivated to know that the adventure is only beginning!"

## Why it matters for planetary defence

It is worth stepping back from the images and the timelines to ask why any of this matters. The Didymos system was chosen for the DART experiment precisely because it posed no threat to Earth: the binary pair orbits the Sun safely, and Dimorphos was a convenient, measurable target whose orbital change could be observed from Earth. But the technique it tested is meant for a day nobody wants to arrive — the day astronomers find a real asteroid on a collision course with Earth, with years rather than decades of warning.

In that scenario, the options are limited. A gravity tractor, which uses a spacecraft's own gravitational pull to tug an asteroid over years, requires decades of warning that may not exist. Nuclear deflection carries political and technical complications that make it a last resort. A kinetic impactor can be built, launched, and flown in a few years — it is the most mature option, and the most likely to be available on short notice. But for that option to be credible, the physics must be understood, not merely demonstrated once. A single experiment proves that an impactor can move an asteroid. It does not prove how much impactor is needed, or what happens to a rubble pile under a high-speed strike, or whether the debris thrown off helps or hurts.

Hera is the mission that finishes understanding it. The close-up image from 60 kilometres is the first page of that second half of the experiment — the measurement half, after DART's impact half. Over the next six months, Hera and its CubeSats will weigh Dimorphos, map the crater, sound its interior, and trace the debris that crossed the gap to Didymos. The result should be a recipe: given an asteroid of a certain size, structure, and composition, here is the impactor that will move it, and here is the margin of safety.

That is why a single black-and-white photograph of a lumpy rock, taken from 60 kilometres away, matters as much as any image in space exploration this year. It is not a portrait of a distant world. It is the first evidence in a forensic investigation — and the verdict will shape how humanity defends itself against the sky.
