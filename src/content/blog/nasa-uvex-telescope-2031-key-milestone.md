NASA has given its next great ultraviolet eye on the universe a formal green light. The UltraViolet EXplorer, known as UVEX, has passed the technical and programmatic review called Key Decision Point C, a milestone that moves the mission out of the design phase and into full-scale development. With that decision, NASA has committed to launching the observatory by January 2031 at a total lifecycle cost of 590 million dollars, turning a telescope that has lived mostly in proposals and viewgraphs into a piece of hardware that will actually be built, tested, and flown.

The announcement, published by NASA on October 27, 2026, is the kind of bureaucratic sentence that hides an enormous scientific promise. UVEX will survey the entire sky in ultraviolet light, a wavelength range that is invisible to the human eye but rich in information about the hottest, youngest, and most violent things in the cosmos. It will hunt for small galaxies that have so far escaped detection, watch massive stars live fast and die young, and — perhaps most dramatically — swing into action within hours when gravitational-wave detectors feel the shudder of two neutron stars colliding, catching the ultraviolet flash that other telescopes may miss entirely.

For astronomers, the decision matters because the ultraviolet sky has been badly neglected for more than a decade. The last dedicated ultraviolet survey telescope, GALEX, was switched off in 2013 after a decade of work. Since then, ultraviolet astronomy has survived largely on borrowed time from Hubble, an observatory launched in 1990 that was never designed as a survey machine. UVEX is meant to change that, and to do so with a wide-field camera that can sweep the sky far faster than Hubble ever could.

## A green light with a price tag and a date

Key Decision Point C, or KDP-C, is one of the gates every NASA science mission must pass. At this review, agency officials examine whether the design is mature, the cost estimate is credible, the schedule is realistic, and the risks are understood. Passing it means the mission is confirmed: NASA commits to the budget and the launch window, and the project team is authorized to begin building flight hardware.

In UVEX's case, that commitment is a launch by January 2031 and a total lifecycle cost of 590 million dollars. The mission was originally selected for development in 2024 as a Medium-Class Explorer, or MIDEX, mission — a category of NASA astrophysics missions designed to deliver major science at a moderate price, on a faster schedule than flagship observatories. Earlier planning documents had targeted a launch in 2030; the new commitment of January 2031 is the formal, funded date that the project will now be held to.

The mission's principal investigator is Fiona Harrison at the California Institute of Technology in Pasadena. The team spans the University of California, Berkeley, NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Northrop Grumman, and the Space Dynamics Laboratory, a spread of university, agency, and industry partners that is typical of a mission at this scale. Harrison is no stranger to leading space telescopes: she was the principal investigator for NuSTAR, NASA's X-ray observatory that has been mapping the high-energy sky since 2012.

For a mission of this size, the price tag is modest by modern astrophysics standards. The James Webb Space Telescope cost roughly ten billion dollars; the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is a multi-billion-dollar flagship. UVEX, at 590 million dollars for its whole life from development through operations, is designed to punch above its weight by doing one thing exceptionally well: looking at the entire sky in ultraviolet light, repeatedly, and quickly.

## What the telescope actually is

UVEX is a wide-field ultraviolet space telescope built around a 75-centimeter mirror — about 30 inches across — in a three-mirror anastigmat design. It will carry imagers in two ultraviolet bands, far-ultraviolet light at wavelengths of roughly 139 to 190 nanometers and near-ultraviolet light at 203 to 270 nanometers, plus a spectrograph covering 115 to 265 nanometers. The imagers will resolve details down to about 2.25 arcseconds, and the spectrograph will split light finely enough to reveal the chemical fingerprints of hot gas and stars.

The observatory is planned for a highly elliptical orbit around Earth, swinging from a perigee of about 104,000 kilometers out to an apogee of roughly 396,000 kilometers — near the distance of the Moon — on a 13.7-day loop. That looping, far-flung orbit keeps the telescope clear of Earth's glare and radiation belts for long stretches, giving it the dark, stable sky it needs for deep survey work. The planned prime mission lasts two years, though, like GALEX before it, a successful survey telescope can keep working well beyond its nominal lifetime if funding and hardware allow.

The design is deliberately a survey machine rather than a pointed observatory. Where Hubble stares deeply at one small patch of sky at a time, UVEX will tile the heavens, building up a map of the ultraviolet sky that astronomers can mine for years. Its surveys are planned in tiers: a medium imaging survey covering 7,500 square degrees down to a faintness of 25.75 magnitudes in both ultraviolet bands, and a deep imaging survey covering 500 square degrees to 27th magnitude, overlapping the deep fields already mapped by the Vera Rubin Observatory, Euclid, and Roman. Together with an all-sky survey, those layers will give astronomers both breadth and depth.

## The low-mass galaxy frontier

UVEX's first major science goal is the low-mass galaxy frontier, and it is a genuine gap in our knowledge of the universe. Galaxies with stellar masses between about a million and a billion times that of the Sun — dwarfs by cosmic standards — should dominate the local galaxy population by number. They are also the closest analogs of the early galaxies now being found by the James Webb Space Telescope, small and metal-poor, forming stars in fits and starts. Yet only a tiny fraction of them are actually known, and existing counts of such galaxies are uncertain by a factor of twenty or more.

The problem is that, in visible and infrared light alone, a nearby dwarf galaxy and a massive galaxy far away in the early universe look confusingly alike. Ultraviolet light breaks that degeneracy. A nearby star-forming dwarf glows in the ultraviolet from its hot young stars; a distant galaxy's ultraviolet light has been stretched by the expansion of the universe out of the ultraviolet bands entirely. Adding far- and near-ultraviolet measurements to optical and infrared data therefore sharpens distance estimates dramatically, cutting the number of interlopers by about half when UVEX data are combined with Rubin and Euclid observations.

The practical payoff is concrete. The medium survey is designed to detect a dwarf galaxy of about a million solar masses forming stars at a distance of 100 megaparsecs — roughly 326 million light-years. The deep survey will push further and fainter in the fields where other great observatories have already stared. The result should be a census of small galaxies that is not biased toward only the brightest, most furiously star-forming systems, but includes the quiet majority that spend most of their lives between bursts of star formation.

This matters because small galaxies are where much of the universe's story is written in miniature. Their shallow gravitational wells make them sensitive to the winds blown by supernovae, to the radiation of their own stars, and to the dark matter halos in which they sit. Counting them accurately, and measuring how they form stars over time, tests our models of galaxy formation at the scale where those models are most uncertain — and where the discrepancies between theory and observation have been most stubborn.

## Chasing the flash after a gravitational wave

UVEX's second headline goal is speed. When the LIGO, Virgo, and KAGRA gravitational-wave detectors register the ripple of two neutron stars spiraling together, astronomers have hours, not weeks, to catch the aftermath. The collision throws off a fireball of neutron-rich debris that glows first in ultraviolet light, then fades and reddens over days as heavier elements form. That glowing debris, called a kilonova, is where the universe forges gold, platinum, and other heavy elements — and its early ultraviolet light carries the fingerprints of the jet punching out of the merger and the fastest, lightest ejecta.

The only kilonova ever caught in detail, the counterpart to the gravitational-wave event GW170817 in August 2017, was observed in ultraviolet light by Hubble and Swift, but only after a delay and with narrow fields of view. UVEX is designed to do better. It will carry the first rapidly deployable ultraviolet spectroscopic capability on a survey telescope, meaning it can slew quickly to a gravitational-wave localization region, scan it with its wide field, find the new ultraviolet source among the stars, and then take its spectrum — all while the kilonova is still young and bright.

That is harder than it sounds. Gravitational-wave localizations can sprawl across hundreds of square degrees, and ultraviolet sensitivity varies across the sky because of dust in our own galaxy. Researchers have already been building the tools for the job: a 2025 study led by Caltech and Johns Hopkins scientists laid out an observing strategy in which UVEX adjusts its exposure time field by field, using a mathematical optimization technique called mixed-integer linear programming to maximize the chance of catching a kilonova. The strategy has been built into an open-source scheduling toolkit, so that when the first alert of the UVEX era arrives, the telescope will not be improvising.

The stakes go beyond a single event. Each kilonova observed early in ultraviolet light sharpens our understanding of how neutron-star mergers work, how much heavy-element material they eject, and how the physics of extreme matter behaves at densities no laboratory can reach. If the merger rate turns out lower than hoped — only a handful of neutron-star mergers have been confirmed since 2017 — UVEX's survey work ensures the mission still delivers transformational science even in a quiet gravitational-wave era.

## A spectrograph that can pivot

Beyond its two headline surveys, UVEX will carry an ultraviolet spectrograph to study stellar explosions and massive stars. Massive stars are the chemical factories of the universe, and their powerful winds and explosive deaths shape the galaxies around them. Their light peaks in the ultraviolet, and their spectra reveal the composition, temperature, and velocity of the gas they throw off.

The spectrograph's rapid-response capability matters for more than kilonovae. Supernovae, tidal disruption events in which a star is shredded by a black hole, and other fast-changing cosmic events all evolve fastest in their first hours and days. A telescope that can point quickly and take an ultraviolet spectrum in that window captures physics that is gone by the time slower observatories arrive. In that sense, UVEX is as much a time-domain machine as a survey machine — a telescope built for a sky that changes.

This is part of a broader shift in astronomy. The great survey telescopes of the 2020s — Rubin scanning the southern sky every few nights, Roman mapping the infrared sky, Euclid charting the dark universe — are turning astronomy into a science of movies rather than snapshots. UVEX adds the ultraviolet reel to that film, and its rapid-response mode means it can zoom in on the most dramatic frames. As NASA itself has noted, the ultraviolet survey will complement the other wide surveys of the decade, helping to build a modern, multi-wavelength map of the universe.

## Why ultraviolet, and why now

To understand why UVEX matters, it helps to remember what ultraviolet light actually shows. Stars hotter than the Sun — massive, young, short-lived stars — pour out most of their energy in the ultraviolet. So do the accretion disks around black holes, the shock waves of supernovae, and the glowing gas of star-forming regions. Ultraviolet light is the light of heat, youth, and violence. A universe mapped only in visible and infrared light is a universe with its youngest, hottest chapters dimmed or missing.

GALEX proved the value of that map. Launched in 2003, it surveyed the sky in ultraviolet light for a decade, cataloging star formation across cosmic time, catching giant stellar flares, and finding baby galaxies in our own cosmic neighborhood. It was extended three times before being decommissioned in June 2013. Since then, the ultraviolet sky has gone largely unsurveved — a gap of nearly two decades by the time UVEX launches.

Hubble has kept ultraviolet astronomy alive in the meantime, but Hubble is a pointed observatory with a narrow field, not a surveyor. It can study one object exquisitely; it cannot map the sky. UVEX's wide field and survey strategy are designed to do what Hubble cannot: build the catalog from which the next generation of discoveries will be drawn, much as GALEX's catalog fed research for years after the spacecraft fell silent.

The timing also matters for the fleet. <a href="/blog/nasa-roman-telescope-fully-functional-science-commissioning/">NASA's Roman Space Telescope is moving toward science operations</a>, Euclid is already charting the dark universe, and Rubin is sweeping the southern sky. UVEX, arriving by January 2031, will slot into that fleet as its ultraviolet member, giving astronomers the missing wavelength band for the same sky. The <a href="/blog/euclid-31-oldest-quasars/">oldest quasars found by Euclid</a> and the <a href="/blog/nasa-prima-far-infrared-telescope/">far-infrared view planned for NASA's PRIMA telescope</a> show the same logic at work: no single wavelength tells the whole story, and the greatest discoveries come when surveys in different bands are combined.

## Who builds it, and what comes next

With KDP-C passed, the UVEX team moves into the phase engineers call implementation: finalizing designs, ordering parts, building and testing flight hardware, and integrating the telescope, spacecraft, and instruments into a single observatory. The partners named by NASA — Caltech, UC Berkeley, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Northrop Grumman, and the Space Dynamics Laboratory — will divide that work across universities, NASA centers, and industry, a model that has served the Explorer program well for decades.

There are still gates ahead. Key Decision Point D will authorize the move from assembly to launch preparations, and the mission will face reviews of its readiness at each step. Schedules in spaceflight are famously fragile, and a launch commitment of January 2031 is a promise that will be tested by supply chains, testing surprises, and the ordinary friction of building precision hardware. But KDP-C is the gate that matters most for a mission's existence: it is the point at which NASA stops studying a telescope and starts building one.

For the scientists who have waited since GALEX, that distinction is everything. A telescope on paper can be cancelled; a telescope in a clean room is much harder to stop. The ultraviolet sky has waited thirteen years for a successor. With this decision, the wait has a date attached to it — and a price, and a team, and a spacecraft design that will soon begin taking physical shape.

## The sky that waits

There is a particular romance to a survey telescope. It does not chase a single famous target; it maps everything, and trusts that the universe will reward the effort. GALEX did exactly that, and its catalog still yields papers more than a decade after the spacecraft was switched off. UVEX's catalog will be deeper, sharper, and wider, and it will arrive in an era when gravitational-wave detectors, giant survey telescopes, and follow-up observatories can act on its discoveries within hours.

The most exciting UVEX discovery may well be something nobody has predicted — a class of object that only shows itself in ultraviolet light, or a flare, a flash, or a fading ember that a survey of the whole sky catches by luck and by design. That is the bet behind every great survey, and it is a bet that has paid off again and again in astronomy's history.

On October 27, 2026, NASA made that bet official. The ultraviolet sky has a new telescope coming, and it has a launch date. By January 2031, if all goes to plan, a 75-centimeter mirror will open its eye above the atmosphere, sweep the heavens in light our eyes cannot see, and begin writing the next chapter of the ultraviolet universe — one wide field at a time.
