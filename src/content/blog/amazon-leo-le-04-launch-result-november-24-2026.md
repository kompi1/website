Amazon's fourth Ariane 6 mission ended the way the company needed it to end. At 11:14 UTC on Tuesday, November 24 — 18:14 in Jakarta, at the very last minute of the day's launch window — an Ariane 64 rocket rose from Europe's Spaceport in Kourou, French Guiana, carrying 36 Amazon Leo broadband satellites. Two hours and four minutes later, the last of them separated from the rocket's upper stage, and Arianespace could confirm what the previous day's scrub had put in doubt: LE-04 was a complete success.

The result mattered beyond the 36 satellites themselves. Nine days earlier, Amazon had watched 40 satellites of the same constellation destroyed when United Launch Alliance's Vulcan rocket failed on its return to flight. One of the company's three heavy-lift launch providers was grounded again, another had lost a rocket on the test stand earlier in the year, and the arithmetic of Amazon's Federal Communications Commission deadlines was getting harder by the week. LE-04, the sixteenth launch of the constellation and the tenth flight of Ariane 6, was the one launch in the manifest that could not afford to go wrong. It did not.

Arianespace confirmed the deployment in a statement issued shortly after the mission ended, saying the satellites had been "delivered with precision into low Earth orbit" at an altitude of approximately 460 kilometres. Spaceflight Now, which covered the flight live, updated its report at 10:25 a.m. EST with the sentence the constellation team had been waiting for: "Arianespace successfully deployed all 36 satellites." Amazon's own mission updates page put it the same way, noting that the flight brings the constellation past 400 satellites deployed and that its team in Redmond, Washington, had assumed control of the new spacecraft.

## A launch at the very edge of the window

The window on Tuesday was short: 10:45 to 11:14 UTC, 17:45 to 18:14 in Jakarta, a span of just 29 minutes. Arianespace had already used one day of it. The mission had been scheduled for Monday, November 23, and the countdown on that day ran deep enough that the mobile gantry work was complete and the rocket stood exposed on the pad before a technical event in the ground support systems stopped everything. Arianespace halted the countdown hours before liftoff, kept the rocket and its 36 satellites safe and stable, and stood down for a day.

Tuesday's attempt did not go smoothly either. The target time inside the window moved from 17:45 to 17:46 WIB, then held, then moved again as the countdown worked through its final checks. By the time the clock reached the end of the window, the mission was down to its last seconds of margin. Then, at 11:14 UTC exactly, the four P160C solid rocket boosters and the Vulcain 2.1 core engine lit, and VA271 left ELA-4, the Ariane 6 launch complex at the Guiana Space Centre, in the rocket's most powerful configuration.

It was the second flight of the upgraded P160C boosters, which carry 156 tonnes of propellant each — 14 tonnes more than the P120C boosters they replace — and add roughly 10 to 15 percent to the launcher's performance. The four boosters burned for a little over two minutes before separating, and the core stage carried the stack onward while the Vinci upper-stage engine prepared for its two burns: the first to reach a parking orbit, the second, after a long coast, to circularise at the deployment altitude of about 460 kilometres.

There was nothing unusual about the ascent. That was the point. Ariane 6 has now flown ten times and succeeded ten times, and LE-04 was its fifth launch of 2026 — a cadence the programme has been building toward since the rocket's debut flight in July 2024. For Arianespace, which counts VA271 as its 363rd flight, the mission was also a milestone of a quieter kind: the tenth flight of a new heavy-lift rocket is the moment a launcher stops being a development project and starts being an operational system, and the company's chief executive said so in the hours after liftoff.

"Having now placed 136 Amazon Leo satellites into orbit this year, Arianespace has reached another milestone in the deployment of this ambitious constellation," said David Cavaillolès, the company's chief executive. The number matters because it is the whole of Arianespace's contribution so far: four Ariane 6 flights in 2026, 32 satellites on the first two and 36 on each of the last two, adding up to 136 spacecraft delivered for a single customer in under ten months.

## Two hours, four minutes, 36 satellites

The deployment itself was unhurried. After the Vinci engine's second burn put the upper stage into the target orbit, the 36 satellites began separating in sequence, a process that started about 99 minutes after liftoff and ran for roughly 25 minutes. The last separation came two hours and four minutes after launch — about 20:18 in Jakarta — and the mission was declared complete. Amazon's account of the flight gives the deployment altitude as approximately 465 kilometres, 289 miles, a small difference from Arianespace's 460-kilometre figure that reflects the two organisations rounding the same trajectory differently.

What happened next was invisible but decisive. Control of the satellites passed to Amazon's 24-hour mission operations centre in Redmond, Washington, where the team made initial contact with each spacecraft and began health checks. The satellites will spend the coming days and weeks raising themselves from their deployment orbit to their assigned operational altitude of 630 kilometres, 392 miles, using their own electric propulsion, and then go through commissioning — the careful process of verifying every system before they are allowed to carry customer traffic.

That commissioning step is where the constellation's arithmetic becomes tangible. Spaceflight Now counts 432 Leo satellites launched in total once LE-04 is included, and Amazon's own tally put 392 satellites in orbit and working before this flight. The 36 new arrivals, once they reach 630 kilometres and pass their checks, will bring the working fleet to 428 — the third-largest satellite constellation in orbit, behind only Starlink and China's Guowang network, and the largest that any single company other than SpaceX has ever assembled.

Steve Metayer, Amazon Leo's vice president of production, framed the flight as the product of a partnership rather than a single launch. "Every Ariane 6 mission we fly with Arianespace builds on the one before it," he said. "Our Leo team works side by side with Arianespace on everything from satellite integration to launch day operations, and that shared expertise and trust is the driving force behind our cadence together this year. LE-04 is another strong result for that partnership, and we're looking forward to many more missions together as we prepare to roll out Amazon Leo to customers."

The satellites themselves arrived in French Guiana in August, were stacked by mid-September, and were encapsulated inside the fairing at the start of November — a campaign that had already survived one delay before the scrub, when reported turbopump issues on the Vulcain 2.1 main engine pushed the launcher's schedule earlier in the year. The 36-satellite payload also matched LE-03, the June flight that was the heaviest commercial payload ever launched on an Ariane rocket.

## From scrub to success in 24 hours

The day before the launch, LE-04 looked like it might become another entry in a year of delays. The timeline below shows how quickly the picture changed — and how much of the year's story was already packed into the 24 hours around the flight.

- **April 2022:** Amazon selects Arianespace for 18 launches, alongside 38 Vulcan flights and 12 New Glenn flights, in the largest commercial launch deal ever signed.
- **February 2026:** LE-01 (VA267) flies 32 satellites on Ariane 6, the first of four Amazon missions on the rocket this year.
- **April 2026:** LE-02 (VA268) flies 32 more satellites.
- **June 2026:** LE-03 (VA269) flies 36 satellites — the heaviest commercial payload ever launched on an Ariane rocket — and the first flight of the upgraded P160C boosters.
- **September 2026:** Amazon orders six additional Ariane 64 launches, expanding its Arianespace contract from 18 to 24 missions.
- **October 2026:** Amazon builds its 1,000th Leo satellite at its facilities in Kirkland, Washington.
- **November 15, 2026:** ULA's Vulcan rocket fails on its LV-01 return-to-flight mission, destroying 40 Amazon Leo satellites — the first total mission failure for ULA since the company was founded in 2006.
- **November 23, 2026:** LE-04 scrubs hours before liftoff after a technical event in the ground support systems at Kourou. The rocket and satellites are kept safe and stable.
- **November 24, 2026, 11:14 UTC:** VA271 lifts off at the very end of the 29-minute window.
- **November 24, 2026, ~13:18 UTC:** The last of the 36 satellites separates, two hours and four minutes after liftoff. All 36 are confirmed deployed.
- **November 24–25, 2026:** Amazon's Redmond operations centre takes control; the satellites begin their climb to the 630-kilometre operational orbit.

The scrub itself deserves a closer look, because it shows how thin the margins have become. Arianespace halted the countdown on Monday after the ground support problem appeared, and the company's statements stressed that the rocket and the satellites were safe — language that matters when a payload is worth tens of millions of dollars and the customer has just lost 40 spacecraft nine days earlier. The 24-hour turnaround, from scrub to a successful liftoff at the end of the next day's window, is the kind of operational tempo that a launch provider can only deliver when the vehicle, the ground systems, and the range are all genuinely ready. It is also the tempo Amazon needs: with 24 Arianespace missions now under contract and 20 still to fly, Kourou has become the most reliable address in the company's launch manifest.

## What happens to the satellites now

The satellites that separated over the Atlantic on Tuesday evening are not yet part of the working constellation. They are in a parking orbit, and the next phase of their lives belongs to Amazon's operations team rather than to Arianespace.

Each spacecraft must first be contacted and checked. Amazon's Redmond centre runs around the clock, and the first hours after deployment are spent confirming that every satellite's power, communications, and propulsion systems survived the launch. Only then does the slow climb begin: the satellites use their onboard electric thrusters to raise their orbits from roughly 460 kilometres to 630 kilometres, a process that takes days to weeks depending on how much thrust each spacecraft can spare and how the constellation's traffic managers slot them into the fleet.

Commissioning follows. The satellites are tested end to end — antennas, inter-satellite links, and the ground network that connects them to Amazon's cloud — before they are declared ready to carry customer traffic. It is a deliberate process, and Amazon has run it 15 times before for batches of 24 to 36 spacecraft. The company says the satellites already in orbit are all working, and the 36 new arrivals will be folded into the same routine.

The operational altitude matters because it defines the constellation's geometry. At 630 kilometres, each satellite covers a wide patch of the Earth's surface, and the fleet as a whole is designed to provide continuous coverage once the first-generation constellation of 3,232 spacecraft is complete. The satellites at 460 kilometres are below their working altitude, which is normal: launch vehicles deliver to a lower orbit and the spacecraft climb under their own power, a trade that lets the rocket carry more satellites per flight at the cost of a longer commissioning period.

For customers, the practical meaning of LE-04 is incremental but real. Amazon has said it is preparing to roll out Leo service, and every successful batch moves the constellation closer to the density at which continuous, reliable broadband becomes possible. The company does not publish a firm commercial start date, and it has been careful not to promise one, but the cadence of its launches — four Ariane 6 missions in ten months, plus Falcon 9 flights in 2025 — shows a programme that is now deploying hardware faster than it is losing it, which is the minimum condition for a constellation to exist at all.

## The rocket crunch that made LE-04 matter

To understand why a routine successful launch drew so much attention, it helps to look at what happened to Amazon's other rockets this year. The company bought its way into orbit in April 2022 with the largest commercial launch purchase in history: 18 flights on Arianespace's Ariane 6, 38 on ULA's Vulcan, and 12 on Blue Origin's New Glenn. The plan assumed all three rockets would be flying by the middle of the decade. None of them was ready on time, and 2026 made the problem worse.

Vulcan's year began with an anomaly on the USSF-87 national-security mission in February, when one of the Northrop Grumman-built GEM-63XL solid rocket motors malfunctioned. The rocket was grounded for most of 2026 while ULA investigated, and the grounding froze the largest single block of Amazon's manifest — 38 launches — for the better part of a year. When Vulcan finally returned to flight on November 15, carrying 40 Amazon Leo satellites on the LV-01 mission, it failed again: this time the problem was with the rocket's main booster, and the mission was lost entirely. It was the first full mission failure for ULA since the company was founded in 2006, and it cost Amazon 40 spacecraft in a single morning.

Blue Origin's New Glenn had its own disaster. The company's fourth New Glenn rocket, intended to launch 48 Amazon Leo satellites, was destroyed along with some launch infrastructure during an explosion on the pad in a static fire test of its BE-4 engines earlier in the year. The satellites were not on board when the rocket blew up, which spared Amazon a second payload loss, but the accident pushed Blue Origin's return to flight into early 2027. The company had been working toward flying again by the end of 2026; that target is now gone.

Arianespace, by contrast, has not suffered a major setback this year, though it has had its own troubles. Reported turbopump issues on the Vulcain 2.1 main engine caused schedule delays for the Ariane 6 programme, and the rocket's manifest is crowded: VA271 was the fifth Ariane 6 launch of 2026, and the vehicle is shared between European institutional missions and commercial customers. But the rocket has flown ten times and succeeded ten times, and in a year when both of its American competitors lost vehicles, that record is the reason Amazon keeps adding to its Arianespace order.

Amazon has been adapting. In late 2025 it converted an option with Blue Origin to fly a total of 24 batches of satellites on New Glenn rockets, and at the same time it bought ten more Falcon 9 launches from SpaceX. The three Falcon 9 flights it had already purchased were all flown in 2025, and they worked. In September 2026 it added six more Ariane 64 launches, taking its Arianespace total from 18 to 24. The arithmetic that follows is stark: with Vulcan grounded and New Glenn not flying until 2027, only Ariane 6 and Falcon 9 are currently available to Amazon, and the two rockets together can carry only about 996 more satellites — enough for roughly 1,392 in total, still short of the 1,616 the company needs to reach half its constellation.

The table makes the year's story plain. Arianespace has delivered 136 satellites in four flights without losing one. ULA's single Amazon flight of the year lost 40. Blue Origin flew none. SpaceX's three Falcon 9 flights in 2025 delivered their payloads, and the ten additional Falcon 9 launches Amazon bought in late 2025 are now among the most valuable slots in the manifest — not because Falcon 9 is the best rocket for the job, but because it is one of only two that are flying.

Amazon's public response to the Vulcan failure was measured. "There is risk with any launch, and that's especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01," the company said in a statement after the anomaly. "ULA has been a trusted partner for Amazon, and we believe they'll learn from this mission and deliver on their long-term plans for Vulcan." The statement was gracious, and it was also the only thing the company could say: it needs Vulcan to work. The 38 launches it bought from ULA remain the largest single block of its manifest, and no other provider can absorb them.

## The FCC clock

Behind the launch manifest sits a regulatory deadline that explains the urgency. Amazon was originally required by the Federal Communications Commission to deploy half of its first-generation constellation — 1,616 satellites — by July 30, 2026. It filed in January 2026 for a modification of that requirement, asking for a 24-month extension. At the time of filing, the company reported that it had launched 180 satellites and estimated it would have deployed approximately 700 by July 30, 2026.

In June 2026, the FCC's Space Bureau partially granted the waiver. Jay Schwarz, the bureau's chief, wrote in the order that Amazon was exempt from the 50 percent requirement — but that the company was still obliged to deploy the full first-generation constellation of 3,232 satellites by July 30, 2029. The order also required Amazon to provide detailed schedules of future launches, along with information on its financial and operational investments in the constellation and its mitigation efforts so far.

The arithmetic since then has been unforgiving. With 432 satellites launched and 428 working after LE-04, Amazon has roughly 1,184 spacecraft to go before it reaches the halfway mark, and 2,800 before the constellation is complete. At the current pace — 16 launches in about 17 months since the first satellites flew — the 2029 deadline is reachable only if the launch rate rises sharply, and that rise depends on Vulcan and New Glenn returning to flight. LE-04 helps, but one successful Ariane 6 flight every two or three months cannot close a gap measured in thousands of satellites.

There is also a spectrum condition attached to the waiver, as reported when it was granted: Amazon risks losing priority access to its radio spectrum after March 30, 2028, unless half the constellation is operating by then. That date is now less than a year and a half away, and it is the deadline that will shape Amazon's launch buying in 2027 more than any other single number. Every successful flight between now and then is worth more than the satellites it carries, because it keeps the spectrum — and with it, the business case for the whole constellation — intact.

## What comes next

LE-04 leaves Amazon with 20 Arianespace launches still under contract, out of the 24 it has now ordered, and a manifest that is finally diversified in the right direction: more flights on the rocket that is flying, more Falcon 9 capacity bought as insurance, and a New Glenn programme that should return in early 2027 with 24 batches on its books. The company's own production line is not the constraint — the 1,000th satellite rolled out of its Kirkland, Washington, factory in October, and the satellites for the next flights are already being built.

The next test of the manifest comes quickly. Hours after LE-04 lifted off, SpaceX launched the classified USSF-266 mission for the United States Space Force from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California, with booster B1081 landing on the droneship Of Course I Still Love You on its 29th flight — the 681st booster landing in SpaceX's history — a reminder that the same launch ranges and the same rockets serve a crowded queue of government and commercial customers. For Amazon, the queue that matters most is its own: the company needs Vulcan to return, New Glenn to fly, and Ariane 6 to keep its perfect record, all at the same time.

For Arianespace, the flight is a proof point it can take to every customer on its manifest. Ten flights, ten successes, five launches in a single year, and a commercial customer that has now entrusted it with 136 satellites and ordered six more launches on the strength of the results. The European rocket was designed to give Europe independent access to space; it is now also, by accident of timing and by the reliability of its record, the most dependable ride Amazon Leo has.

The night of November 24 ended with 36 new satellites climbing toward their working orbits and a constellation past 400 spacecraft. The scrub on November 23 is already a footnote. What remains is the harder part: building the remaining 2,800 satellites, finding the rockets to launch them, and doing it all before the deadlines in 2028 and 2029 arrive. LE-04 proved the system works. The next two years will prove whether it works fast enough.
