NASA has formally asked private industry a question it has been circling for years: who can save the Hubble Space Telescope before gravity finishes what time started? The NASA Hubble reboost RFI 2032, issued on October 29, 2026, invites commercial companies to pitch a mission to capture Hubble, lift it to a safer orbit, service it, or, failing that, guide it to a controlled end. Responses are due by 3 p.m. Eastern time on Monday, November 30, 2026. Any rescue mission would have to fly by 2032. There is, for now, no contract, no budget line, and no promise that NASA will buy anything at all. What there is, for the first time, is a deadline written against a hard physical clock: left alone, the most famous telescope in history will fall out of the sky in the early 2030s.

Hubble's predicament is simple orbital physics. The telescope has no propulsion system of its own, so it cannot fight the faint but relentless drag of Earth's upper atmosphere. Every spacecraft in low Earth orbit faces the same slow decay, but most modern satellites carry thrusters to correct it. Hubble, designed in an era when astronauts in the Space Shuttle would visit regularly, never needed its own engines. The shuttle fleet retired in 2011. Nothing has raised Hubble's orbit since 2002. The result is a slow, measurable slide that NASA can now put numbers on, and the numbers are what forced this week's announcement.

## A slow fall, measured in kilometres

When astronauts aboard the Space Shuttle Columbia last raised Hubble's orbit in 2002, the telescope circled Earth at roughly 583 kilometres, about 362 miles, above the surface. Today it flies at roughly 470 kilometres, about 292 miles. That is a drop of more than 110 kilometres over 24 years, and the descent is not linear. As the orbit shrinks, the atmosphere thickens, drag increases, and the fall accelerates. The final shuttle servicing visit, STS-125 aboard Atlantis in May 2009, replaced instruments and repaired the telescope, but it did not raise its altitude. Since then, Hubble has been gliding downward with no way to stop itself.

NASA's own lifetime analysis, performed in July 2026 and cited in the new request for information, puts the risk in stark probabilistic terms. There is a 10 percent chance of reentry by September 2032, rising to a 50 percent chance by December 2033. The agency's public announcement frames the most likely window as 2033 to 2034. Those forecasts can shift in either direction with solar activity, because the Sun's 11-year cycle puffs up the upper atmosphere and increases drag, and the current cycle has run hotter and longer than many models expected. The uncertainty is the point: NASA cannot wait until the forecast sharpens, because by then it may be too late to build and fly a rescue.

The altitude target in the request is specific. A company that proposes a reboost must be able to lift Hubble to at least 600 kilometres, about 373 miles. That is only slightly above the orbit Hubble occupied in 2002, but the physics of the thinner air at that height matters enormously. Analysts cited in the coverage of the announcement estimate that a boost to 600 kilometres would likely keep Hubble aloft for at least another 25 years, potentially extending its working life into the 2050s. The telescope's instruments would still age, and its gyroscopes, which have already forced operational changes, would remain a constraint. But the sky would not take the telescope back.

Hubble's resilience so far is part of why the rescue case is credible at all. In June 2024 the telescope transitioned to a one-gyroscope operating mode after years of gyroscope degradation, a change NASA managed without ending science operations. The telescope that would be rescued is not the pristine instrument launched in 1990. It is a veteran operating on backups and workarounds, and still producing science that astronomers compete fiercely to use.

## What NASA is actually asking for

The request for information, posted by NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, which manages Hubble, describes three distinct options, and a company may propose any of them. The first is the headline act: develop, test, and operate a servicing vehicle that captures Hubble in low Earth orbit and boosts it to at least 600 kilometres. The second is a servicing mission to enhance the observatory's performance and increase its resilience and expected lifetime, which could mean new instruments, replacement parts, or both. The third is the option nobody wants to dwell on but every responsible operator must plan for: a mission to perform a controlled reentry and disposal at the end of Hubble's mission, steering the telescope into the atmosphere over an uninhabited ocean area rather than letting it fall wherever physics decides.

There is one strict requirement that applies to the first two options, and NASA states it plainly: the boost or servicing mission must not harm the telescope. That single sentence carries enormous engineering weight. Hubble is a 12,200-kilogram observatory, about 13.2 metres long and 4.3 metres across at its widest, carrying a 2.4-metre primary mirror and optics that cannot be replaced if damaged. Any vehicle that grabs it must control the combined spacecraft gently, without imparting torques or shocks that could damage the mirror, the solar arrays, or the instruments. The request asks respondents to specify how much the telescope would be allowed to rotate during capture and how long they expect to remain attached to it, details that reveal how seriously NASA is taking the dynamics of the problem.

The division of labour is also spelled out. NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center would provide technical information about the telescope, consultation with its Hubble experts, and coordinated observatory operations for rendezvous, capture, boost, servicing, or reentry. The contractor would supply everything else: the launch vehicle, the servicing spacecraft, mission operations, and crew if the proposal calls for astronauts. The notice explicitly allows both crewed and robotic approaches. That openness matters, because the two paths imply very different costs, risks, and timelines, and NASA says it wants to learn what industry can actually do before it decides which path, if any, to fund.

What the notice is not, NASA stresses, is a procurement. It is not a request for proposals, it carries no government commitment, and the agency will not pay companies for their responses. Officials want to know who is interested, what a mission would cost, what payment assumptions a company would make, and who could realistically reach Hubble in time. A formal solicitation could follow, but no date has been set, and no price has been indicated. For companies weighing whether to respond by November 30, the calculation is therefore speculative: invest engineering time now for a mission that may never be bought, in the hope of shaping a programme that could become one of the most prestigious servicing jobs in spaceflight.

## A second chance for an old idea

This is not the first time NASA has asked this question. In 2022, Jared Isaacman, then a private astronaut, proposed a mostly self-funded mission using a SpaceX Crew Dragon to raise Hubble's orbit, an idea associated with the Polaris programme of private spaceflights. NASA studied the concept and, in 2023, issued a similar request for information that drew eight responses, including a robotic proposal from Astroscale and Momentus. The idea was ultimately rejected, and no mission followed. Isaacman is now NASA's administrator, and his name is on this week's announcement, which adds a layer of personal history to the new request that the space community has been quick to notice.

In the agency's announcement, Isaacman framed the decision in terms of value for money and timing. "Given the time we have until Hubble's anticipated re-entry, we are eager to explore the breadth of possibilities to boost Hubble to a higher orbit while we develop the flagship mission to follow," he said. The second half of that sentence is doing quiet work. Hubble's successor in visible and ultraviolet light, the Habitable Worlds Observatory, is not expected before the 2040s. Between Hubble's fall and that flagship's arrival lies a gap of roughly a decade in which humanity would have no large space telescope working in the wavelengths where Hubble excels. The request is, in effect, an attempt to buy time across that gap.

The 2023 exercise is instructive about what has changed and what has not. The earlier request also sought ideas with no funding attached, and the proposals it received were never built. What has changed since is the urgency of the numbers: the July 2026 lifetime analysis gives the reentry risk a sharper edge than it had three years ago, and the servicing market has matured. Northrop Grumman's Mission Extension Vehicle, for example, has already demonstrated on-orbit servicing and lifetime extension for commercial satellites in geostationary orbit, a different regime from Hubble's low orbit but a proof that the business of touching another spacecraft and changing its fate is no longer theoretical. The question is whether any of that capability can be adapted to a 12-tonne telescope in a decaying orbit, on a schedule that leaves room for development, testing, and a launch by 2032.

## Why Hubble still matters enough to rescue

It is tempting to treat the James Webb Space Telescope and the forthcoming Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope as Hubble's replacements, and to conclude that losing Hubble would be sad but survivable. Astronomers who use the telescopes push back hard on that framing, because the instruments do not overlap the way the shorthand suggests. Webb and Roman work mainly in the infrared. Hubble's strength is visible and ultraviolet light, wavelengths that reveal hot young stars, the chemistry of exoplanet atmospheres, and the structure of galaxies in ways infrared cannot duplicate. Losing Hubble would not just remove a telescope; it would remove a wavelength range from space astronomy for a decade or more.

The demand figures make the point in the starkest terms. NASA reports that demand for Hubble observing time exceeds supply by seven to one, a ratio that has held even as Webb has come online. Seven proposals compete for every slot. That oversubscription is not nostalgia. It reflects the fact that Hubble, 36 years after launch, still answers questions no other observatory can, from the ultraviolet glow of star-forming regions to the precise measurement of the universe's expansion rate. The telescope's longevity is itself a scientific asset: decades of observations of the same targets, taken with the same instruments, form a baseline that cannot be recreated by a new telescope.

Hubble's cultural weight matters too, though NASA's request is written in the dry language of procurement. The telescope has produced the images that define space in the public imagination, from the Pillars of Creation to the deep fields that revealed thousands of galaxies in a patch of apparently empty sky. That public affection is not a line item in a request for information, but it shapes the politics of any decision to let the telescope burn. A controlled disposal is a responsible plan. An uncontrolled reentry of a beloved observatory, with debris potentially surviving to the ground, would be a different kind of event entirely, and NASA's request explicitly includes disposal planning because the agency cannot responsibly ignore that outcome.

The contrast with Hubble's most recent public showcase is worth noting. Just days before this announcement, NASA released new side-by-side views of a dusty pillar in the Carina Nebula from Hubble and Webb, images that showed the same structure in visible and infrared light and demonstrated, in a single comparison, why two telescopes seeing different wavelengths are better than one. That story, covered in <a href="/blog/cats-head-nebula-2026-webb-hubble-carina/">Cat's Head Nebula 2026: Webb and Hubble Reveal a Newborn Star's Eye in Carina</a>, is a reminder that Hubble's visible-light view is not a legacy capability waiting to be retired. It is an active partner in the multi-wavelength astronomy that defines the field today.

## The engineering problem, in plain terms

Catching Hubble is not like docking with the International Space Station, which was built for visitors, has docking ports, and can cooperate in its own rendezvous. Hubble was built for the Space Shuttle, whose astronauts grabbed it with a robotic arm and berthed it in a payload bay. The telescope does have one gift for would-be rescuers: a passive Soft Capture Mechanism, a docking ring fitted to its aft shroud during the 2009 servicing mission precisely so that a future spacecraft, crewed or robotic, could attach to it. That ring is why the new request is plausible at all. Without it, a servicing vehicle would have to grapple the telescope's body or its appendages, a far riskier proposition for a 12-tonne spacecraft with fragile solar arrays and a delicate mirror.

Even with the capture ring, the engineering challenges are formidable. A servicing vehicle must rendezvous with a target that cannot help it, match its orbit and attitude, attach without imparting damaging loads, and then either fire its own thrusters to raise the combined stack or perform delicate servicing work, all while keeping the telescope pointed safely and its instruments protected. If the mission is crewed, the vehicle must also keep astronauts safe during proximity operations with a massive, uncooperative object. If it is robotic, the autonomy and dexterity required exceed anything yet demonstrated on a target of Hubble's size in low Earth orbit. The request's questions about rotation limits and attachment duration are NASA's way of forcing respondents to confront these dynamics rather than hand-waving past them.

There is also the question of what servicing would even mean in 2032. Hubble's instruments are ageing, and some have already been replaced or repaired by astronauts in ways a robotic mission could not replicate. A servicing mission that merely boosts the orbit would preserve the telescope as it is, buying time for its existing instruments to keep working. A mission that also upgraded instruments or replaced failing components could extend Hubble's scientific life beyond what a boost alone would achieve, but it would be far more complex and expensive. NASA's request deliberately leaves both possibilities open, asking companies to describe what they could enhance, not just what they could lift. The trade-off between a simple, safe boost and an ambitious, risky servicing job is one of the central judgments any respondent must make, and one of the main things NASA hopes to learn from the responses.

The mass of the problem deserves emphasis. At roughly 12,200 kilograms after its 2009 servicing visit, Hubble is heavier than most satellites that servicing companies have handled, and its 13.2-metre length makes it an awkward load. Raising such a mass by 130 kilometres or more requires substantial propellant, and the servicing vehicle must carry that propellant to Hubble's orbit in the first place. The mission design is therefore a stack of trade-offs: launch vehicle capacity, propellant mass, rendezvous precision, and the structural limits of a telescope that was never designed to be pushed around by something attached to its back end. None of these problems is obviously insurmountable. All of them together explain why NASA is asking for information rather than announcing a mission.

## The gap that makes the rescue worth pricing

The strategic argument for rescuing Hubble rests on what comes after it. The Habitable Worlds Observatory, the flagship that will eventually succeed Hubble in visible and ultraviolet astronomy, is still in its design phase. NASA's own architecture studies, covered in <a href="/blog/habitable-worlds-observatory-6-meter-design-2026/">Habitable Worlds Observatory 6-Meter Design Takes Shape in NASA's New Architecture Study</a>, describe a roughly six-metre telescope aimed at imaging Earth-like exoplanets, but the observatory is not expected to be operational before the 2040s. That leaves a gap of a decade or more in which the world's astronomers would have no large ultraviolet and optical space telescope at all, a gap that would break long-running observing programmes, strand the scientists trained on Hubble data, and surrender a wavelength range to ground-based telescopes that cannot match space-based ultraviolet work because Earth's atmosphere blocks most ultraviolet light entirely.

The Roman Space Telescope, which recently completed its observatory checkout and entered science commissioning as described in <a href="/blog/nasa-roman-telescope-fully-functional-science-commissioning/">Fully Functional and Almost Ready: NASA's Roman Telescope Enters Its Final Science Commissioning</a>, will be a magnificent survey instrument when it begins mapping the dark universe in early 2027. But Roman, like Webb, is an infrared telescope at heart, and its wide-field surveys complement rather than replace Hubble's targeted ultraviolet and visible observations. The three telescopes together form a system: Webb sees the cold and distant universe in infrared, Roman surveys vast swaths of sky in infrared, and Hubble sees the hot, energetic, and nearby universe in visible and ultraviolet light. Remove Hubble and the system loses a leg.

That gap argument is also what makes the request a test case for a broader shift in how NASA buys spaceflight. The agency is increasingly asking commercial companies to provide services, from cargo delivery to lunar landers, rather than building and operating everything itself. A Hubble rescue would be the most symbolically potent version of that shift: a private company, paid by NASA, saving a public observatory. The emerging in-space servicing, assembly, and manufacturing market, often shortened to ISAM, has been waiting for a flagship customer. Hubble could be that customer, if the economics work and the engineering holds. If they do not, the request will still have served a purpose, by forcing the market to price the job honestly and by giving NASA a documented basis for deciding whether to fund a rescue, plan a disposal, or accept the risk of an uncontrolled reentry.

## What happens if nobody comes

The third option in NASA's request deserves as much attention as the first two, because it is the option that will be exercised if the rescue economics fail or the engineering proves too risky. A controlled reentry mission would attach a vehicle to Hubble and steer it into the atmosphere over an uninhabited stretch of ocean, ensuring that any surviving debris falls where it cannot harm anyone. The alternative, an uncontrolled reentry, would scatter debris along a ground track determined by orbital mechanics and the state of the upper atmosphere on the day of the fall, with no ability to choose where the pieces land. Most of a spacecraft burns up on reentry, but a telescope with a large mirror and dense components is not guaranteed to burn completely, and NASA's request treats disposal planning as a responsibility, not an afterthought.

The timeline for a disposal mission is the same as for a rescue: by 2032, before the reentry probability climbs steeply. That deadline creates a decision point that NASA cannot defer indefinitely. If the November 30 responses show that no company can credibly rescue Hubble within the budget and schedule, the agency will have to choose between funding a disposal mission, accepting an uncontrolled reentry, or finding another path, such as a government-built solution. Each choice carries costs and risks, and the request for information is designed to make those choices explicit rather than letting them drift until the physics makes them for NASA.

There is also a quieter risk that the request implicitly acknowledges: the possibility that Hubble's science ends before its orbit does. The telescope's instruments and gyroscopes are ageing, and a failure could end useful observations even if the orbit is saved. A boost mission that arrives after a critical failure would preserve a silent telescope. That is one reason the request asks about servicing and resilience alongside the boost itself. The goal is not merely to keep a piece of hardware in orbit. It is to keep a working observatory doing science, and the distinction matters for how companies should design their proposals and how NASA should judge them.

## A market test with a famous name

For the commercial space industry, the Hubble request is a peculiar opportunity: a high-profile, technically demanding job with no guaranteed payment, a hard deadline, and a customer that has already rejected a similar idea once. Companies that respond will be signalling that they believe in-space servicing has matured enough to handle a target like Hubble, and that they are willing to invest in proving it. Companies that do not respond will be signalling, by omission, that the job is still beyond the market's reach or beyond its appetite for risk. Either signal is useful to NASA, which is why the request is structured as an information-gathering exercise rather than a competition.

The precedent of the 2023 responses will loom over the new round. Eight companies or teams answered last time, including established servicing players and newer entrants, and none of their proposals became a mission. Respondents this time will need to explain what has changed: better technology, lower costs, a clearer NASA commitment, or a different mission architecture. The involvement of Isaacman, who championed the idea as a private citizen before becoming administrator, may give respondents more confidence that the agency is serious this time. It may also raise expectations that NASA will follow through with funding if the responses are strong. The request itself, carefully, promises neither.

The broader context is a NASA that is being asked to do more with constrained budgets, and an administrator who has argued publicly for cost-effective ways to extend spacecraft that are already working. Hubble is the ultimate test of that philosophy. It is already built, already launched, already proven, and already paid for many times over. Extending its life by boosting its orbit is, in principle, far cheaper than building a replacement, and far faster than waiting for the Habitable Worlds Observatory. The question is whether "in principle" survives contact with the engineering reality of catching a 12-tonne telescope and pushing it uphill.

## The months ahead

The immediate calendar is short. Responses are due November 30, 2026, barely a month after the request was issued. That compressed schedule suggests NASA wants a quick read of the market, not a leisurely study, and it puts pressure on companies to assemble credible proposals fast. After the responses arrive, NASA will assess them, and the agency's next step, whether a formal solicitation, a funded study, or a quiet decision to plan for disposal, will reveal how seriously the rescue option is being taken. There is no published date for that decision, and the space community will be watching for signals in NASA's budget requests and programme announcements through 2027.

Longer term, the deadline that matters is 2032. A mission that must fly by then needs to be selected, funded, built, and tested on a schedule that leaves little slack. Spacecraft development rarely runs on time, and a servicing vehicle for Hubble would be among the most complex ever attempted. If NASA waits until 2030 to choose a provider, the schedule becomes nearly impossible. If it chooses in 2027, the schedule is demanding but plausible. The November 30 responses are therefore not just a survey. They are the opening move in a procurement timeline that must move quickly if it is to move at all.

For astronomers, the stakes are personal as well as professional. Many of the scientists who will use Hubble in the 2030s were not born when it launched. The telescope has outlived careers, outlived the shuttle that serviced it, and outlived the assumptions of the era that built it. Its survival into the 2050s, which a boost to 600 kilometres could plausibly deliver, would make it a 60-year-old observatory, an almost absurd longevity for a machine designed in the 1970s and launched in 1990. That longevity is not guaranteed by any request for information. But it is, for the first time in years, on the table as a real possibility rather than a wistful hope.

## A telescope worth catching

There is a temptation, when writing about Hubble, to slip into elegy, to treat the telescope as already lost and to mourn it in advance. The new request resists that temptation, and so should any fair account of it. Hubble is not dead. It is working, oversubscribed, and producing science. Its orbit is decaying, but decay is a slow process with a known timeline and a known remedy. The remedy requires a spacecraft that does not yet exist, a budget that has not yet been allocated, and a decision that has not yet been made. Those are serious obstacles. They are not, however, laws of physics.

The story of Hubble has always been a story of rescue. The telescope launched with a flawed mirror in 1990, and astronauts fixed it in 1993. It has been repaired, upgraded, and coaxed through failures that would have ended lesser missions. Each rescue required someone to decide that the telescope was worth the effort, and each time that decision was vindicated by the science that followed. The question NASA has now put to industry is whether that pattern can continue without astronauts in a shuttle, with a commercial vehicle instead, in an era when the space economy is supposed to be able to do such things routinely. The answer will arrive first in the form of proposals on November 30, and eventually, if the economics and engineering align, in the form of a spacecraft climbing toward a small, ageing telescope and gently pushing it back uphill.

If that spacecraft flies, Hubble will keep watching the universe in visible and ultraviolet light while its infrared successors map the cosmos in their own wavelengths, and the gap that now threatens astronomy in the 2030s will close before it opens. If it does not fly, Hubble will fall, probably in 2033 or 2034, and the sky will lose one of its great eyes for a decade or more. Either way, the decision is now in motion, with a deadline attached and a clock ticking in the thin air 470 kilometres overhead. For a telescope that has spent 36 years showing humanity what it cannot see with its own eyes, the least the spacefaring world can do is look up, do the maths, and try to catch it before it falls.
