Somewhere in the Moon's Ocean of Storms sits a hole in the ground nearly a hundred metres wide. It is steep-sided, almost perfectly circular, and it drops roughly fifty metres straight down into permanent darkness. Lunar orbiters have photographed it for years, and each image has raised the same tantalising question: does this pit open into something much bigger — a long, hollow lava tube snaking for kilometres beneath the lunar surface, big enough to hide a human outpost from radiation, micrometeorites and the savage temperature swings of the surface?

NASA now intends to find out. On September 30, 2026, the agency announced it had selected a new investigation called GIMLI — Geophysical Instruments for Marius Lunar pit Investigation — whose sole purpose is to settle the question of what lies beneath the Marius Hills Pit. If the pit does connect to a substantial underground cavern, it would be one of the most consequential discoveries in lunar exploration: a ready-made, radiation-proof volume of space that could one day house the equipment, laboratories and even living quarters of humanity's first permanent Moon base. If it does not, the measurements will still give scientists their clearest look yet at how the Moon's volcanic past sculpted the ground future astronauts will walk on.

The mission is named, with a wink, after the cave-loving dwarf Gimli from J.R.R. Tolkien's *The Lord of the Rings*. But the science is serious, and so is the timing. The investigation is one of three new research selections NASA is sending toward the lunar surface through its PRISM programme — Payloads and Research Investigations on the Surface of the Moon — a trio of missions aimed squarely at the practical problems of building and sustaining a permanent human presence on the Moon. NASA's updated announcement on October 2 placed the investigations among a new group of lunar science projects focused on regions that could become increasingly important as exploration ramps up. And ramping up it is: with the Artemis programme pushing toward a sustained lunar footprint and China racing toward its own crewed landing around 2030, the scouting of the Moon's most promising real estate has begun in earnest.

## A Hole in the Ocean of Storms

The target of the new investigation sits in a volcanic region called the Marius Hills, within Oceanus Procellarum — the "Ocean of Storms" — a vast, dark lava plain on the western edge of the Moon's near side. The Marius Hills Pit, often called the Marius Hills "hole," is a steep-sided circular opening in that volcanic terrain. According to EarthSky's reporting on the mission, the pit is about 90 metres (295 feet) across and approximately 50 metres (164 feet) deep. Other analyses of Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter images have described an opening of roughly that scale with a floor locked in permanent shadow. Either way, it is enormous by human standards — you could drop a fifteen-storey building into it and not see the roof.

What makes this particular hole special is what scientists believe lies beneath it. On Earth, lava tubes form when the outer layer of a lava flow cools and hardens while molten rock keeps moving beneath it. Later, when the lava flow stops and drains away, it leaves behind a hollow channel or tunnel. Lunar geologists have long suspected that the same process operated on the Moon billions of years ago, when the mare plains were flooded with molten basalt. The Marius Hills Pit is believed to be a "skylight" — a place where the roof of such a lava tube collapsed, punching an opening from the surface down into the void below.

It is an elegant hypothesis, and orbital evidence supports it: scientists already have indications that there is empty space beneath the surface near Marius Hills. But indications are not proof. Photographs from orbit can show the opening; they cannot show the tunnel. That is what GIMLI is for. As Gareth Morgan of the Planetary Science Institute has noted, answering the question of what lies beneath the pit would also clarify how lunar volcanism built the surface we see today — a scientific payoff that reaches far beyond the practical question of where to put a habitat.

The pit is not the only such feature on the Moon. Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter imagery has revealed dozens of pits scattered across the surface, several of them widely interpreted as skylights into subsurface voids. But Marius Hills is among the most promising candidates, and it is the one NASA has now chosen to investigate at close range. For the first time, instruments designed to see underground will be aimed at one of these mysterious openings — and whatever they find, the answer will rewrite our understanding of the lunar subsurface.

## The Mission That Won't Go Down the Hole

GIMLI will not, despite its name, send a robot rappelling down into the pit. The risk and complexity of a descent into a 50-metre-deep, shadow-drenched shaft — with sheer walls, unknown floor conditions and no direct sunlight for solar power — put that option firmly out of reach for now. Instead, the investigation will do something arguably more powerful: it will interrogate the ground from the surface, combining several different geophysical techniques to build a picture of what lies beneath.

The plan calls for ground-penetrating radar, which sends signals through lunar material and detects boundaries between solid rock and empty space; seismic instruments, which measure how artificially generated vibrations move through the subsurface; highly sensitive gravity observations, which look for tiny variations in the local gravitational field that could reveal where solid rock is missing underground; and cameras, which will examine the pit walls, the exposed rock layers and the surrounding terrain. Together, those observations will be combined with the geophysical measurements to cross-check any candidate void from multiple angles.

Using several methods at the same location matters because each instrument sees the underground environment differently. Radar may detect the roof or walls of a cavity. Seismic waves behave differently when travelling around empty spaces. Gravity measurements can reveal whether a large volume of rock is absent beneath the instruments. If all three methods identify the same underground feature, researchers could begin estimating its shape, depth and extent with considerably greater confidence than any single technique could deliver on its own. In effect, GIMLI could provide the clearest evidence yet of whether the visible pit is merely a local collapse or the entrance into a much larger lunar tunnel.

The investigation is led by Dr. Nathaniel Putzig of the Planetary Science Institute, with Honeybee Robotics and other research organisations involved in the effort. It was selected through NASA's PRISM programme, with delivery to the Moon expected through the agency's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) initiative — the programme under which NASA contracts commercial landers to carry scientific payloads to the lunar surface. An official targeted launch or arrival date for the GIMLI payload has not yet been announced. The science writer Brooks Mendenhall described the mission in *Astronomy* magazine on October 9, 2026, helping to bring the investigation to a wider audience beyond the space-science community.

## Why a Cave Could Change Everything

To understand why NASA is investing in a cave-hunting mission, consider the problem of living on the Moon. The lunar surface is a punishing environment. With no appreciable atmosphere and no global magnetic field, the surface is exposed to constant bombardment by cosmic radiation and solar energetic particles. Temperatures swing violently between the two-week-long lunar day and the two-week-long lunar night. Micrometeoroids rain down continuously, and larger impacts are a standing threat. Every one of those hazards demands shielding — and shielding, in the brutal economics of spaceflight, means mass, and mass means money.

That is the fundamental appeal of a lava tube. Several metres of solid lunar rock overhead would provide natural protection from radiation, damp the worst of the temperature extremes, and absorb the constant pitter-patter of micrometeoroid impacts — shielding that would otherwise have to be launched from Earth at enormous cost or mined and piled up by heavy equipment. In its announcement, NASA identifies those potential benefits directly: understanding such spaces could inform future habitat planning, especially if measurements reveal substantial protected areas beneath the surface.

The logic has been familiar to science-fiction writers for more than a century, from the subterranean cities of early lunar fiction to the buried bases of modern films. What is new is that it has entered the engineering trade space. As one recent analysis put it, NASA's cave-hunting investigations are a clear signal that the classic trope of humans living in Moon caves is no longer just pulp — it is rapidly becoming an engineering trade study. The questions engineers now ask are concrete: how wide are the tubes, how stable are their roofs, how would astronauts get in and out, how would you seal and pressurise a section of one?

GIMLI is not the step that answers those questions. Its immediate task is more fundamental: establish what lies beneath the opening. A detected cavern would not automatically become a suitable home. Any future assessment would need to address access, structural stability, available space, and all the systems astronauts require to live and work there. But without this first step — the confirmation that the caverns actually exist and a measurement of their scale — none of the later questions can even be asked seriously. Either result matters. A connected cavern would raise new scientific and exploration questions. Its absence would still sharpen our understanding of the pit's origin and improve scientists' understanding of the local geology. The next advance depends on collecting measurements at the surface and testing the possibilities that orbital images have kept open.

There is a deeper scientific prize, too. The Moon's volcanic history is written into its subsurface, and the lava tubes — if they exist — are some of the best-preserved pages. Because the interior of a sealed tube has been shielded from space weathering and surface impacts, its walls could preserve pristine samples of ancient basalt, layered in the order they were emplaced billions of years ago. Geologists would love to read that record. A confirmed tube would be a window into the era when the Moon's interior was still hot and its great lava plains were still being born.

## Three Missions, One Goal: The First Moon Base

GIMLI is not flying alone. NASA selected three research investigations in this round, and together they read like a checklist for everything a permanent Moon base would need: shelter, supplies and safety.

The second investigation, the Lunar Environment Monitoring Station — South Pole, will deploy an autonomous facility to track conditions and hazards over extended periods at the lunar south pole — the region where Artemis missions are headed and where water ice is thought to be most abundant. The station will detect micrometeoroids, measure volatile substances in the Moon's thin outer layer, and use a short-period seismometer to detect vibrations and moonquakes. That data will help engineers design safer infrastructure for permanent lunar habitats — bridges, landing pads, pressurised modules and the rest — by telling them exactly what the environment throws at them. The investigation is led by Dr. Mehdi Benna of the University of Maryland, Baltimore County.

The third, known as DISCO, tackles the supply question. It will conduct the first direct measurements of water ice on the lunar surface from ground level, focusing on extremely cold regions called micro-cold traps — small, permanently shadowed pockets where ice may be stable even at low latitudes. Understanding the location, quantity and distribution of ice could enable astronauts to extract water locally and potentially convert it into oxygen for breathing and hydrogen for rocket propellant — the difference between hauling every drop from Earth and living off the land. The investigation will also examine how landing procedures affect surface conditions and terrain stability, a practical concern as landers of increasing size begin touching down. DISCO is directed by Dr. Ariel Deutsch of NASA's Ames Research Center.

## The Race Is the Reason

It is worth asking why NASA chose this moment to prioritise cave scouting. The answer is the calendar. The Artemis programme is committed to establishing a sustained human presence on the Moon — not the flags-and-footprints visits of the Apollo era, but an enduring foothold, with crews rotating through a surface habitat and eventually living and working there for months at a time. That transition changes everything about how the Moon must be understood. A site that was fine for a three-day Apollo excursion can be completely inadequate for a permanent base. Sustained presence demands a detailed, three-dimensional understanding of the ground: where the ice is, where the hazards are, and where nature has already done some of the construction work.

China's accelerating lunar programme sharpens the urgency. With a crewed landing targeted around 2030 and a joint International Lunar Research Station planned with Russia at the south pole, the competition for the Moon's best sites is real, and it is happening now. The south pole draws most of the attention because of its water ice, but ice is not the only resource that matters. A confirmed, accessible lava tube in the near-side maria would be a prize of its own — a construction-ready volume that no amount of launched shielding mass could easily replicate.

NASA's trio of new investigations reflects that reality. Combined with GIMLI's geophysical scan of the subsurface, these missions sketch a near-future in which robots survey and model cave systems before humans ever set foot inside — much like drones and rovers scout Martian terrain today. The hazard-tracking payload helps engineers design habitats, suits and vehicles that can withstand long stays instead of short Apollo-style visits. The ice-search mission addresses the logistics problem of not hauling every drop of water and kilogram of propellant from Earth. And GIMLI's quest for a natural underground shelter attacks one of the biggest pain points in building any off-world base: how to protect people without burying them in metres of imported shielding material.

There is also a planetary-defence dimension that rarely makes the headlines. The same radar and seismic techniques GIMLI will use to map a subsurface void are close cousins of the instruments used to probe the interiors of asteroids and to assess the structure of potentially hazardous near-Earth objects. Every improvement in our ability to see inside solid rock from the surface pays dividends elsewhere in solar-system science. The Moon, as ever, is the proving ground.

## What Happens Next

For now, the timeline is open. NASA has not announced a targeted launch or arrival date for GIMLI, and the investigation's delivery depends on the CLPS programme's cadence of commercial lunar landings — a pipeline that has been growing busier, but which still runs on the uncertain schedules of a young commercial industry. The lander that carries GIMLI will need to set down near enough to the Marius Hills Pit for its instruments to do their work, and the payload's exact configuration — which sensors ride together, how the seismic source will be generated, how far the radar will penetrate — will be worked out as the mission moves from selection into development.

But the selection itself is the milestone that matters today. NASA has formally committed resources to answering one of the most evocative open questions in lunar science: whether the Moon hides caverns large enough to shelter a base. The agency announced the selection on September 30, 2026, and updated its public statement on October 2, folding GIMLI into a broader push to characterise the lunar environments that future explorers will inherit. From here, the work proceeds through the familiar stages of instrument development, integration with a CLPS lander, and — eventually — the long, quiet cruise to the Ocean of Storms.

When the data finally come back, they will arrive as radar echoes, seismic traces and gravity anomalies — unglamorous signals, translated by patient analysis into a picture of the invisible. And then, perhaps, we will know: the hole in the Ocean of Storms is a doorway. Behind it, darkness, dust, and room enough for a city. The Moon has been keeping its secrets for four billion years. GIMLI is going to ask it, politely and with excellent instruments, to start talking.
