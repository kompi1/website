Amazon's comeback launch never left the ground on Monday. The Ariane 6 rocket carrying 36 Amazon Leo broadband satellites was scrubbed hours before its planned liftoff from the Guiana Space Centre in Kourou, French Guiana, after a technical event in the launcher's ground support systems interrupted the countdown. The rocket and its 36 satellites are safe, and Arianespace says the next launch opportunity is no earlier than Tuesday, November 24.

The mission, designated LE-04 by Amazon and VA271 by Arianespace, was due to lift off in a 29-minute window opening at 11:09 UTC — 18:09 WIB — on Monday, November 23. Instead, the day ended with the rocket still standing on its pad, its mobile gantry still in place, and a constellation programme that has now waited eight days for a flight it desperately needs. The scrub was the second blow to Amazon's deployment schedule in nine days: on November 15, a Vulcan rocket failure destroyed 40 satellites bound for the same network, and the Ariane flight that was supposed to restart the programme has now slipped at least a day.

Arianespace announced the halt in a short statement on its newsroom, saying the launch chronology "was interrupted following a technical event affecting the launcher's ground support systems." The company added that the chronology "was stopped before the mobile gantry removal and the start of launcher system operations," and that "Ariane 6 and its passengers, the 36 Amazon Leo satellites, remain in stable and safe conditions." Technical teams are assessing the event and preparing for launch operations to resume, the company said, promising further updates as soon as they become available.

Launch trackers recorded the scrub at 06:43 UTC on Monday — 13:43 WIB — roughly four and a half hours before the window was due to open. Space.com updated its coverage later in the morning to reflect the postponement, noting that the next launch opportunity would be no earlier than November 24. Trackers have since updated the target to Tuesday, November 24, with the same 11:09–11:38 UTC window, although Arianespace itself has so far confirmed only that the next attempt will come no earlier than that date.

## What happened on the pad

The detail that matters most in Arianespace's short statement is the timing. The chronology was stopped before the mobile gantry was removed and before the launcher's own systems were switched on. In the choreography of an Ariane 6 countdown, that is early. The mobile gantry — the tall, box-like structure that encloses the rocket on the pad — is rolled back hours before liftoff, exposing the vehicle for its final preparations. Stopping before that step means the fault was caught while the rocket was still buttoned up inside its shelter, and before the vehicle's own systems had even begun their launch-day operations.

That matters for two reasons. First, it means the rocket itself was never put through the stresses of a late countdown — no cryogenic loading in anger, no engine systems armed, no hold called at the last second with propellant already aboard. Second, it strongly suggests the problem sits in the ground equipment that prepares and services the launcher, rather than in the rocket or the satellites. Arianespace has not said what the ground equipment problem was or how long the fix will take, and no cause has been stated anywhere in the company's public updates. What is known is only what the operator chose to say: a technical event affecting the launcher's ground support systems, caught early, with the vehicle and payload safe.

The distinction is not a small one. A fault in ground support equipment is, in principle, the most recoverable kind of launch problem. Ground systems — the umbilicals, the fueling lines, the power and data connections, the environmental controls inside the gantry — can be inspected, repaired, and retested without touching the rocket. A fault in the vehicle itself, by contrast, can mean rolling back, destacking, and weeks of work. The fact that Arianespace is talking about the next opportunity coming as early as Tuesday, rather than weeks away, is itself a signal: whatever failed, the company believes it can be assessed and cleared on a timescale measured in days, not months.

It is also worth noting what did not happen. There was no ignition, no abort at T-minus seconds, no dramatic hold with the rocket already fuelled. The countdown was interrupted, not abandoned mid-flight. The 36 satellites remain encapsulated inside the fairing, the rocket remains vertical on the pad, and the campaign can, in principle, pick up where it left off once the ground systems are cleared. That is the best version of a bad day at a spaceport — and it is a very different kind of failure from the one Amazon suffered eight days ago.

## The new target: Tuesday, November 24

Arianespace's statement gives the next opportunity as "no earlier than November 24," without confirming a time. Launch trackers, which follow the operator's filings and range notices, have updated the target to Tuesday, November 24, with the same 29-minute window: 11:09 to 11:38 UTC. For viewers in Indonesia, that would again mean a window of 18:09 to 18:38 WIB, with liftoff targeted for the opening of the window. The webcast, which Arianespace runs from about 30 minutes before liftoff, would begin at around 17:39 WIB.

Nothing about that Tuesday date is guaranteed. A "no earlier than" date is a floor, not a promise: it is the earliest the mission could fly if the ground-systems event is understood and fixed in time. If the assessment turns up something more serious, or if the repair needs parts or tests that cannot be completed by Tuesday morning, the date will move again. Arianespace has said it will provide further updates as soon as they become available, and the confirmation that matters — a firm launch time for Tuesday — will come from the operator, not from trackers.

For Amazon, a one-day slip is the smallest possible delay, and the company will take it gladly if Tuesday holds. But the margin is thin. The constellation programme is already running behind the schedule its licence demands, and every day a batch of 36 satellites sits on the ground is a day the network does not grow. A slip of a day is nothing; a slip that stretches into a week, or that reveals a deeper problem with the ground systems at Kourou, would be something else entirely.

## Timeline: a mission delayed twice

LE-04 has now been delayed twice, and the two delays could hardly be more different in character. The first was a slow, technical slip measured in weeks. The second was a sudden halt measured in hours. Together they tell the story of a mission that has struggled to get off the ground.

| Date | Milestone |
| --- | --- |

| October 7, 2026 | Original earliest launch date (NET) passes without a flight |

The first delay began in the summer, when an anomaly was found in a liquid-hydrogen turbopump during an engine acceptance test on a test stand. The fault was in the cryogenic propulsion system, not in the satellites or the launch pad, and the French newspaper La Tribune reported on October 13 that ArianeGroup had resolved it. On October 22, the European Space Agency's director of space transportation, Géraldine Naja, told reporters that the problem was not something the agency was worried about, while acknowledging that it had delayed a launch planned for 2026 into the following year.

That first slip cost LE-04 about 47 days. A provisional schedule had the mission launching no earlier than October 7, and the flight did not get a confirmed date until Arianespace announced November 23 on October 14. The delay rippled through Arianespace's year: the company had been chasing seven or eight Ariane 6 flights in 2026, and VA271 would be only the fifth. A December flight carrying the EUMETSAT MetOp-SG B1 weather satellite is still on the schedule, which would take the year to six flights at most — and five if the December mission slips into January.

Monday's scrub is different in kind. It was not a design problem or a test-stand discovery, but a fault in the equipment that prepares the rocket on the pad — the kind of problem that can appear on the day itself, with no warning in the weeks before. That is why it is both less alarming and more frustrating than the turbopump delay. Less alarming, because ground equipment can be fixed without redesigning anything. More frustrating, because it struck on the morning the mission was finally supposed to fly, after 47 days of waiting.

## Why this flight matters: eight days after Vulcan

LE-04 was always an important flight. It became a more significant one because of what happened eight days ago. On November 15, United Launch Alliance's Vulcan rocket lifted off from Cape Canaveral carrying 40 Amazon Leo satellites on mission LV-01, the first Leo flight on Vulcan. The rocket failed. A Centaur upper-stage anomaly about ten minutes into flight meant the mission never reached orbit, and all 40 satellites were lost. It was ULA's first outright mission failure since the company was formed in 2006, and it came on a flight that was itself the return to flight for a rocket grounded since February.

In a preliminary update on November 18, ULA said its initial review pointed to inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to the BE-4 engines as the cause of low booster thrust, and that the indications pointed to the vehicle's fuel supply rather than to engine performance. The company said the upper stage's RL10 engines appeared to operate normally but could not make up for the earlier performance shortfall, and that its solid rocket boosters also appeared to perform normally. Those assessments remain preliminary. The investigation continues, with the Federal Aviation Administration overseeing the work and approving the final report and corrective actions. Vulcan has no return-to-flight date.

The loss matters because of scale. Amazon had launched nearly 400 satellites across 14 missions before LV-01, making its network the third-largest constellation in orbit. Forty satellites is about a tenth of what was flying. The Vulcan flight was supposed to push the constellation past 400 spacecraft in orbit; instead it set the count back, and it removed from service a rocket that ULA has contracted to fly 38 Amazon Leo missions.

Readers who want the full account of the Vulcan failure can find it in our earlier reporting on the <a href="/blog/vulcan-lv-01-launch-failure-november-15-2026/">Vulcan LV-01 launch failure November 15 2026</a>, which covers the flight, the loss of all 40 satellites, and what ULA has said since.

That is the context in which Monday's scrub landed. LE-04 was the first Amazon Leo deployment since the Vulcan loss, and the first chance for the programme to show it could keep flying while one of its rockets is grounded. Instead, the comeback flight did not fly at all — not because the rocket failed, but because the ground equipment that serves it did. The two events are unrelated in cause, but they land on the same programme in the same month, and together they have cost Amazon nine days of deployment time and counting.

## Four Ariane flights, one growing constellation

Monday's mission was the fourth time Ariane 6 has carried Amazon Leo satellites, and the pattern across those four flights tells the story of the partnership. The first three are summarised below, with the scrubbed flight alongside them for comparison.

| Mission | Flight | Date | Vehicle | Satellites | Arianespace Leo total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LE-01 | VA267 | Feb 12, 2026 | Ariane 64 (P120C) | 32 | 32 |
| LE-02 | VA268 | Apr 30, 2026 | Ariane 64 (P120C) | 32 | 64 |
| LE-03 | VA269 | Jun 17, 2026 | Ariane 64 (P160C) | 36 | 100 |
| LE-04 | VA271 | Nov 23, 2026 (scrubbed) | Ariane 64 (P160C) | 36 | 136 if successful |

The progression is visible in the numbers. The first two flights each carried 32 satellites on the original P120C boosters. The third flight, on June 17, was the first to use the upgraded P160C boosters and the first to carry 36 satellites — a payload Arianespace described as the heaviest ever launched by an Ariane rocket. LE-04 repeats that 36-satellite configuration, and if it succeeds, Arianespace will have delivered 136 Leo satellites in total, more than a third of everything Amazon has put in orbit.

The June flight is worth remembering for another reason: it showed what the P160C boosters can do. Our report on that mission, <a href="/blog/ariane-64-p160c-amazon-leo/">The Heavyweight Debut: Ariane 64's New P160C Boosters Haul 36 Amazon Leo Satellites to Orbit</a>, covers the first flight of the upgraded boosters and the record payload they carried. LE-04 is the second outing for the same hardware, and a clean flight would confirm that June was not a one-off.

The rocket itself is Ariane 64, the most powerful configuration of Europe's Ariane 6 launcher. Where the two-booster Ariane 62 carries about 10.3 tonnes to low Earth orbit, the four-booster Ariane 64 can carry around 21.6 tonnes, more than double. The upgraded P160C solid rocket boosters, which the European Space Agency says improve performance by almost 15 percent — roughly two extra tonnes to orbit — take Ariane 64's capacity to about 22 tonnes. Monday's flight was only the second flight of the P160C boosters, and the second time they have carried the larger 36-satellite payload for Amazon.

The wider constellation picture is larger still. Amazon's network, formerly known as Project Kuiper before a rebrand in November 2025, is planned at 3,232 satellites. The company's licence requires the full constellation to be in orbit by July 30, 2029. An interim milestone — half the constellation, 1,616 satellites, by July 30, 2026 — was conditionally waived by the FCC in early June 2026, but the waiver carries a cost: satellites launched after July 30 lose spectrum priority in the Ka and Ku bands until March 2028, unless Amazon meets certain conditions sooner. With roughly 400 satellites launched and more than 2,800 still to go, the arithmetic is unforgiving, and it explains why Amazon has launch contracts spread across four rocket families and more than 100 missions on its manifest.

Arianespace is a large part of that plan. The original 2022 agreement covered 18 Ariane 6 launches for Amazon. On September 9, 2026, Amazon ordered six more Ariane 64 flights, taking the commitment to 24 launches, with the new flights beginning in 2029 and the package running through 2031. LE-04 is one of the original 18; the six new ones are still years away.

## What a scrub costs — and what it doesn't

It is easy to overstate a scrub. A rocket that does not launch is not a rocket that has failed, and the history of spaceflight is full of countdowns that stopped and flights that succeeded the next day. The Space Shuttle scrubbed routinely. Falcon 9 has scrubbed for weather, for a sensor reading, for a boat in the range. Ariane itself has scrubbed and flown the next day more than once. A one-day delay, on its own, costs almost nothing: the rocket is still there, the satellites are still there, and the physics of the orbit will be the same tomorrow.

But it is also easy to understate one, and Monday's scrub deserves a clear-eyed accounting of what it does cost. The first cost is time, and time is the one resource Amazon's constellation programme cannot buy more of. The network must reach 3,232 satellites by July 30, 2029, and roughly 400 are in orbit today. That leaves more than 2,800 satellites to launch in less than three years — a pace of nearly a thousand satellites a year, or roughly 25 to 30 full deployment flights a year, across every rocket Amazon has booked. Every day a flight slips is a day that pace is not being kept, and the programme has already lost four months between its last successful deployment in July and this week.

The second cost is confidence. Amazon's launch programme rests on four rocket families — Ariane 6, Vulcan, Atlas V, and Falcon 9 — precisely so that a problem with one does not stop the whole programme. That logic held on paper after the Vulcan failure: Ariane was available, and LE-04 was ready. But Monday showed the other side of that logic. A programme that depends on every rocket working, and every spaceport working, is a programme with many single points of failure, and two of them have now failed in nine days. The causes are unrelated — a fuel-supply problem on Vulcan, a ground-systems event at Kourou — but the effect on the schedule is the same.

The third cost is the one that is hardest to measure: the delay to commercial service. Amazon has not published a date for commercial service, and it has not published subscriber numbers or retail pricing for its three terminal classes, although it has begun testing with select enterprise customers. What is known is that the network is being built for global coverage, with satellites in inclined orbits at 51.9 degrees that serve the latitudes where most of the world's population lives. Every batch of satellites that reaches orbit brings that service closer. Every batch that stays on the ground pushes it back.

Set against those costs, the scrub also cost Amazon nothing that cannot be recovered. No hardware was lost. No satellite was damaged. No investigation will ground the rocket for months, as the Vulcan investigation has grounded ULA's vehicle. If the ground-systems event is cleared by Tuesday, the flight will go, the satellites will separate at about 465 kilometres, and Amazon's operations centre in Redmond, Washington, will take control, raise them to their 630-kilometre operational altitude, and begin commissioning. The story of Monday will then be a footnote: a scrub, a day's delay, a launch the next morning.

That is the test for Tuesday. A successful launch will make Monday look like routine spaceflight — a countdown stopped early, a fault found and fixed, a flight a day late. A second scrub, or a delay that stretches past Tuesday, will start to look like something else: a ground-systems problem that is not yet understood, at a spaceport whose launch rate Amazon's schedule increasingly depends on. The difference between those two stories will be decided by what Arianespace's technical teams find when they examine the ground equipment, and by what the company says next.

There is also a wider question that Monday's scrub raises, and it is about cadence. Arianespace had been chasing seven or eight Ariane 6 flights in 2026; VA271 would be only the fifth. The most recent Ariane 6 flight before LE-04 was VA270 on August 27, when an Ariane 62 carried the EUMETSAT MTG-I2 weather satellite into geostationary transfer orbit — the rocket's first mission to that destination. Our coverage of that flight, <a href="/blog/ariane-6-mtg-i2-launch/">Europe's Weather Eye Gets Sharper: Ariane 6 Delivers MTG-I2 to Orbit on Its First-Ever GTO Mission</a>, describes a mission that completed Europe's next-generation meteorological imaging trio. That was nearly three months ago. A December flight carrying the MetOp-SG B1 weather satellite is still on the schedule, which would take the year to six flights at most. The programme's first flight was in July 2024, and by February 2026 the European Space Agency counted five flights, all in the two-booster Ariane 62 configuration. The four-booster Ariane 64 has flown three times since, all for Amazon. The companies say the supply chain is on track to support nine to ten launches a year. A fifth Ariane 6 flight in a calendar year, and a fourth for the same customer, would be a meaningful step toward that rate — if it flies.

## The competition does not wait

While Amazon's satellites sit on the ground at Kourou, the market they are meant to serve keeps moving. SpaceX's Starlink, the constellation Amazon Leo is built to compete with, currently consists of more than 11,000 operational spacecraft, according to Space.com. That is roughly twenty-eight times the number Amazon has in orbit. Starlink is already selling service around the world; Amazon is still building the network that will let it start.

The gap is not a verdict. Starlink began launching years earlier, and Amazon's constellation was always a second mover by design — a network built later, with newer satellites, on a licence deadline that runs to 2029. But the gap is a measure of what is at stake in every deployment flight, and it is why a scrub matters more to Amazon than it would to a programme with no deadline and no competitor. Each batch of 36 satellites closes the gap by a fraction. Each day without a launch leaves it where it is.

That is also why Amazon spread its launches across four rocket families and more than 100 missions. The strategy is to make the constellation's growth independent of any single vehicle. Monday tested that strategy in a new way: not a rocket failure, but a spaceport failure, at the one European launch site the programme uses. There is no backup Kourou. If the ground systems at the Guiana Space Centre need days to fix, the only alternatives are other rockets on other continents, and those have their own queues.

## What to watch for on Tuesday

Three things will decide whether Tuesday is the day LE-04 finally flies. The first is Arianespace's next update. The company said it would provide further updates as soon as they become available, and the confirmation of a Tuesday launch time — or a further delay — will come from the operator. Until that confirmation arrives, the Tuesday date remains what Arianespace called it: no earlier than November 24, not a scheduled launch.

The second is the countdown itself. If the ground-systems event is fixed, the chronology will resume where it stopped — with the mobile gantry rolling back to expose the rocket, the launcher's systems switching on, and the final preparations running through the afternoon in Kourou. A smooth run through those steps, with no repeat of the fault, would be the first real evidence that Monday's problem is behind the mission. A hold at the same point would suggest the opposite.

The third is the flight, if it happens. Liftoff would come at the opening of the window, 11:09 UTC — 18:09 WIB — with the four P160C boosters separating just 2 minutes and 23 seconds after launch and the core stage's Vulcain engine cutting off at 7 minutes and 45 seconds. Then comes the long coast: roughly two hours before the 36 satellites separate at about 465 kilometres, at around 20:13 WIB. Arianespace will announce the separation; Amazon will then confirm first contact from its operations centre in Redmond. Those two confirmations, hours apart, are the real result of the night — and they are the same confirmations Monday never got the chance to produce.

For now, the rocket is on the pad, the satellites are safe, and the countdown is stopped. Monday was supposed to be the night Amazon's deployment programme restarted after the Vulcan loss. Instead it became a reminder that in spaceflight, the rocket is only part of the machine — and that sometimes the thing that stops a launch is not the vehicle, or the weather, or the payload, but the ground beneath it. The next chance comes on Tuesday. SpaceNews will carry the result in the morning edition.
